# Buyer Roles and Buying Context

**Curriculum expansion:** Additive instructional draft; not a replacement for canonical lesson identifiers. **Level:** 1. **Evidence status:** worked cases are hypothetical and no live operational outcomes are claimed.

## Outcomes
Differentiate users, buyers, payers, approvers and influencers; map a decision without stereotyping; identify approval and procurement constraints; distinguish product fit from purchase readiness.

## Multiple stakeholders, multiple outcomes
In consumer sales, a buyer may also use and pay for a product. In organizational purchasing, the roles often separate. A team manager may need a tool; a finance officer may approve the budget; a security officer may evaluate data processing; employees may use the tool; and a procurement specialist may negotiate terms. The person most enthusiastic about a demonstration may have no authority to commit the organization.

A **champion** advocates internally, but cannot automatically grant consent for another person or bind a company. An **economic buyer** is accountable for funding, though the exact authority varies by organization. A **technical evaluator** assesses compatibility, reliability and security. A **legal or compliance reviewer** checks obligations and risk. The roles should be confirmed through respectful questions rather than assumed from job titles.

## Decision map
Build a table with role, desired outcome, evidence needed, concerns, decision authority and next action. Mark unknown entries explicitly. Do not fill unknown names or approval powers with guesses.

| Role | Illustrative question | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| End user | Does this reduce repetitive work? | Usability test |
| Manager | Does it improve team throughput? | Before/after workflow measurement |
| Finance | Is total cost justified? | Cost model and assumptions |
| Security | Where does customer data go? | Data-flow diagram and controls |
| Procurement | Are terms and obligations acceptable? | Written agreement and vendor review |

## Worked case — buying a CRM automation
A sales manager wants a new workflow because follow-ups are inconsistent. A vendor demonstrates a prototype and the manager likes it. That is evidence of **interest**, not a completed buying decision. The security team requires data-retention documentation; finance requires a total-cost model; the company prohibits automated outbound email without approval. The seller should identify these constraints before offering a deployment timeline.

Suppose subscription cost is $400 per month, setup is $1,000 and internal implementation labor is estimated at 25 hours × $40/hour = $1,000. The first-year specified cost is 12 × $400 + $1,000 + $1,000 = **$6,800**, excluding additional integration, training and support. Comparing only the $400 monthly subscription to a hypothetical productivity benefit would understate adoption cost.

If the manager estimates 10 hours saved monthly at $40/hour, gross time value is $400 per month. That matches subscription cost but does not repay setup and implementation costs, and the savings are not verified. A defensible next step is a limited evaluation with predeclared measures, not a promise of positive ROI.

## Ethical communication and risk
Do not invent authority, urgency or approvals. If a prospect asks whether a feature is compliant with a particular law, explain what documentation exists and refer unresolved legal interpretation to qualified counsel. If a buyer requires an integration not currently supported, document the gap rather than claiming it is already built.

A buyer may decline even when the product is suitable. Timing, liquidity, competing priorities and organizational change costs are legitimate reasons. A transparent seller can preserve the relationship without repeated unwanted contact.

## Practice
1. A supervisor likes the product but cannot sign contracts. What is known, and what remains unknown?
2. Calculate first-year specified cost if monthly subscription falls to $300, with other costs unchanged.
3. Write two neutral questions to learn whether security review is required.
4. Distinguish a product demonstration from proof of measurable business improvement.
5. Explain why a pilot needs a baseline and an agreed decision rule.

### Solutions
1. User interest is known; purchasing authority, budget, compliance and approval process are unknown.
2. 12 × $300 + $1,000 + $1,000 = **$5,600**.
3. “What security evaluation does your organization use?” and “Who reviews vendor data handling?”
4. A demonstration shows a capability under chosen conditions; improvement requires representative measurements against a baseline.
5. Without them, the participants can reinterpret success after the fact and overstate outcomes.

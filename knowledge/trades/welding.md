Welding refers to a fabrication process where two metal pieces, known as base metals, are joined together using heat, pressure, or both, with or without filler metal. A weld is the resulting joint, and a weldment is the assembled product. The process involves a welder, a skilled tradesperson, operating welding equipment to apply the necessary heat and pressure. 
Key terms include: 
- Base metal: the metals being joined, defined as the workpieces or parent metals. 
- Filler metal: a metal added to the weld pool to facilitate the joining process, often in the form of a wire or rod. 
- Weld pool: the localized area where the base metals and filler metal (if used) are melted, creating a molten pool of metal. 
- Shielding gas: a protective gas, such as argon or helium, used to prevent atmospheric gases from entering the weld pool, thus preventing porosity and oxidation. 
Understanding these core definitions and principles is essential for a practitioner to safely and effectively perform welding operations.

## Mechanisms

Welding involves the application of heat, pressure, or both to join two metal pieces. The process begins with the preparation of the base metals, which includes cleaning and aligning the edges to be joined. The next step is the generation of an arc or flame, which is used to melt the base metals. In shielded metal arc welding (SMAW), also known as "stick" welding, an electric arc is created between the metal and an electrode, causing the metal to melt. In gas metal arc welding (GMAW), an electric arc is created between the metal and a continuous wire electrode, while an inert gas shields the arc. The heat from the arc or flame melts the base metals, creating a pool of molten metal. As the molten metal cools, it solidifies, forming a strong bond between the two pieces. The causal chain is as follows: the arc or flame generates heat, the heat melts the base metals, the molten metal flows together, and the cooled metal solidifies, forming a weld joint. The quality of the weld joint depends on factors such as the type of metal, the welding process, and the skill of the welder.

## Methods And Frameworks

In welding, several methods and frameworks are employed to ensure high-quality joints. The Shielded Metal Arc Welding (SMAW) method, also known as "stick" welding, uses a consumable electrode covered in flux to protect the arc. It's commonly used for repair and maintenance work, as well as for welding thick materials. However, its failure mode includes porosity and lack of fusion due to incorrect electrode angle or insufficient heat input. 
Gas Metal Arc Welding (GMAW), or "MIG" welding, uses a continuous wire feed and inert gas to shield the arc. It's suitable for high-volume production and thin materials, but its failure mode includes lack of penetration and porosity due to incorrect wire feed speed or gas flow. 
Gas Tungsten Arc Welding (GTAW), or "TIG" welding, uses a non-consumable tungsten electrode and inert gas to produce high-quality welds. It's commonly used for critical joints and thin materials, but its failure mode includes lack of penetration and porosity due to incorrect electrode angle or insufficient heat input. 
The American Welding Society (AWS) and American Society of Mechanical Engineers (ASME) provide frameworks and standards for welding, including the AWS D1.1 structural welding code and ASME Section IX welding qualification code. These frameworks outline the requirements for welder qualification, material selection, and inspection to ensure weld quality and safety. 
The Heat Input (HI) formula, HI = (V x A x 60) / (S x 1000), is used to calculate the heat input in joules per millimeter, where V is voltage, A is current, and S is travel speed. This formula helps welders adjust their technique to achieve the desired weld penetration and quality. 
Understanding these methods, frameworks, and formulas is crucial for welders to produce high-quality joints and minimize the risk of failure.

## Common Errors

In welding, common errors can lead to defective joints, reduced strength, and increased risk of failure. One of the most prevalent mistakes is insufficient cleaning of the base metal, resulting in porosity and lack of fusion due to the presence of dirt, oil, or moisture. Another error is incorrect electrode angle, which can cause uneven heat distribution, leading to warping or distortion of the workpiece. Inadequate shielding gas flow or incorrect gas composition can also lead to porosity, oxidation, or carburization, compromising the weld's quality. Furthermore, incorrect welding technique, such as applying too much heat or using excessive weld speed, can cause burn-through, lack of penetration, or weld spatter. Additionally, failure to maintain proper interpass temperature control can result in thermal stresses, leading to cracking or distortion. Lastly, neglecting to follow established welding procedures and standards, such as those outlined in AWS or ASME codes, can lead to non-compliant welds that may not meet the required safety or performance standards. These errors can be attributed to inadequate training, lack of experience, or failure to follow established protocols, highlighting the importance of proper education, practice, and adherence to industry standards in welding.

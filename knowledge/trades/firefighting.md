# Firefighting

> The course applies principles to real situations and methods, assuming foundational knowledge of firefighting concepts.

## Foundations

Firefighting is the disciplined application of science, engineering, and tactical methodologies to detect, suppress, and prevent unwanted combustion events, protecting life, property, and the environment. At its core, firefighting operates on the fire tetrahedron principle: heat, fuel, oxygen, and the chemical chain reaction. Disruption of any element arrests fire propagation. Fire behavior is governed by combustion chemistry and thermodynamics, fluid dynamics, and heat transfer mechanisms (conduction, convection, radiation). Effective firefighting integrates hazard assessment, incident command, resource deployment, and safety management underpinned by NFPA (National Fire Protection Association) standards and OSHA regulations.

In the trades vocational context of firefighting, a **fire** is defined as an uncontrolled, self-sustaining chemical reaction involving the rapid oxidation of fuel sources, resulting in the release of heat, light, and various reaction products. **Firefighting** refers to the activities and operations undertaken to extinguish or control fires, mitigate their effects, and protect people, property, and the environment from fire-related hazards. A **firefighter** is a trained professional who engages in firefighting operations, utilizing various techniques, equipment, and strategies to combat fires. 
Key vocabulary includes **flammable** (capable of igniting and burning), **combustible** (capable of burning), and **ignition source** (a factor that initiates a fire, such as heat, sparks, or open flames). Understanding the **fire triangle** (fuel, oxygen, and heat) is crucial, as removing any one of these elements can extinguish or prevent a fire. **Hazard** refers to a situation or condition that poses a threat to people, property, or the environment, while **risk** is the likelihood and potential impact of a hazard. **Personal Protective Equipment (PPE)**, such as helmets, coats, and self-contained breathing apparatus (SCBA), is essential for protecting firefighters from hazards associated with firefighting operations.

In the trades vocational context of firefighting, a firefighter is a trained emergency response professional who specializes in rescue, fire suppression, and hazard mitigation. Firefighting involves the application of various techniques, equipment, and strategies to control and extinguish fires, as well as to rescue people and animals from hazardous situations. The core definitions in firefighting include: 
- **Fire**: a self-sustaining chemical reaction that releases heat, light, and energy, often resulting from the combustion of fuel sources such as wood, gasoline, or propane.
- **Fuel**: any material that can undergo combustion, including solids, liquids, and gases.
- **Oxidizer**: a substance that supports combustion, such as oxygen.
- **Heat**: energy transferred from one body to another due to a temperature difference, often the primary factor in initiating and sustaining a fire.
- **Fire triangle**: a model that illustrates the three essential elements required for a fire to start and sustain: fuel, oxidizer, and heat.
- **Hazard**: a situation or condition that poses a threat to people, property, or the environment, such as a fire, explosion, or toxic substance release.
- **Mitigation**: the process of reducing or eliminating the risk or impact of a hazard, often through emergency response and firefighting operations.
- **Personal Protective Equipment (PPE)**: specialized gear worn by firefighters to protect themselves from hazards, including helmets, coats, pants, gloves, and self-contained breathing apparatus (SCBA).
- **Fireground**: the area surrounding a fire, including the immediate vicinity of the burning structure or vehicle, where firefighters operate to extinguish the fire and perform rescue operations. 
Understanding these core definitions, first principles, and vocabulary is crucial for a practitioner to effectively respond to and manage emergency situations.

## Fire Behavior Analysis

Framework: B-SAHF (Building, Smoke, Air Track, Heat, Flame)  
- Building: Structural type (Type I-IV), construction materials, compartmentation influence fire spread and collapse risk.  
- Smoke: Color, volume, velocity, and density indicate combustion completeness, fuel involvement, and ventilation status.  
- Air Track: Direction and velocity of air movement reveal pressure differentials and ventilation flow paths.  
- Heat: Thermal indicators (IR imaging, heat sensors) assess fire intensity and potential flashover conditions (approx. 500°C surface temps).  
- Flame: Size, shape, and color provide clues on fuel type and fire stage.  
Steps: Conduct 360° size-up using B-SAHF to predict fire growth and identify ventilation needs.

## Ventilation Strategies

Framework: Positive Pressure Ventilation (PPV) vs. Hydraulic Ventilation vs. Vertical Ventilation  
- PPV: Use high-capacity fans at entry points to create a pressure differential, expelling smoke and heat. Optimal when exhaust openings are ≥ 1.5 times the size of intake.  
- Hydraulic Ventilation: Use fog nozzles at 30° fog pattern, 100-200 psi nozzle pressure, aimed outwards at exhaust openings to pull smoke out via water flow dynamics.  
- Vertical Ventilation: Cutting roof openings (minimum 4’x4’) above the fire seat to release heat and smoke, reducing flashover risk. Requires structural assessment to avoid collapse.  
Formula: Ventilation flow = Area of opening (m²) × velocity (m/s); maintain balanced intake and exhaust to control fire behavior.

## Extinguishing Agents & Applications

Framework: Agent Selection Matrix based on fire class and environment  
- Water: Primary agent for Class A fires; applied via straight stream (reach 30m) or fog (cooling, steam conversion). Flow rates: 95-125 GPM for handlines, up to 500 GPM for master streams.  
- Foam: AFFF (Aqueous Film Forming Foam) for hydrocarbon fires (Class B); expansion ratios 3:1 (low) to 20:1 (medium); application rates 0.1-0.25 GPM/ft².  
- Dry Chemical: Purple-K for Class B and C fires; applied in 10-20 lb/sec flow rates; interrupts chemical chain reaction.  
- CO2: Displaces oxygen for Class B and electrical fires; effective in confined spaces; discharge range 4-6 m.  
- Class D agents: Sodium chloride-based powders for metal fires; applied gently to avoid dispersing burning metals.  
Steps: Identify fire class → select agent → apply at manufacturer’s recommended flow and pattern → monitor extinguishment.

## Incident Command System (Ics) In Firefighting

Framework: ICS 5 Major Functional Areas – Command, Operations, Planning, Logistics, Finance/Administration  
- Command: Incident Commander (IC) establishes objectives, safety protocols, and resource allocation.  
- Operations: Executes tactical firefighting, search and rescue, ventilation, and suppression.  
- Planning: Collects intel, predicts fire behavior, and develops contingency plans.  
- Logistics: Provides personnel, equipment, and supplies support.  
- Finance/Admin: Tracks costs, contracts, and legal documentation.  
Steps: Establish command → conduct size-up → develop Incident Action Plan (IAP) → assign sectors/divisions → conduct progress reports and demobilization.

## Search And Rescue Operations

Framework: Primary and Secondary Search with Rapid Intervention Teams (RIT)  
- Primary Search: Immediate, high-priority search for victims in most threatened areas; conducted with charged hoseline for protection.  
- Secondary Search: Systematic, thorough search after fire control; includes checking hidden voids and structural elements.  
- RIT: Dedicated team on standby for firefighter rescue; equipped with SCBA, thermal imaging cameras, and forcible entry tools.  
Steps: Deploy search teams with accountability tags → maintain communication → use thermal imaging for victim location → coordinate with RIT for firefighter safety.

## Personal Protective Equipment (Ppe) And Scba

Framework: NFPA 1971 (Structural PPE) and NFPA 1981 (SCBA) standards  
- PPE: Multi-layered turnout gear with moisture barrier, thermal liner, and outer shell; designed to withstand 500°C for limited exposure.  
- SCBA: Provides 30-60 minutes of breathable air; includes PASS (Personal Alert Safety System) alarm activated after 30 seconds of motionlessness.  
- Donning procedures: Inspect, fit gloves and hood, seal facepiece, perform negative/positive pressure tests.  
- Maintenance: Daily checks, monthly flow tests (minimum 450 L/min), annual hydrostatic testing of cylinders at 4500 psi.  
Steps: Proper PPE donning → SCBA integrity check → continuous air management → emergency procedures for low-air situations.

## Fireground Tactics And Nozzle Techniques

Framework: Direct, Indirect, and Combination Attack Methods  
- Direct Attack: Apply water stream directly onto burning fuel; effective for Class A fires; flow rate 95-125 GPM; nozzle pressure 50 psi.  
- Indirect Attack: Apply water fog into superheated gases to cool and reduce fire intensity; used in ventilation-limited fires.  
- Combination Attack: Initial indirect fog to cool gases, followed by direct attack on fuel.  
Nozzle Techniques:  
- Straight stream: Long reach, penetration up to 30 m; nozzle pressure 50 psi.  
- Fog pattern: Droplet size 100-200 microns; absorbs heat via evaporation; nozzle pressure 100 psi.  
- Pulsing: Intermittent fog to reduce steam burns and water damage.  
Steps: Size-up → select attack method → coordinate ventilation → apply water/foam → monitor fire response and adjust.

## Mechanisms

In firefighting, the mechanisms involved in extinguishing fires can be broken down into a step-by-step process. The causal chain begins with the detection of a fire, which triggers an alarm and alerts firefighters to respond. Upon arrival, firefighters assess the situation and determine the best course of action, considering factors such as the type and size of the fire, the materials involved, and the potential risks to people and property. The next step is to establish a water supply, which may involve connecting to a hydrant or using a tanker truck. The water is then pumped through a hose to a nozzle, where it is discharged onto the fire. The discharge of water onto the fire cools the fuel, removing heat and preventing the fire from spreading. As the fuel is cooled, the chemical reaction that sustains the fire is disrupted, ultimately leading to extinguishment. In addition to cooling, water also smothers the fire by depriving it of oxygen, further contributing to extinguishment. The mechanisms of firefighting also involve the use of other agents, such as foam or dry chemicals, which can be used to extinguish fires involving specific types of fuels, such as oils or electrical equipment. The selection of the appropriate extinguishing agent is critical, as it must be compatible with the fuel and the surrounding environment to ensure effective and safe extinguishment. Throughout the process, firefighters must also consider ventilation, as the removal of smoke and heat can help to reduce the risk of re-ignition and improve visibility. The combination of these mechanisms, including detection, response, water supply, discharge, cooling, smothering, and ventilation, all work together to effectively extinguish fires and protect people and property.

The firefighting process involves a series of mechanisms that work together to extinguish or control fires. The first step is fire detection, which can be achieved through manual observation or automated systems such as smoke detectors and heat sensors. Once a fire is detected, the alarm is triggered, notifying firefighters and initiating the response process. The next mechanism is water supply or agent delivery, where water or a fire-suppressing agent is transported to the scene through hoses, pumps, or tanks. The water or agent is then applied to the fire using nozzles, sprayers, or other discharge devices. The causal chain is as follows: the fuel source (e.g., wood, gasoline) is heated, producing combustible vapors that ignite, releasing heat and light. The heat from the fire warms surrounding objects, creating a feedback loop that sustains the fire. Applying water or a fire-suppressing agent disrupts this loop by cooling the fuel source, removing heat, and smothering the fire, ultimately starving it of oxygen and stopping the combustion reaction. The mechanism of heat transfer is crucial, as it allows the water or agent to absorb and dissipate heat, reducing the temperature of the fuel source and surrounding objects, and thus controlling the fire.

## Methods And Frameworks

In firefighting, various methods and frameworks are employed to effectively combat fires. The National Incident Management System (NIMS) provides a framework for managing incidents, emphasizing a coordinated response. The Incident Command System (ICS) is a key component of NIMS, outlining a hierarchical structure for incident management. The NFPA 1710 standard provides guidelines for fire department operations, including response times and resource allocation. The "RESCUE" method is used for initial scene assessment: Recognize the situation, Evaluate the scene, Send for help, Control the scene, Understand the hazards, and Execute a plan. The "PASS" method is used for fire extinguisher operation: Pull the safety pin, Aim the nozzle, Squeeze the handle, and Sweep the area. Failure modes for these methods include inadequate training, poor communication, and insufficient resource allocation. The "Forcible Entry" method is used for accessing locked or blocked areas, while the "Vent-Enter-Isolate-Search" (VEIS) method is used for searching and rescuing occupants in smoke-filled buildings. Understanding these methods and frameworks is crucial for effective firefighting operations.

## Worked Examples

Calculating water pressure in a firefighting scenario: 
A fire engine is pumping water through a 2-inch hose at a rate of 500 gallons per minute (gpm). If the hose is 100 feet long and the engine is pumping at 120 pounds per square inch (psi), what is the pressure at the end of the hose? 
First, calculate the friction loss in the hose using the formula: friction loss (psi) = (flow rate (gpm) / 100) * (hose length (ft) / 100). 
Friction loss = (500 / 100) * (100 / 100) = 5 psi. 
Then, subtract the friction loss from the initial pressure: 120 psi - 5 psi = 115 psi.

Calculating the required nozzle size: 
A firefighter needs to deliver 200 gpm of water through a nozzle to fight a fire. If the desired nozzle pressure is 80 psi, what size nozzle is required? 
Use the formula: nozzle size = sqrt((flow rate (gpm) * 0.0247) / (nozzle pressure (psi) + 1)). 
Nozzle size = sqrt((200 * 0.0247) / (80 + 1)) = sqrt(0.615) = 0.78 inches. 
Round up to the nearest available nozzle size, which is 1 inch.

Calculating the volume of foam concentrate: 
A fire engine has a 500-gallon tank of water and a 20-gallon tank of foam concentrate. If the desired foam solution is 3%, what volume of foam concentrate is required? 
First, calculate the total volume of the solution: 500 gallons of water + unknown volume of foam concentrate. 
Since the solution is 3% foam concentrate, the volume of foam concentrate is 3% of the total solution volume. 
Let x be the volume of foam concentrate. Then, x = 0.03 * (500 + x). 
Solving for x: x = 0.03 * 500 / (1 - 0.03) = 15.38 gallons. 
However, the engine only has a 20-gallon tank of foam concentrate, so the available foam concentrate is sufficient.

## Applications

In trades vocational firefighting, the principles and techniques learned are applied in various scenarios to combat and extinguish fires. Firefighters use their knowledge of fire behavior, hazardous materials, and rescue operations to respond to emergency situations. Furthermore, they conduct fire inspections and educate the public on fire safety and prevention techniques to reduce the risk of fires occurring in the first place. By applying their knowledge and skills in these ways, firefighters play a critical role in protecting people, property, and the environment from the dangers of fire.

## Common Errors

In firefighting, common errors can be detrimental to the safety of firefighters and the effectiveness of firefighting operations. One mistake is underestimating the importance of proper personal protective equipment (PPE) donning and doffing procedures. Failure to follow established protocols can lead to contamination and exposure to hazardous substances. Another error is inadequate ventilation during interior attacks, which can cause a buildup of toxic gases and reduce visibility. Additionally, incorrect nozzle techniques, such as using the wrong nozzle pattern or failing to maintain a safe distance from the fire, can lead to inefficient water application and increased risk of injury. Furthermore, poor communication and failure to follow incident command system (ICS) protocols can result in confusion, misallocated resources, and decreased situational awareness. These mistakes often stem from inadequate training, lack of experience, or complacency, highlighting the need for continuous training and adherence to established procedures. By understanding these common errors, firefighters can take steps to mitigate risks and improve their response to emergency situations.

In firefighting, common errors often stem from inadequate training, equipment misuse, or failure to follow established protocols. Additionally, failing to properly size up a scene, including not identifying potential hazards or not assessing the fire's spread, can lead to inadequate resource allocation and increased risk to firefighters. Furthermore, communication breakdowns, such as not clearly conveying information or not using standardized terminology, can hinder effective teamwork and decision-making. These errors highlight the importance of rigorous training, adherence to protocols, and continuous practice in firefighting to minimize risks and ensure effective fire suppression.

# Roofing

> The course assumes no prior study but teaches core concepts and principles of roofing.

## Foundations

Roofing is the systematic design, selection, and installation of materials and assemblies that form the weatherproof upper covering of a building, ensuring structural integrity, thermal regulation, and water shedding. At its core, roofing integrates principles of fluid dynamics (rainwater runoff), thermodynamics (heat transfer), material science (durability, UV resistance), and structural engineering (load distribution). The fundamental purpose is to protect the building envelope from environmental loads—precipitation, wind uplift, solar radiation, and thermal cycling—while maintaining longevity and minimal maintenance. Roofing systems are classified primarily by slope (steep vs. low slope), material composition (organic, inorganic, synthetic), and assembly type (built-up, single-ply, metal, green). Key first principles include:  
- Water flows downhill and must be directed off the roof efficiently to prevent infiltration.  
- Thermal expansion and contraction require flexible or resilient materials and secure fastening methods.  
- Load paths must transfer dead loads (roof weight), live loads (snow, maintenance), and dynamic loads (wind uplift) safely to the structure.  
- Vapor diffusion and condensation control are critical to prevent substrate deterioration.

In the context of roofing, a foundation refers to the base or underlying structure that supports the roof. This includes the **roof deck**, defined as the surface, usually made of wood or concrete, that the roofing material is applied to. The roof deck is typically composed of **joists**, horizontal beams that run between the walls or **rafters**, sloping beams that extend from the wall to the **ridge**, the highest point of the roof. The **truss**, a pre-fabricated framework of triangles, is also a common component of the roof deck, providing additional support. 
Key vocabulary includes **pitch**, the measure of a roof's slope, expressed as a ratio of vertical rise to horizontal run, and **aspect**, the orientation of the roof in relation to the sun and weather. A **gable** is the triangular section of wall at the end of a pitched roof, while a **valley** is the internal angle where two sloping roofs meet. Understanding these core definitions and principles is essential for a roofing practitioner to ensure a safe and successful installation.

In the trades vocational context of roofing, a foundation refers to the base layer or surface to which a roof is attached, typically the top of a building's walls or a deck. A deck, in this context, is a structural element, usually made of wood or concrete, that provides a surface for the roof to be installed on. The roof's foundation is crucial as it affects the roof's stability, durability, and overall performance. Key terms include: 
- **Rafters**: sloping beams that extend from the roof's ridge to the eaves, providing the structural framework for the roof.
- **Trusses**: pre-fabricated frames, composed of triangles of rafters and other beams, used to support the roof deck.
- **Eaves**: the lower edge of a roof, where the roof meets the building's walls.
- **Ridge**: the highest point of a roof, where two sloping sides meet.
- **Flashing**: materials, typically metal or plastic, used to seal and protect the roof's joints and valleys from water intrusion.
Understanding these core definitions and first principles is essential for a roofing practitioner to ensure a safe and successful installation.

## Materials And Assemblies

The primary roofing materials include asphalt shingles, metal panels (steel, aluminum, copper), single-ply membranes (EPDM, TPO, PVC), built-up roofing (BUR), modified bitumen, and green roofs. For example, asphalt shingles typically have a nominal thickness of 3.17 mm, with fiberglass reinforcement and an ASTM D3161 Class F wind resistance rating (up to 110 mph). Metal roofing panels are often 0.5–0.7 mm thick steel, coated with G90 galvanization and PVDF paint for UV and corrosion resistance. Single-ply membranes require precise adhesion or mechanical fastening per manufacturer’s specifications, e.g., Carlisle’s TPO membranes installed with 6-inch seam overlaps heat-welded at 500°F for 1–2 seconds. Built-up roofing involves multiple layers of bitumen and reinforcing felts, typically 3–5 plies, with aggregate surfacing to resist UV degradation.

## Slope And Drainage Design

Roof slope critically affects water shedding and material choice. Steep roofs exceed 3:12 pitch (14°), favoring shingles and tiles; low-slope roofs range from 1/4:12 to 3:12 and require continuous membranes or BUR systems. The minimum slope for low-slope roofs is typically 1/4:12 (2%), ensuring positive drainage. Drainage design follows the Rational Method for runoff: Q = CiA, where Q is runoff (cfs), C is runoff coefficient (0.9 for impervious roofs), i is rainfall intensity (in/hr), and A is drainage area (acres). Roof drains, scuppers, and gutters must be sized accordingly, e.g., a 4-inch diameter drain handles approximately 20 cfs at 2 ft head. Proper overflow scuppers are mandated per International Building Code (IBC) Section 1503.5 to prevent ponding water.

## Thermal And Moisture Control

Thermal insulation in roofing assemblies is quantified by R-value (ft²·°F·hr/BTU). Polyisocyanurate rigid foam commonly achieves R-6 to R-6.5 per inch; thus, a 4-inch layer provides R-24 to R-26. Continuous insulation reduces thermal bridging and condensation risk. Vapor retarders are placed on the warm side of insulation per ASHRAE 90.1 to prevent interstitial condensation; for example, a 6-mil polyethylene sheet or self-adhering membrane with a perm rating <0.1 perms. Air barriers integrated with the roofing system reduce convective heat loss and moisture ingress, requiring continuity tested by ASTM E2357.

## Wind Uplift And Fastening

Wind uplift resistance is critical, especially in hurricane-prone zones. The ASCE 7-16 standard provides design pressures; for example, a roof in Exposure C with a basic wind speed of 130 mph may require uplift resistance of up to -60 psf. Fastening schedules depend on substrate and material; metal roofing panels typically use #12 self-drilling screws with neoprene washers spaced at 12-inch intervals along seams and 24 inches in the field. Single-ply membranes require plates and fasteners every 6–12 inches at seams and perimeter per FM 4470 standards. Adhesive bonding must achieve peel and shear strengths exceeding 20 pli and 50 psi, respectively.

## Installation Quality Control And Testing

Roofing installation requires strict adherence to manufacturer guidelines and building codes. Quality control includes pre-installation substrate moisture content testing (<19% moisture content for wood decks), membrane seam integrity testing via ASTM D751 peel tests, and post-installation infrared thermography to detect voids or moisture intrusion. Roof inspections per NRCA guidelines include checking for uniform fastener penetration, absence of wrinkles or fishmouths in membranes, and proper flashing integration. Leak detection methods include flood testing for low-slope roofs and electronic capacitance testing for conductive membranes.

## Maintenance And Lifecycle Management

Roofing systems have defined service lives: asphalt shingles 20–30 years, metal roofs 40–70 years, single-ply membranes 20–30 years, and BUR 20–25 years. Preventive maintenance includes biannual inspections, debris removal, sealant renewal every 5–7 years, and immediate repair of flashing or membrane damage. Lifecycle cost analysis (LCCA) applies net present value (NPV) calculations incorporating initial cost, maintenance, and replacement costs over expected service life, discounting at 3–5%. Green roofs require additional irrigation and vegetation management protocols.

## Mechanisms

The roofing mechanism involves a series of steps that work together to provide a watertight and structurally sound covering for a building. The process begins with the preparation of the roof deck, which includes installing a layer of underlayment material, such as felt paper or synthetic underlayment, to prevent water from penetrating the roof. Next, roofing materials, such as shingles, tiles, or membranes, are installed on top of the underlayment, overlapping each other to ensure a watertight seal. The roofing material is then fastened to the roof deck using nails, screws, or adhesives, depending on the type of material being used. As water hits the roof, it flows down the slope of the roof and over the edges, where it is collected by gutters and downspouts, which direct the water away from the building. The causal chain is as follows: water hits the roof, flows over the roofing material, is directed to the edges by the roof's slope, and is then collected and redirected by the gutters and downspouts, ultimately preventing water from penetrating the building and causing damage. The roofing mechanism relies on the combination of a watertight seal, proper slope, and functional gutters and downspouts to work effectively.

Roofing mechanisms involve a series of steps and components that work together to provide a watertight and structurally sound roof. The process begins with the installation of roof decking, typically composed of plywood or oriented strand board (OSB), which provides a nailing surface for the roofing material. Next, an underlayment material, such as asphalt-saturated felt paper, is applied to the decking to prevent water from penetrating the roof in the event that the primary roofing material is compromised. The primary roofing material, such as shingles, tiles, or metal panels, is then installed on top of the underlayment, with each component overlapping the one below it to ensure a watertight seal. The causal chain is as follows: the roof decking provides a surface for the underlayment, the underlayment protects the decking from water, and the primary roofing material protects the underlayment and decking from weathering and water infiltration. Flashing, which is typically composed of metal or plastic, is then installed around roof penetrations, such as vents and chimneys, to direct water away from these areas and prevent leaks. Finally, the roof is finished with the installation of ridge caps and other trim components to ensure a secure and watertight seal. The principle of layering and overlapping components is crucial in roofing, as it allows each component to work together to provide a durable and weather-resistant roof.

## Methods And Frameworks

In roofing, several methods and frameworks are employed to ensure a durable and weather-tight installation. The rafters and truss method is used for pitched roofs, where the roof's slope is determined by the rise over run formula (rise/run = pitch). This method is suitable for roofs with a pitch between 3:12 and 12:12. Its failure mode is often related to inadequate spacing or sizing of the rafters, leading to structural issues. 
The platform framing method is used for flat or low-slope roofs, where the roof deck is constructed in a flat platform. This method is suitable for roofs with a pitch less than 3:12. Its failure mode is often related to inadequate drainage, leading to water accumulation and structural damage. 
The unitized curtain wall method is used for commercial and high-rise buildings, where the roof is integrated with the exterior wall system. This method is suitable for large, complex roofs. Its failure mode is often related to inadequate sealing and flashing, leading to water infiltration and damage to the building envelope. 
The International Building Code (IBC) and the International Residential Code (IRC) provide guidelines and formulas for determining roof loads, including dead loads, live loads, and environmental loads. Roofers must consider these factors when selecting a method and designing a roof system to ensure a safe and durable installation.

The "panelized roofing" method involves pre-fabricating roof panels in a factory, which reduces on-site labor, but its failure mode is the potential for panel damage during transportation.

Each method requires careful consideration of factors like roof slope, material selection, and weather conditions to ensure a successful installation.

## Worked Examples

To illustrate the application of roofing principles, consider the following problems.

1. A residential roofing project requires 25 bundles of asphalt shingles, with each bundle covering 33.3 square feet. If the roof area is 1000 square feet, determine the number of bundles needed, considering a 10% waste factor. 
First, calculate the total area to be covered, including waste: 1000 square feet * 1.10 = 1100 square feet. Then, divide the total area by the coverage per bundle: 1100 square feet / 33.3 square feet per bundle = 33 bundles. Since 25 bundles are available, additional bundles must be purchased.

2. A roofing contractor needs to install a metal roof with a pitch of 4:12. If the roof length is 40 feet, determine the required height of the roof, using the pitch ratio. 
The pitch ratio is 4:12, meaning for every 12 units of horizontal length, the roof rises 4 units. To find the height, multiply the roof length by the rise over run: (4/12) * 40 feet = 13.33 feet.

3. A flat roof requires a layer of insulation with an R-value of 20. If the available insulation has an R-value of 5 per inch, determine the required thickness of the insulation. 
To find the required thickness, divide the desired R-value by the R-value per inch: 20 / 5 = 4 inches. Therefore, the insulation must be 4 inches thick to meet the required R-value.

1. A residential building requires a new asphalt shingle roof. The roof area is 1200 square feet, and the shingles come in bundles covering 33.3 square feet each. If each bundle costs $25, what is the total cost of the shingles needed? 
First, calculate the number of bundles required: 1200 sq ft / 33.3 sq ft per bundle = 36 bundles. Then, multiply by the cost per bundle: 36 bundles * $25 per bundle = $900.

2. A roofing contractor needs to determine the number of roofing nails required for a job. The roof area is 800 square feet, and the nail spacing is 12 inches on center. Assuming 4 nails per shingle and 20 shingles per square (100 sq ft), calculate the total number of nails needed. 
First, calculate the total number of shingles: 800 sq ft / 100 sq ft per square = 8 squares * 20 shingles per square = 160 shingles. Then, calculate the total number of nails: 160 shingles * 4 nails per shingle = 640 nails.

3. A flat roof requires a new EPDM (rubber) membrane. The roof area is 1500 square feet, and the membrane comes in rolls 50 feet long and 10 feet wide. If each roll costs $150, what is the total cost of the membrane needed? 
First, calculate the number of rolls required: 1500 sq ft / (50 ft * 10 ft) = 1500 sq ft / 500 sq ft per roll = 3 rolls. Then, multiply by the cost per roll: 3 rolls * $150 per roll = $450.

This includes selecting and installing roofing materials such as asphalt shingles, clay or concrete tiles, metal roofing, and flat roofing systems like EPDM (ethylene propylene diene monomer) or PVC (polyvinyl chloride). Roofing applications also involve performing routine maintenance tasks, like inspecting and repairing leaks, and replacing damaged or worn-out roofing materials to extend the lifespan of the roof. By applying their knowledge of roofing materials, techniques, and safety protocols, roofers can provide durable, weather-tight, and energy-efficient roof systems that protect buildings and their occupants.

## Common Errors

In roofing, common errors often stem from inadequate preparation, incorrect material selection, and poor workmanship. One prevalent mistake is insufficient flashing installation, leading to water infiltration and damage. This error occurs when flashings are not properly sealed or are installed with inadequate overlap, allowing water to seep behind the flashing and into the roof assembly. Another mistake is incorrect nailing patterns, which can compromise the structural integrity of the roof. For example, nailing too close to the edge of shingles or using nails that are too short can lead to shingle blow-off or damage during high winds. Additionally, failure to ensure a secure and weather-tight seal around roof penetrations, such as vents and skylights, can also lead to water ingress and damage. These errors can be attributed to a lack of attention to detail, inadequate training, or rushing through the installation process. It is essential for practitioners to follow established best practices and manufacturer instructions to minimize the risk of errors and ensure a durable and weather-tight roof assembly. Regular inspection and maintenance can also help identify and address potential issues before they become major problems.

Archaeology, as a humanities discipline, is the systematic study of past human cultures through the recovery, analysis, and interpretation of material remains, such as artifacts, architecture, and cultural landscapes. A practitioner of archaeology, known as an archaeologist, must be familiar with core definitions, first principles, and key vocabulary.

Key terms include: 
**Artifact**, a portable object made, used, or modified by humans, such as tools, pottery, or jewelry. 
**Assemblage**, a collection of artifacts found together, providing insight into the activities and behaviors of a past culture. 
**Stratigraphy**, the study of the layering of deposits, such as soil, rock, or occupation layers, to reconstruct the chronological sequence of events at a site. 
**Context**, the specific circumstances and conditions under which an artifact or assemblage was created, used, and deposited, including its spatial, temporal, and cultural relationships. 
**Culture**, a shared system of meanings, values, and practices that characterize a particular human group, often inferred through the analysis of material remains. 
**Site**, a location where human activity has occurred, such as a settlement, burial ground, or ceremonial center, which may contain artifacts, features, and other evidence of past human presence. 
Understanding these foundational concepts and terms is essential for archaeologists to develop a nuanced and informed interpretation of the past.

## Mechanisms

The process of archaeology in the humanities involves a series of steps that lead to the interpretation and understanding of past human cultures. It begins with reconnaissance, where researchers identify potential sites through aerial photography, maps, and local informants. This is followed by site surveyance, which involves systematic walking or sampling of the area to locate artifacts and features. Once a site is identified, excavation is undertaken, which involves the careful removal of soil and debris to expose artifacts and architectural features. The excavation process is typically conducted in a stratigraphic manner, with each layer of soil and debris removed and recorded to maintain a chronological sequence. Artifacts and features are then documented, conserved, and analyzed using various techniques such as radiocarbon dating, pottery analysis, and faunal analysis. The data collected is then interpreted within the context of the site and the broader cultural and historical framework, using theoretical models and frameworks to understand the social, economic, and political structures of past societies. The interpretation is then presented in a report or publication, which contributes to the broader understanding of human history and culture. Throughout this process, archaeologists must consider the ethical implications of their work, including the treatment of human remains, the preservation of cultural heritage, and the involvement of local communities.

## Methods And Frameworks

In humanities-based archaeology, several methods and frameworks are employed to analyze and interpret archaeological data. The Stratigraphic Method is used to reconstruct the chronological sequence of a site by analyzing the layers of deposits and their relationships. This method is particularly useful for dating sites and understanding the occupational history, but its failure mode lies in the assumption of undisturbed stratigraphy, which may not always be the case due to natural or human-induced disturbances. 
The Seriation Method is a statistical technique used to date sites based on the presence and frequency of specific artifacts, and is useful when other dating methods are not available. However, its failure mode lies in the assumption that artifact styles change at a constant rate, which may not be accurate. 
The Harris Matrix is a framework used to record and analyze the stratigraphic relationships between different layers and features at a site, providing a detailed and systematic understanding of the site's occupational history. Its failure mode lies in the complexity of the matrix, which can be difficult to interpret and may not account for all possible relationships. 
The Binfordian Model is a framework used to analyze the formation processes of the archaeological record, taking into account the natural and cultural factors that contribute to the creation of the record. This model is useful for understanding the taphonomy of a site, but its failure mode lies in the assumption that all formation processes can be accounted for, which may not be the case due to the complexity of the processes involved. 
The Middle Range Theory is a framework used to link the archaeological record to the past human behavior that created it, by developing and testing hypotheses about the relationships between artifacts and human activities. Its failure mode lies in the difficulty of establishing a direct link between the archaeological record and past human behavior, which may be influenced by various factors such as preservation and sampling biases. This method is particularly useful for understanding the occupation history of a site, but its failure mode lies in the potential for disturbance or contamination of the layers, which can lead to incorrect interpretations. It is most effective when used in conjunction with other methods, such as radiocarbon dating, but its failure mode occurs when the matrix becomes overly complex, making it difficult to interpret. The Binfordian model of site formation processes is used to understand how sites are created and transformed over time, taking into account factors such as natural processes, human activities, and post-depositional disturbances. This model is useful for reconstructing the taphonomy of a site, but its failure mode lies in its reliance on a thorough understanding of the site's formation history, which may not always be available. The formula for calculating the Minimum Number of Individuals (MNI) is used in zooarchaeology to estimate the number of animals represented by a collection of bones, taking into account the number of identified specimens and their skeletal elements. This formula is useful for understanding the subsistence strategies of past societies, but its failure mode occurs when the sample size is too small or the bones are too fragmented, leading to inaccurate estimates. These methods and frameworks, among others, provide a structured approach to analyzing and interpreting archaeological data, allowing researchers to reconstruct the past and understand the complexities of human societies.

## Worked Examples

1. Dating a Ceramic Artifact: A ceramic artifact is found at an archaeological site with a known occupation span of 1000 BCE to 500 CE. The artifact is analyzed using thermoluminescence dating, which yields an age of 2500 years. To determine the date of the artifact, we subtract the age from the present day (assuming the present day is 2023 CE): 2023 CE - 2500 years = 523 BCE. This date falls within the known occupation span, supporting the authenticity of the artifact.

2. Calculating Population Density: An archaeological site covers 10 hectares and has yielded evidence of 20 dwellings. Assuming an average of 5 people per dwelling, the total population can be estimated as 20 dwellings * 5 people/dwelling = 100 people. To calculate the population density, we divide the total population by the site area: 100 people / 10 hectares = 10 people/hectare.

3. Interpreting Stratigraphic Layers: A stratigraphic excavation reveals three layers: Layer 1 (top) contains artifacts from the Roman period (0-500 CE), Layer 2 contains artifacts from the Hellenistic period (300-0 BCE), and Layer 3 (bottom) contains artifacts from the Bronze Age (3000-1200 BCE). Using the principle of superposition, we can infer the relative chronology of the layers: Layer 1 (Roman) is the most recent, followed by Layer 2 (Hellenistic), and then Layer 3 (Bronze Age). This interpretation is based on the principle that older layers are buried beneath younger layers.

## Applications

Archaeological methods and techniques are used to locate, excavate, and conserve historical sites and artifacts, providing valuable insights into the past. In cultural resource management, archaeologists work with government agencies, private companies, and indigenous communities to identify and protect cultural heritage sites from destruction or damage due to development projects. Historical preservation efforts rely on archaeological research to inform the restoration and conservation of historic buildings, monuments, and landscapes. Additionally, archaeology informs museum exhibitions and collections, enabling curators to contextualize and interpret artifacts for the public. The application of archaeological principles also extends to forensic science, where techniques such as stratigraphy and material analysis are used to investigate crimes and reconstruct past events. ## Common Errors

In the field of archaeology, practitioners often make mistakes that can compromise the integrity of their findings and interpretations. One common error is the failure to consider the context of an artifact, leading to incorrect assumptions about its significance and function. For example, excavating an artifact without properly documenting its stratigraphic location can make it difficult to determine its chronological context, potentially leading to misinterpretation of the artifact's role in the culture being studied. Another error is the reliance on incomplete or inaccurate historical records, which can result in a skewed understanding of the past. Additionally, archaeologists may fall into the trap of presentism, projecting modern cultural values and beliefs onto ancient societies, rather than attempting to understand the cultural context in which the artifacts were created. This can lead to misinterpretation of artifacts and a lack of understanding of the cultural nuances of the past. Furthermore, the failure to consider alternative explanations for observed phenomena can result in overly simplistic or misleading conclusions. It is essential for archaeologists to approach their work with a critical and nuanced perspective, recognizing the complexity and variability of human cultures and the potential for multiple interpretations of the same data. By being aware of these common errors, archaeologists can strive to produce more accurate and comprehensive understandings of the past.

## Advanced

They explore the intersection of archaeology with other disciplines like anthropology, history, and sociology. Advanced studies in archaeology often involve critiques of traditional methodologies and the development of new approaches, such as post-processualism and feminist archaeology. Open questions in the field include the role of archaeology in contemporary society, the impact of globalization on cultural heritage, and the ethics of collecting and displaying archaeological artifacts.

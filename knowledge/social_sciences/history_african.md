## Foundations

African history is the systematic study of human societies, state-formation, economic networks, and cultural evolutions on the African continent from hominid origins to the contemporary era. It rejects the Eurocentric "historical vacuum" myth by utilizing an interdisciplinary methodology combining historical linguistics, archaeological stratigraphy, archaeometallurgy, paleoclimatology, genetics, and oral historiography. First principles dictate: (1) Africa is the primary locus of human evolutionary development (phylogenetic origin); (2) African historical trajectories are driven by internal dynamics, environmental adaptation (such as the desertification of the Sahara starting ~3500 BCE), and indigenous technological innovations (such as independent iron metallurgy in the Great Lakes and Termit regions); and (3) historical reconstruction requires decolonizing the archive by cross-referencing oral traditions with external documentary sources (Arabic, Ge'ez, European) and empirical scientific data.

VANSINA'S METHODOLOGY OF ORAL TRADITION (HISTORIOGRAPHY): Jan Vansina’s framework codifies oral tradition as a reliable historical source through systematic textual criticism. The process follows five rigorous phases: (1) Identification of the transmission chain (chain of informants from the initial observer to the current performer); (2) Typological classification of the tradition (e.g., *dinye* historical narratives, *clio* genealogies, panegyrics, or epic poetry like the Sunjata Epic); (3) Internal criticism to detect structural bias, mnemonic distortions, and political functions (e.g., legitimizing a ruling dynasty); (4) External corroboration using archaeological horizons, solar eclipse records (e.g., the 1680 Libanza eclipse in Central Africa), or written accounts; and (5) Reconstruction of the "archetype" text by stripping away accretion layers added over generations.

## The Greenberg-Ehret Linguistic Phylogeny Model

This model reconstructs precolonial African migrations and cultural history using historical-comparative linguistics. Joseph Greenberg classified African languages into four distinct phyla: Afroasiatic, Nilo-Saharan, Niger-Congo, and Khoisan. Christopher Ehret refined this by applying glottochronology and lexicostatistics to track divergence dates. The method involves: (1) compiling a 100-word Swadesh core vocabulary list for related languages; (2) calculating the percentage of shared cognates ($C$); (3) applying the glottochronological formula $t = \ln(C) / (2 \ln(r))$, where $r$ is the retention rate (typically 80.5% per millennium) and $t$ is time depth; and (4) analyzing loanwords for technological innovations (e.g., reconstructing Proto-Bantu terms for "iron" *-gèdà* and "agriculture" *-kàdà* to map the spread of metallurgy and farming from the Grassfields of Cameroon across Sub-Saharan Africa starting ~3000 BCE).

## Common Errors

In the study of African History within the humanities, several common errors arise from misconceptions, oversimplifications, or lack of contextual understanding. One prevalent mistake is the tendency to view African history through a Eurocentric lens, where African societies are judged against European standards of development, politics, and culture. This approach neglects the unique historical trajectories, cultural achievements, and political systems that have existed in Africa. For instance, the perception of African states as "tribal" or "primitive" ignores the complex, organized empires and kingdoms that have flourished on the continent, such as the Kingdom of Kongo, the Ashanti Empire, and the Zulu Kingdom.

Another error is the homogenization of African experiences, treating the continent as a single, monolithic entity rather than acknowledging its vast diversity of languages, cultures, and historical paths. This simplification overlooks the significant regional differences and the impacts of external factors like slavery, colonialism, and globalization on various African societies.

Furthermore, there's a tendency to underplay the agency of African peoples in shaping their own history, instead attributing historical changes primarily to external actors such as European colonizers. This perspective diminishes the role of African leaders, movements, and populations in resisting colonial rule, fighting for independence, and constructing post-colonial nations.

Lastly, the failure to critically engage with sources, particularly those from the colonial period, can lead to the perpetuation of biased narratives. Many historical accounts from this time were written from a colonial perspective, often justifying domination and portraying African societies in a negative light. It's crucial for historians to approach these sources with a critical eye, recognizing their limitations and seeking out African voices and perspectives to provide a more balanced understanding of African history.

# Social Psychology

> The course teaches core concepts, key terminology, and vocabulary of social psychology, assuming no prior study.

## Foundations

Social psychology, a branch of social sciences, examines how individuals' thoughts, feelings, and behaviors are influenced by the presence of others, either directly or indirectly. A **social group** refers to two or more individuals who share a common identity, purpose, or set of norms. **Social influence** occurs when an individual's attitudes, beliefs, or behaviors are affected by others, often through **conformity**, **compliance**, or **obedience**. Conformity involves changing one's behavior to match a group's norms, while compliance entails going along with a request or rule, and obedience refers to following direct orders from an authority figure. **Social norms** are unwritten rules that govern behavior in a particular context, shaping what is considered acceptable or unacceptable. **Attitudes** are evaluations or feelings towards a person, object, or idea, consisting of cognitive (beliefs), affective (emotions), and behavioral (actions) components. Understanding these core concepts is essential for analyzing **social interactions**, which involve reciprocal exchanges between individuals, and **intergroup relations**, which examine interactions between different social groups. Key vocabulary also includes **in-group** (the group to which an individual belongs) and **out-group** (a group to which an individual does not belong), as these distinctions can significantly impact social perceptions and behaviors.

## Mechanisms

Social psychology operates through various mechanisms that influence human behavior and interactions. The process begins with perception, where individuals interpret and organize sensory information from their environment. This perception is then influenced by cognitive biases, such as the fundamental attribution error, where people overestimate the role of personality and underestimate the impact of situational factors in shaping behavior.

As individuals perceive and interpret their social world, they form attitudes, which are evaluations of people, objects, or ideas. These attitudes can be explicit, consciously held, or implicit, operating outside of conscious awareness. Attitudes, in turn, influence behavior through the principle of consistency, where individuals tend to act in ways that align with their attitudes to reduce cognitive dissonance.

Social influence is another key mechanism, where the thoughts, feelings, and behaviors of others affect an individual's own. This can occur through conformity, where individuals adopt the behaviors or attitudes of a group to fit in, or obedience, where individuals comply with the directives of an authority figure. Additionally, social learning theory posits that people learn new behaviors and attitudes by observing and imitating others, a process facilitated by observation, imitation, and reinforcement.

The causal chain in social psychology is complex and bidirectional. For instance, an individual's behavior can influence others' attitudes and behaviors, which in turn can feedback to affect the original individual's perceptions and attitudes. Understanding these mechanisms provides insight into how social psychological phenomena, such as persuasion, group dynamics, and interpersonal attraction, actually work.

## Methods And Frameworks

Social psychology employs various methods and frameworks to understand social behavior and interactions. The Experimental Method is used to establish cause-and-effect relationships between variables, involving the manipulation of independent variables and measurement of dependent variables. Use it to test hypotheses about social phenomena, but be aware of its failure mode: artificial laboratory settings may not generalize to real-world situations. 
The Survey Method involves collecting self-report data through questionnaires or interviews, suitable for descriptive research and exploratory studies. However, it is prone to social desirability bias and respondent dishonesty. 
The Observational Method, including naturalistic observation and participant observation, is used to study social behavior in natural settings. Its failure mode lies in observer bias and reactivity, where the observer's presence influences the behavior being observed. 
The Social Learning Theory model, formulated by Albert Bandura, explains how people learn new behaviors through observation and reinforcement. Use it to understand the acquisition of behaviors, but recognize its limitation in accounting for cognitive processes. 
The Theory of Planned Behavior formula, developed by Icek Ajzen, predicts behavioral intentions based on attitudes, subjective norms, and perceived behavioral control. Apply it to understand the precursors to voluntary behaviors, but be aware of its failure mode: it may not account for non-volitional or habitual behaviors. 
The Six-Category Model of Social Influence, proposed by Robert Cialdini, categorizes influence tactics into reciprocity, commitment and consistency, social proof, authority, liking, and scarcity. Use it to understand persuasive strategies, but recognize its limitation in explaining individual differences in susceptibility to influence.

## Worked Examples

1. **Conformity Experiment**: In a classic study, 5 confederates and 1 participant were shown a line segment and asked to identify its length compared to 3 other lines. The confederates gave incorrect answers, and the participant conformed to the group's opinion 32% of the time. If 100 participants were tested, and the conformity rate was 32%, how many participants conformed? 
Answer: 32 participants conformed (100 x 0.32 = 32). 
2. **Social Identity Theory**: A study on group membership found that 60% of participants identified as members of Group A, while 40% identified as members of Group B. If 150 participants were surveyed, how many identified as Group A members? 
Answer: 90 participants identified as Group A members (150 x 0.6 = 90). 
3. **Attitude Change**: A persuasion campaign aimed to change attitudes towards a social issue. The campaign resulted in a 25% increase in favorable attitudes among 200 participants. How many participants showed a change in attitude? 
Answer: 50 participants showed a change in attitude (200 x 0.25 = 50).

Answer: 80 * 0.8 = 64 students.

If department A has a 25% higher salary average, and social identity theory states that individuals derive self-esteem from group membership, what percentage of employees would be expected to identify strongly with their department, assuming a direct correlation between salary and identification? 
Answer: Since department A has a higher salary, 100% of department A and 0% of department B would identify strongly, for simplicity. However, in reality, the relationship is complex and influenced by multiple factors. 
3. Cognitive Dissonance: A person buys a product for $100, but later discovers it is available for $80. If the person experiences dissonance, what actions might they take to reduce it, according to social psychology principles? 
Answer: The person might rationalize the purchase by emphasizing the product's quality or uniqueness, or they might seek social support by reading positive reviews or asking friends for validation, to reduce the dissonance between their actions and the new information.

## Applications

Social psychology has numerous practical applications in various domains. In marketing, understanding social influence and persuasion techniques helps develop effective advertising campaigns. For instance, the use of social proof, such as customer testimonials, can increase consumer trust and purchasing decisions. In public health, social psychology informs the design of interventions aimed at promoting healthy behaviors, like exercise and vaccination, by leveraging social norms and group identity. In education, social psychology principles, such as social learning theory and self-efficacy, are applied to improve student motivation, engagement, and academic achievement. Additionally, social psychology is used in organizational settings to enhance teamwork, leadership, and communication, as well as to address issues like workplace discrimination and harassment. In the realm of policy-making, social psychology insights are used to develop and evaluate programs aimed at promoting social cohesion, reducing prejudice, and fostering community engagement. By understanding how social factors influence human behavior, social psychology provides a foundation for developing evidence-based interventions and policies that can positively impact individuals and society.

## Common Errors

In social psychology, practitioners often make mistakes that can lead to flawed conclusions and interventions. One common error is the fundamental attribution error, where individuals overestimate the role of personality and underestimate the impact of situational factors in shaping behavior. Another mistake is the false consensus effect, where people overestimate the extent to which others share their opinions and behaviors. The actor-observer bias is also prevalent, where individuals attribute their own behavior to situational factors but attribute others' behavior to their character. Additionally, the self-serving bias leads people to attribute their successes to internal factors and their failures to external factors. These errors occur due to cognitive biases and heuristics, such as the availability heuristic, where people overestimate the importance of vivid or readily available information. Furthermore, the illusion of control and the illusion of asymmetric insight also lead to errors in social perception and judgment. Understanding these common errors is essential to develop effective interventions and to improve social psychological research. By recognizing these biases and heuristics, practitioners can take steps to mitigate their influence and develop more accurate and nuanced understandings of social behavior.

## Advanced

Social psychology's advanced topics delve into the intricacies of human interaction, cognition, and behavior. Graduate-level research explores the complexities of social influence, group dynamics, and interpersonal relationships. The study of social identity theory, for instance, examines how individuals derive a sense of self from group memberships and how this affects intergroup relations. Another area of investigation is the concept of emotional intelligence, which involves the ability to recognize and regulate emotions in oneself and others. Open questions in the field include the role of culture in shaping social psychological phenomena, the impact of technology on social interactions, and the development of effective interventions to promote social change. The field is moving towards a greater emphasis on diversity, equity, and inclusion, with researchers examining the social psychological factors underlying social injustices and developing strategies to address them. Additionally, the integration of social psychology with other disciplines, such as neuroscience and sociology, is yielding new insights into the biological and structural underpinnings of social behavior. As the field continues to evolve, researchers are incorporating innovative methodologies, such as social network analysis and big data analytics, to study social psychological phenomena in greater depth and complexity.

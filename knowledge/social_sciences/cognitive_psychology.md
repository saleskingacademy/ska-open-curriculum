# Cognitive Psychology

> The course covers core concepts, key terminology, and principles of cognitive psychology, assuming some basic knowledge of social sciences.

## Foundations

Cognitive psychology, a branch of social sciences, examines mental processes that underlie human behavior, including perception, attention, memory, language, problem-solving, and decision-making. **Cognition** refers to the processes by which we acquire, process, and use knowledge. A **cognitive process** is a mental operation that takes in information, transforms it, and produces an output, such as a perception, thought, or action. **Mental representation** is the way in which we organize and store knowledge in our minds, including concepts, schemas, and scripts. **Schemas** are mental frameworks that help us organize and make sense of information, while **scripts** are mental representations of routine events or activities. **Cognitive bias** refers to a systematic error in thinking or decision-making, resulting from mental shortcuts or heuristics. Understanding these core concepts is essential for analyzing human behavior and developing interventions to improve cognitive functioning. **Social cognition** is a subfield that explores how we perceive, interpret, and interact with others, including **attribution theory**, which examines how we explain the causes of behavior.

Familiarity with these core definitions and principles is essential for practitioners to investigate cognitive phenomena in social contexts.

## Mechanisms

Cognitive psychology in social sciences examines the mental processes that underlie human behavior, including perception, attention, memory, language, problem-solving, and decision-making. The causal chain begins with sensory input, which is processed through attentional mechanisms that filter out irrelevant information. This filtered information is then encoded into memory, either into short-term memory (STM) or long-term memory (LTM), depending on the depth of processing and the presence of attention. Information in STM is temporarily held and either rehearsed, leading to transfer to LTM, or decayed and forgotten. In LTM, information is stored in an associative network, where concepts and ideas are interconnected, facilitating retrieval through spreading activation. When information is retrieved from memory, it is reconstructed based on the associative network and the context in which it is recalled, a process known as reconstruction. This reconstructed information then influences perception, decision-making, and behavior, completing the causal chain. The mechanisms of cognitive psychology are dynamic and interactive, with each stage influencing the next, and are shaped by both bottom-up (sensory input) and top-down (expectations, prior knowledge) processes. Understanding these mechanisms is crucial for explaining human behavior in social contexts.

Cognitive psychology in social sciences examines the mental processes that underlie human behavior and social interactions. Language plays a crucial role in shaping cognitive processes, as it influences how individuals think, communicate, and interact with others. Decision-making is the outcome of these cognitive processes, where individuals weigh options, consider social norms and expectations, and make choices that impact their behavior and social interactions. The causal chain is as follows: attention influences perception, perception affects memory, memory informs language and decision-making, and decision-making, in turn, impacts behavior and social interactions. Understanding these mechanisms is essential for explaining how individuals process social information, form attitudes and opinions, and engage in social behaviors.

## Methods And Frameworks

Cognitive psychology in social sciences employs various methods and frameworks to understand mental processes. The Think-Aloud Protocol is used to gather data on cognitive processes, where participants verbalize their thoughts while performing a task. This method is useful for understanding decision-making and problem-solving, but its failure mode lies in potential biases due to social desirability and reactivity. 
The Cognitive Load Theory (CLT) framework is applied to understand how cognitive resources are allocated during learning and task performance. CLT is useful for instructional design, but its failure mode occurs when it oversimplifies complex cognitive processes. 
The Theory of Planned Behavior (TPB) model is utilized to predict behavioral intentions, incorporating attitudes, subjective norms, and perceived behavioral control. TPB is useful for understanding health behaviors, but its failure mode lies in neglecting the role of habits and emotions. 
The Dual-Process Theory (DPT) framework distinguishes between automatic and controlled processing, useful for understanding biases and heuristics. DPT's failure mode occurs when it fails to account for the interplay between the two processes. 
The Signal Detection Theory (SDT) model is applied to understand how individuals make decisions under uncertainty, useful for understanding perception and memory. SDT's failure mode lies in its assumption of a fixed decision criterion, neglecting contextual influences. 
These methods and frameworks provide a foundation for understanding cognitive processes in social sciences, but their limitations must be acknowledged to ensure accurate application.

## Worked Examples

To illustrate key concepts in cognitive psychology, consider the following examples. 
1. **Attention and Distraction**: A researcher investigates the impact of distractions on attention. Participants complete a task requiring focused attention, with and without distractions. Results show that, on average, participants complete the task 30% faster without distractions. This demonstrates the concept of attention as a limited resource, where distractions compete for cognitive resources.
2. **Memory Recall**: A study examines the effect of retrieval practice on memory recall. Participants learn a list of 20 words, then practice recalling them either 1, 3, or 5 times. Results indicate that participants who practiced recall 5 times remembered 85% of the words, compared to 60% for those who practiced only once. This illustrates the principle of spaced repetition in strengthening memory.
3. **Decision Making**: A experiment tests the influence of framing effects on decision making. Participants are presented with a scenario where they can either gain $100 or lose $100, framed as either a 50% chance of gaining $100 or a 50% chance of losing $100. Results show that 70% of participants prefer the gain-framed option, while 40% prefer the loss-framed option. This demonstrates the concept of framing effects, where the presentation of information influences decision-making processes.

This outcome reflects the principle of loss aversion, where the fear of losing $100 (if the gamble fails) outweighs the potential gain, influencing decision-making processes.

## Applications

Cognitive psychology has numerous applications in social sciences, particularly in fields such as education, marketing, and public policy. In education, understanding how people process and retain information informs the development of effective instructional strategies, such as spaced repetition and active recall. In marketing, cognitive psychology principles like priming, framing effects, and cognitive biases are used to design persuasive advertisements and influence consumer behavior. Additionally, cognitive psychology informs public policy initiatives, such as campaigns to promote healthy behaviors or reduce energy consumption, by leveraging insights into decision-making and behavioral change. Furthermore, cognitive psychology is applied in human-computer interaction to design user-friendly interfaces and improve user experience. The field also informs social influence and persuasion techniques, which are crucial in areas like political communication and social movements. By understanding how people perceive, process, and respond to information, social scientists can develop evidence-based interventions to address various social issues.

## Common Errors

In cognitive psychology, as studied in social sciences, practitioners often make mistakes that can lead to flawed conclusions and interventions. One common error is the fundamental attribution error, where individuals overestimate the role of personality and underestimate the impact of situational factors in shaping behavior. This error occurs because people tend to focus on dispositional factors, such as character and intentions, rather than considering the influence of environmental and social contexts. Another error is the confirmation bias, where individuals selectively seek and interpret information that confirms their pre-existing beliefs, while ignoring or dismissing contradictory evidence. This bias can lead to a narrow and inaccurate understanding of cognitive processes and behaviors. Additionally, the hindsight bias, where individuals believe that they would have predicted an outcome after it has occurred, can also lead to errors in understanding cognitive processes. These errors can be attributed to cognitive heuristics, such as mental shortcuts and rules of thumb, which can simplify decision-making but also lead to systematic biases and errors. Furthermore, the tendency to rely on intuitive judgments, rather than systematic and empirical evidence, can also lead to errors in cognitive psychology. By recognizing these common errors, practitioners can take steps to mitigate their influence and develop more accurate and effective theories and interventions.

## Advanced

In cognitive psychology, graduate-level research explores the complexities of human cognition, delving into the neural mechanisms underlying cognitive processes. One key area of extension is the study of embodied cognition, which posits that cognitive processes are deeply rooted in sensorimotor experiences. This perspective challenges traditional notions of cognition as solely a product of the brain, instead emphasizing the interplay between the brain, body, and environment. Another area of advancement is the investigation of cognitive biases and heuristics, which has significant implications for understanding decision-making and judgment in social contexts. Open questions in the field include the development of a unified theory of cognitive processing, which would integrate findings from various subfields, such as attention, memory, and language. Furthermore, the increasing use of neuroimaging techniques, such as functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI), has raised questions about the relationship between brain activity and cognitive processes, highlighting the need for more nuanced understandings of the neural correlates of cognition. As the field continues to evolve, researchers are incorporating insights from related disciplines, such as social neuroscience and anthropology, to better understand the complex interplay between cognitive processes, culture, and social context.

# Supply Chain Strategy

> The course teaches foundational concepts, key principles, and methods of supply chain strategy, indicating a lower-division course.

## Foundations

A supply chain is a network of entities, including organizations, people, and activities, involved in the production and delivery of a product or service. It encompasses the entire lifecycle of a product, from raw material sourcing to end-customer delivery. Core definitions include: 
**Supply Chain Management (SCM)**, the coordination and management of supply chain activities to maximize efficiency and effectiveness. 
**Logistics**, the planning, coordination, and execution of the movement and storage of goods, services, and related information. 
Key principles include: 
**Just-In-Time (JIT)**, a strategy that aims to produce and deliver products just in time to meet customer demand, reducing inventory and waste. 
**Total Cost of Ownership (TCO)**, the total cost of acquiring, operating, and maintaining a product or service over its entire lifecycle. 
Vocabulary includes: 
**Upstream**, referring to the supply chain activities that occur before the production process, such as sourcing and procurement. 
**Downstream**, referring to the supply chain activities that occur after production, such as distribution and delivery. 
Understanding these definitions, principles, and vocabulary is essential for a practitioner to develop and implement effective supply chain strategies.

In the context of business, a supply chain refers to the network of organizations, people, and activities involved in the production and delivery of a product or service. A supply chain strategy is a comprehensive plan that outlines how a company will manage its supply chain to achieve its business objectives. Key terms include: 
**Supply Chain Management (SCM)**, the coordination and management of supply chain activities to maximize efficiency and effectiveness; 
**Logistics**, the planning, coordination, and execution of the movement and storage of goods, services, and related information; 
**Procurement**, the process of acquiring goods, services, or works from external sources; 
**Inventory Management**, the process of planning, organizing, and controlling the storage and movement of goods or materials; 
**Distribution**, the process of delivering products or services to customers. 
Understanding these concepts is crucial for developing an effective supply chain strategy that aligns with business goals and objectives.

## Mechanisms

In business, a supply chain strategy operates through several key mechanisms that enable the flow of goods, services, and information from raw materials to end customers. The causal chain begins with demand forecasting, where historical data and market trends are analyzed to predict future demand. This forecast informs the production planning process, determining the quantity and timing of goods to be produced. The production plan triggers the procurement of raw materials and components, which are sourced from suppliers based on factors such as cost, quality, and lead time. The sourcing decision is influenced by the supply chain strategy, which may prioritize low-cost country sourcing, nearshoring, or local sourcing. Once materials are procured, they are transported to manufacturing facilities, where they are transformed into finished goods. The finished goods are then stored in inventory until they are shipped to customers, either directly or through intermediaries such as distributors or wholesalers. Throughout this process, information flows through the supply chain, enabling visibility, tracking, and coordination among stakeholders. The supply chain strategy also influences the design of the logistics network, including the location and capacity of warehouses, distribution centers, and transportation modes. Ultimately, the effectiveness of the supply chain strategy is measured by its ability to deliver the right product, in the right quantity, at the right time, and at the right cost, thereby meeting customer demand and driving business success.

In a business context, supply chain strategy involves a series of coordinated mechanisms that enable the flow of goods, services, and information from raw materials to end customers. Production planning, in turn, triggers the procurement of raw materials and components from suppliers. The sourcing mechanism involves selecting and contracting with suppliers, negotiating prices, and managing inventory levels. As goods are produced, they are stored in warehouses or distribution centers, from which they are shipped to customers through various transportation modes. The inventory management mechanism ensures that stock levels are optimized to meet demand while minimizing holding costs. The logistics mechanism oversees the movement of goods, managing transportation, warehousing, and delivery to ensure timely and cost-effective delivery. Throughout the supply chain, information flows through various systems, including enterprise resource planning (ERP), supply chain management (SCM) software, and electronic data interchange (EDI), enabling real-time tracking, monitoring, and decision-making. The payment mechanism facilitates financial transactions between buyers and suppliers, while the risk management mechanism identifies and mitigates potential disruptions, such as supplier insolvency or natural disasters. Ultimately, the supply chain strategy aims to create a competitive advantage by delivering high-quality products, reducing costs, and improving customer satisfaction.

## Methods And Frameworks

In supply chain strategy, several methods and frameworks are employed to analyze, design, and optimize supply chains. The SCOR (Supply Chain Operations Reference) model is used to evaluate and improve supply chain performance, focusing on five key processes: plan, source, make, deliver, and return. It is particularly useful for identifying areas of inefficiency and measuring supply chain performance. However, its failure mode lies in its complexity, requiring significant data and resources to implement effectively. 
The McKinsey 7S framework is applied to assess and align supply chain strategy with organizational design, focusing on strategy, structure, systems, skills, style, staff, and shared values. It is useful for ensuring supply chain strategy alignment with overall business objectives but may fail if not regularly updated to reflect changing market conditions. 
The Total Cost of Ownership (TCO) formula is used to calculate the total cost of acquiring and maintaining a product or service, considering factors such as procurement, inventory, and logistics costs. It is useful for evaluating supplier options and optimizing procurement decisions but may fail if it does not account for all relevant costs or if cost structures change over time. 
The Just-In-Time (JIT) method aims to minimize inventory levels by producing and delivering products just in time to meet customer demand. It is useful for reducing inventory costs and improving supply chain responsiveness but may fail if supply chain disruptions occur or if demand is highly variable. 
The Six Sigma methodology is applied to improve supply chain quality and reduce defects, using statistical tools and techniques to analyze and optimize processes. It is useful for identifying and eliminating supply chain inefficiencies but may fail if not supported by top management or if it becomes too focused on certification rather than continuous improvement. It is useful for ensuring strategic alignment but can be time-consuming and may not account for external factors. It is essential for making informed sourcing decisions but may overlook qualitative factors like supplier reliability. It is effective in reducing inventory costs but is vulnerable to supply chain disruptions and may not be suitable for products with variable demand. The Six Sigma methodology is used to improve supply chain quality and efficiency by identifying and eliminating defects. It is useful for reducing variability but can be resource-intensive and may not address strategic issues. Each method and framework has its strengths and limitations, and the choice of which to use depends on the specific supply chain goals, industry, and organizational context.

## Worked Examples

To illustrate the application of supply chain strategy in business, consider the following examples. 
1. A company producing electronics has two potential suppliers for a critical component: Supplier A offers a price of $10 per unit with a lead time of 6 weeks, while Supplier B offers a price of $12 per unit with a lead time of 3 weeks. If the company requires 1000 units per week and has a holding cost of $0.50 per unit per week, which supplier is more cost-effective? 
The total cost for Supplier A is the purchase price plus the holding cost for the longer lead time: $10 + ($0.50 * 6 weeks * 1000 units/week) = $10 + $3000 = $13 per unit (amortized). 
For Supplier B, it is $12 + ($0.50 * 3 weeks * 1000 units/week) = $12 + $1500 = $12.50 per unit (amortized). 
Thus, Supplier B is more cost-effective despite the higher purchase price due to the reduced lead time and associated holding costs. 
2. A retailer is deciding between a just-in-time (JIT) inventory system and a traditional inventory system. The JIT system requires a $100,000 investment in new inventory management software but reduces average inventory levels from $500,000 to $200,000. If the cost of capital is 10%, which system is more cost-effective? 
The traditional system ties up $500,000 in inventory, costing $50,000 per year in capital costs (10% of $500,000). 
The JIT system ties up $200,000, costing $20,000 per year, but also requires the $100,000 software investment, which can be amortized over its expected life. 
Assuming a 5-year life for the software, the annualized cost is $20,000 (amortization) + $20,000 (inventory cost) = $40,000 per year. 
Since $40,000 (JIT) < $50,000 (traditional), the JIT system is more cost-effective. 
3. A manufacturer must choose between air freight and ocean freight for shipping products from a supplier. Air freight costs $1.50 per unit with a lead time of 2 days, while ocean freight costs $0.50 per unit with a lead time of 30 days. If the manufacturer sells 500 units per day at a price of $10 per unit and has a cost of goods sold of $6 per unit, which shipping method is more profitable? 
The revenue per day is $10 * 500 = $5000. 
The cost of goods sold per day is $6 * 500 = $3000. 
The inventory holding cost for air freight is negligible due to the short lead time, while for ocean freight, it is significant: 30 days * $3000/day = $90,000 in inventory. 
Assuming a 10% cost of capital, the daily holding cost for ocean freight is $90,000 * 10% / 365 days ≈ $25. 
Thus, the profit per day using air freight is $5000 - $3000 - ($1.50 * 500) = $2000 - $750 = $1250. 
For ocean freight, it is $5000 - $3000 - ($0.50 * 500) - $25 = $2000 - $250 - $25 = $1725. 
Therefore, air freight is more profitable despite its higher shipping cost due to reduced inventory holding costs. ## Applications

In business, Supply Chain Strategy is applied in practice through various approaches, including segmentation, where companies categorize their products or services based on their supply chain requirements, and tailor their strategies accordingly. For instance, a company may adopt a responsive strategy for high-demand, low-lead-time products, and an efficient strategy for low-demand, high-lead-time products. Another application is through the use of third-party logistics (3PL) providers, which enable companies to outsource their logistics operations and focus on core competencies. Additionally, companies apply Supply Chain Strategy through the implementation of just-in-time (JIT) production, which aims to minimize inventory levels and reduce waste by producing and delivering products just in time to meet customer demand. Furthermore, the use of technology, such as enterprise resource planning (ERP) systems and transportation management systems (TMS), enables companies to optimize their supply chain operations, improve visibility, and enhance collaboration with suppliers and customers. By applying these strategies, companies can improve their supply chain resilience, reduce costs, and enhance customer satisfaction.

In business, supply chain strategy is applied in various industries to improve efficiency, reduce costs, and enhance customer satisfaction. Companies like Walmart and Amazon have implemented efficient supply chain strategies, enabling them to offer competitive pricing and fast delivery. For instance, Walmart's supply chain strategy involves a hub-and-spoke distribution system, where goods are transported from suppliers to regional distribution centers and then to retail stores. This approach allows for reduced transportation costs and improved inventory management. Similarly, Amazon's supply chain strategy focuses on building a network of fulfillment centers close to customers, enabling fast and reliable delivery. In the automotive industry, companies like Toyota have implemented just-in-time (JIT) production, where components are delivered to the production line just in time to meet demand, reducing inventory holding costs and improving production efficiency. Effective supply chain strategy also involves managing risks, such as supply disruptions and demand fluctuations, through strategies like diversification, hedging, and contingency planning. By applying supply chain strategy principles, businesses can create a competitive advantage and improve their bottom line.

## Common Errors

Practitioners often make mistakes in supply chain strategy due to misconceptions about cost, capacity, and customer needs. A common error is prioritizing cost reduction over service level, leading to inventory shortages and stockouts. This approach neglects the impact of stockouts on customer satisfaction and loyalty. Another mistake is overemphasizing efficiency in supply chain design, resulting in inflexible systems that cannot respond to changes in demand or supply. Additionally, failing to consider the total cost of ownership, including logistics, inventory holding, and quality costs, can lead to suboptimal sourcing decisions. Furthermore, neglecting to align supply chain strategy with business objectives, such as market growth or product innovation, can result in a mismatch between supply chain capabilities and business needs.

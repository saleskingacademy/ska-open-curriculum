# Logistics

> The course teaches specialized logistics concepts with analytical models and methods, assuming prior knowledge of business and management principles.

## Foundations

Logistics is the science and practice of planning, implementing, and controlling the efficient, effective forward and reverse flow and storage of goods, services, and related information between the point of origin and the point of consumption to meet customer requirements. Rooted in military supply chain management, logistics integrates transportation, inventory management, warehousing, material handling, packaging, and information flow. At its core, logistics optimizes the trade-offs between cost, speed, reliability, and flexibility under constraints of demand variability, lead times, capacity, and service level expectations. The first principles include the conservation of flow (inbound = outbound + inventory change), economies of scale and scope in transportation and storage, and the bullwhip effect as a systemic amplification of demand variability through the supply chain.

Logistics, in a business context, refers to the planning, coordination, and execution of the flow of goods, services, and related information from point of origin to point of consumption. A key concept is the Supply Chain, defined as a network of organizations, people, and activities involved in the production and delivery of a product or service. Core components of logistics include Transportation, which involves the movement of goods from one location to another, and Inventory Management, the process of planning, organizing, and controlling the storage and movement of goods. Warehousing is another critical aspect, referring to the storage of goods in a facility. Distribution, the process of getting products to customers, is also a fundamental element. First principles of logistics include the concept of Just-In-Time (JIT) delivery, which aims to minimize inventory by producing and delivering products just in time to meet customer demand. Vocabulary essential for logistics practitioners includes terms like Freight, the goods being transported, and Carrier, the entity responsible for transporting the goods. Understanding these definitions and principles is crucial for effective logistics management in business.

## Inventory Management

The Economic Order Quantity (EOQ) model, formulated by Ford W. Harris (1913), remains foundational. EOQ = √(2DS/H), where D = annual demand units, S = ordering cost per order, H = holding cost per unit per year. EOQ balances ordering and holding costs to minimize total inventory cost. Extensions include the Newsvendor model for perishable or single-period inventory, optimizing order quantity Q* = F⁻¹( (p-c)/p ), where p = selling price, c = cost, and F⁻¹ is the inverse demand distribution function. Safety stock calculation uses the formula SS = Z × σL × √L, where Z is the service factor (e.g., 1.65 for 95% service level), σL is demand standard deviation during lead time L. These models underpin reorder point (ROP) systems: ROP = dL + SS, where d is average demand per unit time.

## Transportation Logistics

The Vehicle Routing Problem (VRP), introduced by Dantzig and Ramser (1959), is central to distribution logistics. VRP seeks to minimize total route cost while servicing a set of customers with vehicle capacity constraints. Exact methods include branch-and-bound and column generation; heuristics like Clarke-Wright Savings Algorithm compute routes by maximizing savings S_ij = c_i0 + c_0j - c_ij, where c_ij is cost between nodes i and j, and 0 is the depot. The Transportation Problem, solved via the Northwest Corner Rule and the MODI method (Modified Distribution), optimizes shipment allocation to minimize total transportation cost subject to supply and demand constraints. Freight consolidation leverages economies of scale, balancing inventory holding costs against transportation cost savings.

## Warehousing & Material Handling

The Cube-per-Order Index (COI) ranks SKUs by volume per order frequency to optimize slotting: COI_i = V_i / F_i, where V_i is cubic volume per unit and F_i is annual order frequency. This guides storage location assignments to reduce picking time. The ABC analysis segments inventory by Pareto principle: A (top 20% SKUs accounting for ~80% value), B (next 30%), and C (remaining 50%). Automated Storage and Retrieval Systems (AS/RS) use algorithms like the Traveling Salesman Problem (TSP) for picker routing optimization. Cross-docking minimizes storage by synchronizing inbound and outbound flows, reducing inventory holding to near zero.

## Supply Chain Network Design

Facility location and network design problems are modeled via Mixed Integer Linear Programming (MILP). The capacitated facility location problem minimizes total fixed and variable costs: minimize ∑_j F_j y_j + ∑_i ∑_j c_ij x_ij, subject to demand satisfaction ∑_j x_ij = d_i, capacity constraints ∑_i x_ij ≤ C_j y_j, and binary facility open variables y_j. Heuristics like Lagrangian relaxation and Benders decomposition solve large-scale instances. Network design incorporates multi-echelon inventory positioning, leveraging the METRIC model (Multi-Echelon Technique for Recoverable Item Control) to optimize spare parts stocking across distribution centers and service points.

## Reverse Logistics

Reverse logistics manages returns, recycling, and disposal. The Closed-Loop Supply Chain (CLSC) framework integrates forward and reverse flows. The return rate (r) and recovery rate (γ) influence inventory and cost models. The remanufacturing lot size Q* can be derived from the Wilson EOQ formula adjusted for returns: Q* = √(2DS / H(1 - rγ)). Reverse logistics requires coordination of collection points, inspection, sorting, and refurbishment, often modeled via network flow optimization with constraints on quality and timing. Environmental regulations (e.g., WEEE Directive) mandate compliance logistics, adding complexity to system design.

## Logistics Information Systems

Enterprise Resource Planning (ERP) systems integrate logistics modules with real-time data for demand forecasting, order processing, and shipment tracking. Advanced Planning and Scheduling (APS) systems use constraint-based optimization and heuristics to balance capacity and demand. Radio Frequency Identification (RFID) technology enhances inventory accuracy and traceability, enabling event-driven logistics. Key Performance Indicators (KPIs) include On-Time Delivery (OTD), Fill Rate, Inventory Turnover Ratio (ITR = COGS / Average Inventory), and Perfect Order Rate. Data analytics and machine learning increasingly optimize route planning, demand forecasting (e.g., ARIMA, LSTM models), and risk management.

## Mechanisms

Logistics in business involves a series of coordinated steps to manage the flow of goods, services, and related information from raw materials to end customers. The causal chain begins with demand forecasting, where historical data and market trends are analyzed to predict future demand. This forecast triggers the procurement process, where raw materials or goods are sourced from suppliers. The procurement mechanism involves issuing purchase orders, negotiating prices, and arranging transportation. Once the goods are received, they are stored in warehouses, where inventory management systems track stock levels, monitor product condition, and optimize storage space. As customer orders are received, the order processing mechanism kicks in, involving picking, packing, and shipping the goods to the customer. Transportation management plays a critical role, with options including road, rail, air, or sea freight, each with its own cost, speed, and reliability considerations. At each stage, information flows through the supply chain, enabling tracking, tracing, and monitoring of goods in real-time. The final step involves delivery to the customer, where the logistics provider may also offer additional services such as installation, maintenance, or returns management. Throughout the process, logistics service providers and businesses use various performance metrics, such as lead time, inventory turnover, and fill rates, to measure efficiency and identify areas for improvement.

## Methods And Frameworks

In logistics, several methods and frameworks are employed to optimize supply chain operations. The Just-In-Time (JIT) method is used to minimize inventory levels by producing and receiving inventory just in time to meet customer demand. This method is effective in reducing inventory costs but can fail if there are supply chain disruptions or inaccurate demand forecasting. The Total Cost of Ownership (TCO) model is used to calculate the total cost of a product or service, including acquisition, operating, and maintenance costs. This model is useful in evaluating supplier options but can be incomplete if it does not account for all relevant costs. The Economic Order Quantity (EOQ) formula is used to determine the optimal order quantity that minimizes total inventory costs, considering factors such as demand rate, ordering cost, and holding cost. This formula is effective in managing inventory levels but can be limited if it does not account for factors such as lead time and supply chain variability. The Six Sigma methodology is used to improve supply chain quality by identifying and eliminating defects, with a goal of achieving near-perfect quality. This methodology is effective in reducing errors but can be resource-intensive and may not be suitable for all types of logistics operations. The SCOR (Supply Chain Operations Reference) model is a framework used to evaluate and improve supply chain performance, covering aspects such as plan, source, make, deliver, and return. This model is useful in benchmarking and improving supply chain operations but can be complex to implement and may require significant data and resources.

## Worked Examples

To illustrate key logistics concepts, consider the following examples. 
1. **Transportation Mode Selection**: A company needs to transport 1000 units of product from a warehouse in Chicago to a distribution center in New York. The product weighs 10 pounds per unit. Truck transportation costs $1.50 per mile, with a total distance of 790 miles. Rail transportation costs $0.50 per mile, with a total distance of 820 miles, but has a fixed surcharge of $500. Calculate the total cost of each mode and select the most economical option. 
For truck: 790 miles * $1.50 per mile = $1185. 
For rail: 820 miles * $0.50 per mile = $410 + $500 surcharge = $910. 
Select rail transportation as it is cheaper. 
2. **Inventory Holding Costs**: A retailer holds an average inventory of 5000 units of a product, with an annual holding cost of 20% of the product's value. If the product costs $10 per unit, calculate the total annual holding cost. 
Annual holding cost = 5000 units * $10 per unit * 20% = $10,000. 
3. **Supply Chain Optimization**: A manufacturer produces 2000 units of a product per day, with a daily demand of 1500 units. The production cost per unit is $5, and the holding cost per unit per day is $0.10. Calculate the total daily cost of production and holding. 
Production cost = 2000 units * $5 per unit = $10,000. 
Holding cost = 500 units * $0.10 per unit per day = $50. 
Total daily cost = $10,000 + $50 = $10,050.

1. A company ships 1000 units of product from a warehouse in Chicago to a distribution center in New York. The shipping cost is $0.50 per mile, and the distance is 790 miles. What is the total shipping cost? 
Total shipping cost = distance * shipping cost per mile = 790 * $0.50 = $395.
2. A retailer requires a minimum inventory level of 500 units to meet customer demand. If the lead time is 7 days and the daily demand is 100 units, what is the reorder point? 
Reorder point = lead time * daily demand = 7 * 100 = 700 units. When the inventory level falls to 700 units, the retailer should reorder to maintain the minimum inventory level.

A manufacturer has two potential warehouse locations: one with a fixed cost of $10,000 per month and variable cost of $5 per unit, and another with a fixed cost of $8,000 per month and variable cost of $6 per unit. If the expected monthly demand is 2000 units, which location is more cost-effective? 
Total cost for location 1 = fixed cost + (variable cost * demand) = $10,000 + ($5 * 2000) = $10,000 + $10,000 = $20,000.
Total cost for location 2 = fixed cost + (variable cost * demand) = $8,000 + ($6 * 2000) = $8,000 + $12,000 = $20,000.
Both locations have the same total cost, so other factors such as proximity to customers or transportation infrastructure should be considered when making a decision.

## Applications

In business, logistics is applied in various ways to manage the flow of goods, services, and information from raw materials to end customers. Companies use logistics to optimize their supply chain operations, reducing costs and improving efficiency. This involves coordinating activities such as procurement, inventory management, transportation, and warehousing. For instance, a manufacturer might use logistics to source raw materials, manage production, and distribute finished goods to retailers. Logistics is also crucial in e-commerce, where companies like Amazon and Walmart rely on efficient logistics systems to deliver products directly to customers. Additionally, logistics is applied in other industries, including healthcare, where it is used to manage the supply of medical equipment and pharmaceuticals, and in food production, where it is used to manage the transportation and storage of perishable goods. Effective logistics management enables businesses to improve customer satisfaction, reduce lead times, and increase competitiveness in the market. By analyzing logistics data, companies can identify areas for improvement and implement strategies to streamline their operations, such as just-in-time inventory management and route optimization.

## Common Errors

In logistics, practitioners often make mistakes that can lead to increased costs, decreased efficiency, and reduced customer satisfaction. One common error is failing to consider the total cost of ownership when selecting transportation modes or logistics providers. This can result in choosing a provider based on low upfront costs, only to incur higher costs in the long run due to poor service, damage to goods, or inefficient routing. Another mistake is inadequate inventory management, such as failing to implement just-in-time (JIT) systems or not accounting for lead times, which can lead to stockouts or overstocking. Additionally, logistics practitioners often underestimate the importance of data analysis and visibility in the supply chain, neglecting to track key performance indicators (KPIs) or failing to invest in technology that provides real-time monitoring and tracking. This lack of visibility can make it difficult to identify and address issues, leading to delays and increased costs. Furthermore, failing to consider the impact of logistics on the overall business strategy, such as not aligning logistics with marketing or sales goals, can result in missed opportunities and decreased competitiveness. By understanding these common errors, logistics practitioners can take steps to avoid them and create more efficient, effective, and customer-focused logistics systems.

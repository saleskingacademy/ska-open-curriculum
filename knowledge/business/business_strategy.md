## Foundations

Strategy is the set of choices about where to play and how to win that create sustainable competitive advantage. It is fundamentally about trade-offs — deciding what NOT to do. Strategy answers: What business are we in? Who are our customers? What unique value do we deliver? Why will we win and keep winning?

PORTER'S FIVE FORCES: Industry attractiveness is shaped by — 1) Threat of new entrants (barriers to entry). 2) Bargaining power of suppliers. 3) Bargaining power of buyers. 4) Threat of substitutes. 5) Competitive rivalry. Analyze these to understand profit potential and position accordingly. Strong forces compress margins; weak forces enable profit.

COMPETITIVE ADVANTAGE (Porter's Generic Strategies): Three paths — 1) Cost Leadership (be the lowest-cost producer). 2) Differentiation (offer unique value worth a premium). 3) Focus (dominate a narrow niche via cost or differentiation). The fatal error is being "stuck in the middle" — neither cheapest nor most distinctive.

Business strategy refers to the long-term plan of action designed to achieve a particular goal or set of goals, typically related to gaining a competitive advantage, increasing market share, or maximizing profitability. A practitioner must understand key terms such as **mission statement**, which defines the purpose, goals, and primary objectives of an organization. The **vision statement** outlines what an organization wants to become or achieve in the future. **Objectives** are specific, measurable, achievable, relevant, and time-bound (SMART) targets that an organization seeks to accomplish. 
**Stakeholders** are individuals or groups with an interest or concern in the organization, including shareholders, employees, customers, and suppliers. **Competitive advantage** refers to a unique benefit or attribute that sets an organization apart from its competitors, such as innovative products, low-cost production, or exceptional customer service. Understanding these core definitions and vocabulary is essential for developing and implementing effective business strategies.

## Swot & Positioning

Strengths and Weaknesses (internal), Opportunities and Threats (external). Position where your strengths meet market opportunity and competitors are weak. Strategic positioning is about being different, not just better — occupying a distinct space in the customer's mind.

## Value Chain & Core Competencies

The value chain maps every activity that adds value (inbound logistics, operations, marketing, service, plus support activities). Competitive advantage comes from performing key activities better or cheaper. Core competencies — the unique capabilities hard for rivals to copy — are the true foundation of advantage.

## Blue Ocean Strategy

Instead of competing in bloody "red oceans" of existing demand, create uncontested "blue ocean" market space. Value innovation — simultaneously pursue differentiation AND low cost by eliminating/reducing industry factors while raising/creating new ones. Make competition irrelevant.

GROWTH STRATEGIES (Ansoff Matrix): Market Penetration (more of existing product to existing market), Market Development (existing product, new markets), Product Development (new product, existing market), Diversification (new product, new market — highest risk). Choose based on capability and risk appetite.

## Business Models & Moats

A business model defines how you create, deliver, and capture value. Economic moats (Buffett) protect profits — network effects, switching costs, cost advantages, intangible assets (brand, patents), and efficient scale. The durability of advantage matters more than its size today.

## Execution & Strategic Planning

Strategy without execution is hallucination. Align structure, incentives, capabilities, and culture to the strategy. Use OKRs (Objectives and Key Results) to cascade goals. Balanced Scorecard tracks financial, customer, process, and learning metrics. Revisit strategy as conditions change — it's a hypothesis to test, not a monument.

## Mastery Levels

L1 — understand competitive forces and positioning. L2-3 — apply strategic frameworks. L4-5 — design competitive strategy and business models. L6-7 — lead strategic transformation. L8 Grandmaster — teach strategy and shape industry-level moves.

## Mechanisms

Business strategy operates through a series of interconnected mechanisms that drive organizational performance. It begins with environmental scanning, where firms gather data on market trends, competitors, and customer needs. This information is then used to formulate a mission statement, which defines the company's purpose and objectives. The next step involves conducting a SWOT analysis, identifying the organization's strengths, weaknesses, opportunities, and threats. This analysis informs the development of strategic goals, which are specific, measurable, achievable, relevant, and time-bound (SMART). 
The strategic goals are then translated into actionable plans through the allocation of resources, such as capital, talent, and technology. This resource allocation is guided by the company's value chain, which outlines the series of activities that create value for customers. The value chain includes primary activities like production and logistics, as well as support activities like marketing and human resources. 
As the strategy is implemented, firms establish key performance indicators (KPIs) to monitor progress and make adjustments as needed. This continuous feedback loop enables organizations to adapt to changing market conditions, stay focused on their strategic objectives, and ultimately achieve sustainable competitive advantage.

## Methods And Frameworks

Business strategy employs various methods, models, and formulas to analyze and develop competitive advantage. The SWOT analysis is used to identify internal strengths and weaknesses, and external opportunities and threats. It is applied when assessing market position and competitive landscape. Failure mode: oversimplification of complex issues. The Porter's Five Forces model analyzes industry competition, barriers to entry, and bargaining power of suppliers and buyers. It is used when evaluating industry attractiveness and potential profitability. Failure mode: neglecting external factors. The Boston Consulting Group (BCG) growth-share matrix categorizes products into stars, cash cows, question marks, and dogs, based on market growth and relative market share. It is applied when prioritizing product investments and divestments. Failure mode: misallocation of resources due to incorrect market growth assumptions. The Ansoff Matrix provides a framework for growth strategies, including market penetration, market development, product development, and diversification. It is used when developing growth strategies and assessing risk. Failure mode: inadequate market research and analysis. The McKinsey 7S framework assesses strategy, structure, systems, skills, style, staff, and shared values to ensure organizational alignment. It is applied when implementing strategic change and ensuring organizational effectiveness. Failure mode: neglecting one or more of the 7S elements, leading to strategic misalignment.

Understanding the appropriate application and limitations of these methods is crucial for effective business strategy development.

2. A retail firm like Walmart, with an average store size of 100,000 sq. ft., wants to reduce its costs by 5% to maintain profitability. By implementing a strategy of supply chain optimization and reducing store size to 80,000 sq. ft., Walmart can achieve cost savings of $1.5 million per store, resulting in a 6% reduction in costs.
3. A company like Amazon, with a customer retention rate of 75%, wants to increase its retention rate to 80%. By implementing a customer relationship management (CRM) strategy that includes personalized marketing and loyalty programs, Amazon can increase customer satisfaction and retention, resulting in an additional $10 million in revenue per quarter.

## Common Errors

In business strategy, common errors include failing to conduct thorough market analysis, resulting in unrealistic assumptions about target markets and customer needs. Another mistake is prioritizing short-term financial gains over long-term sustainability, leading to decisions that compromise a company's competitive advantage. Many practitioners also confuse strategy with tactics, focusing on operational efficiency rather than addressing the underlying questions of how to compete and create value. Additionally, neglecting to consider the dynamic nature of the competitive environment and failing to adapt strategies accordingly can render even the best-laid plans ineffective. Furthermore, overreliance on generic strategies without consideration of a company's unique resources and capabilities can lead to a lack of differentiation and competitiveness. These errors often stem from inadequate understanding of the strategic management process, insufficient analysis of internal and external factors, and a lack of alignment between strategic objectives and organizational capabilities.

The resource-based view (RBV) and the knowledge-based view (KBV) are also explored in depth, as they provide frameworks for understanding how firms leverage internal resources and knowledge to achieve sustainable competitive advantages. Additionally, graduate-level studies examine the role of entrepreneurship, innovation, and organizational ambidexterity in driving strategic success. Open questions in the field include the impact of digital transformation on traditional business models, the integration of sustainability and social responsibility into strategic decision-making, and the development of effective strategies for managing global supply chains and navigating complex, multicultural environments. The field is moving towards a greater emphasis on strategic agility, resilience, and adaptability, as well as the application of advanced analytics and artificial intelligence to inform strategic decision-making. Furthermore, there is a growing recognition of the importance of stakeholder engagement, corporate social responsibility, and ethical considerations in shaping business strategy.

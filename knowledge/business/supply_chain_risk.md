# Supply Chain Risk

## Identification

Risk categories (supply, demand, operational, environmental, geopolitical). Tier mapping (sub-tier visibility). Single source identification. MITIGATION: Dual sourcing, safety stock buffers, supplier diversification (geographic, tier), nearshoring/reshoring, inventory positioning. RESILIENCE: Business continuity planning (BCP), disaster recovery, supply chain stress testing. Agility vs efficiency trade-off. DISRUPTIONS: Pandemic (COVID-19 lessons), natural disasters, cyber attacks, trade wars, Suez Canal blockage. Black swan events.

## Foundations

In logistics supply chain, supply chain risk refers to the potential occurrence of events that can disrupt the normal flow of goods, services, and information from raw material sourcing to end customer delivery. A **supply chain** is a network of organizations, people, and activities involved in the production and delivery of a product or service. **Risk** is the likelihood and potential impact of an event that can affect the supply chain's ability to achieve its objectives. **Logistics** encompasses the planning, coordination, and execution of activities related to the procurement, storage, and transportation of goods. A **disruption** is an event that interrupts the normal flow of supply chain activities, such as natural disasters, supplier insolvency, or transportation accidents. **Resilience** refers to the supply chain's ability to withstand and recover from disruptions. Understanding these core definitions is essential for practitioners to identify, assess, and mitigate supply chain risks. Key vocabulary includes **supply chain vulnerability**, which refers to the susceptibility of a supply chain to disruptions, and **risk mitigation**, which involves implementing strategies to reduce the likelihood or impact of potential disruptions.

## Mechanisms

In logistics supply chain, risk materializes through a series of interconnected events and decisions. The mechanism of supply chain risk involves the identification of potential threats, assessment of their likelihood and impact, and the implementation of mitigation strategies. It begins with the sourcing of raw materials, where suppliers may face operational risks such as natural disasters, equipment failure, or labor disputes. These risks can disrupt the supply of critical components, leading to delays or shortages in production. The causal chain is as follows: supplier insolvency or disruption (cause) leads to inventory stockouts or delays (effect), which in turn causes production slowdowns or stoppages (effect), resulting in late or incomplete shipments to customers (effect). Effective risk management mechanisms involve monitoring supplier performance, diversifying the supplier base, and developing contingency plans to mitigate the impact of disruptions. By understanding these mechanisms, logistics and supply chain managers can proactively manage risks, reduce the likelihood of disruptions, and minimize their impact on the supply chain.

## Methods And Frameworks

In logistics supply chain, several methods and frameworks are used to identify, assess, and mitigate supply chain risks. The Failure Mode and Effects Analysis (FMEA) is a widely used method to identify potential failure modes in the supply chain, assess their likelihood and impact, and prioritize mitigation efforts. The Supply Chain Risk Management (SCRM) framework provides a structured approach to identifying, assessing, and mitigating risks, and is typically used in conjunction with other methods such as the Supply Chain Operations Reference (SCOR) model. The SCOR model provides a framework for analyzing and improving supply chain performance, and can be used to identify potential risks and develop mitigation strategies. The Monte Carlo simulation is a statistical method used to model and analyze complex supply chain systems, and can be used to assess the potential impact of risks on supply chain performance. Each of these methods has its own strengths and weaknesses, and the choice of method will depend on the specific needs and goals of the organization. For example, FMEA is particularly useful for identifying potential failures in the supply chain, while the SCOR model is more focused on analyzing and improving overall supply chain performance. The failure mode of these methods often lies in their inability to account for unforeseen events or "black swans", highlighting the need for ongoing monitoring and review of supply chain risks.

## Worked Examples

To illustrate the application of supply chain risk management, consider the following examples. 
1. A manufacturer sources components from a single supplier. The supplier has a 10% chance of stockout, resulting in a 5-day delay. If the manufacturer's daily holding cost is $100 and the cost of expedited shipping is $500, the expected cost of this risk is (0.1 * $100 * 5) + (0.1 * $500) = $50 + $50 = $100.
2. A retailer relies on a single distribution center. The center has a 5% chance of being affected by a natural disaster, resulting in a 2-week closure. If the retailer's weekly sales are $10,000 and the cost of lost sales is 50% of sales, the expected cost of this risk is 0.05 * $10,000 * 2 * 0.5 = $500.
3. A company transports goods via a single mode of transportation. The transportation mode has a 2% chance of accident, resulting in a 1-day delay. If the company's daily inventory holding cost is $50 and the cost of accident response is $200, the expected cost of this risk is (0.02 * $50 * 1) + (0.02 * $200) = $1 + $4 = $5.

To illustrate the concept of supply chain risk in logistics, consider the following examples. 
1. A manufacturer relies on a single supplier for a critical component. The supplier has a 10% chance of stockout, which would result in a $100,000 loss per day for the manufacturer. Using the expected monetary value (EMV) formula, the expected loss per day is calculated as: EMV = Probability * Loss = 0.10 * $100,000 = $10,000 per day. 
2. A retailer sources products from two suppliers, A and B. Supplier A has a 5% chance of delay, resulting in a $50,000 loss, while Supplier B has a 2% chance of delay, resulting in a $20,000 loss. The expected loss for each supplier is calculated as: EMV_A = 0.05 * $50,000 = $2,500 and EMV_B = 0.02 * $20,000 = $400. 
3. A logistics company transports goods via two routes, Route X and Route Y. Route X has a 15% chance of disruption due to weather, resulting in a $15,000 loss, while Route Y has a 5% chance of disruption due to traffic, resulting in a $10,000 loss. The expected loss for each route is calculated as: EMV_X = 0.15 * $15,000 = $2,250 and EMV_Y = 0.05 * $10,000 = $500. These examples demonstrate how to quantify supply chain risk using probability and loss data, enabling logistics professionals to make informed decisions.

## Common Errors

In logistics supply chain management, common errors in assessing and mitigating supply chain risk include overreliance on sole sourcing, failure to map the entire supply chain, and inadequate consideration of low-probability, high-impact events. Many practitioners mistakenly focus solely on supplier risk, neglecting other risk categories such as transportation, inventory, and demand risks. Another error is underestimating the interdependencies between different components of the supply chain, leading to a lack of resilience and inadequate contingency planning. Furthermore, some practitioners fail to regularly review and update their risk assessments, rendering them ineffective in responding to changing market conditions and emerging risks. Additionally, the use of overly simplistic risk assessment tools and methodologies can lead to inaccurate risk prioritization, resulting in misallocated resources and ineffective mitigation strategies. These errors can be attributed to a lack of understanding of the complexities of supply chain risk, inadequate training, and insufficient investment in risk management capabilities.

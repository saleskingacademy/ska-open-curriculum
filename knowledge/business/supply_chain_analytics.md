# Supply Chain Analytics

> The course assumes prior knowledge of business and management principles and applies advanced analytics techniques to real-world supply chain problems.

## Descriptive

Dashboards, KPIs (OTIF, perfect order, inventory turns, fill rate, cycle time). Data visualization (Tableau, Power BI). DIAGNOSTIC: Root cause analysis, Pareto analysis, correlation analysis. Supply chain mapping and visibility. PREDICTIVE: Demand forecasting (time series, ML — gradient boosting, neural networks), lead time prediction, disruption risk modeling. PRESCRIPTIVE: Optimization (linear programming, mixed-integer programming), simulation (Monte Carlo, discrete event), scenario planning. TOOLS: Supply chain planning platforms (SAP IBP, Blue Yonder, Kinaxis), data lakes, ETL pipelines. DATA: EDI (Electronic Data Interchange), IoT/sensors, POS data, weather data, social media signals. APPLICATIONS: Inventory optimization, transportation routing, network design, supplier risk scoring, S&OP (Sales and Operations Planning).

## Foundations

In logistics supply chain, supply chain analytics refers to the application of quantitative methods and data analysis to improve decision-making across the supply chain. A **supply chain** is a network of organizations, people, and activities involved in the production and delivery of a product or service, from raw material sourcing to end-customer delivery. **Logistics** encompasses the planning, coordination, and execution of activities such as transportation, inventory management, and warehousing. **Analytics** involves the use of statistical and mathematical techniques to analyze data and drive business decisions. Key concepts include **descriptive analytics** (examining historical data to identify trends), **predictive analytics** (using statistical models to forecast future events), and **prescriptive analytics** (providing recommendations for action based on data analysis). A **practitioner** must understand **data visualization** (presenting data in a graphical format), **regression analysis** (modeling the relationship between variables), and **optimization techniques** (using algorithms to find the best solution among possible options). **Supply chain visibility** (the ability to track and monitor supply chain activities in real-time) and **demand forecasting** (predicting future customer demand) are critical applications of supply chain analytics. Key concepts include **demand forecasting**, which is the process of predicting future demand for a product or service, and **supply chain visibility**, which refers to the ability to track and monitor products, services, and information as they move through the supply chain. Practitioners must understand **key performance indicators (KPIs)**, which are measurable values that demonstrate how effectively an organization is achieving its objectives, such as **lead time**, **inventory turnover**, and **fill rate**. A thorough understanding of these foundational concepts is essential for effective supply chain analytics. A **supply chain** is a network of entities, including suppliers, manufacturers, distributors, and customers, that work together to design, produce, and deliver products or services. **Logistics** encompasses the planning, coordination, and execution of activities related to the movement and storage of goods, products, and resources. Key concepts include **demand forecasting**, which is the process of predicting future demand for products or services, and **inventory management**, which refers to the planning and control of inventory levels to meet customer demand while minimizing costs. A **node** is a point in the supply chain where goods are stored, processed, or transferred, such as a warehouse or distribution center. **Edges** represent the connections between nodes, including transportation links and communication channels. Understanding these core definitions and principles is essential for a practitioner to effectively apply supply chain analytics and optimize logistics operations.

## Mechanisms

In logistics supply chain, supply chain analytics involves a series of mechanisms that work together to analyze and optimize supply chain operations. The process begins with data collection, where relevant data is gathered from various sources such as enterprise resource planning (ERP) systems, transportation management systems (TMS), and warehouse management systems (WMS). This data is then cleaned, transformed, and loaded into a data warehouse or a cloud-based platform for analysis. The next step involves applying statistical models and machine learning algorithms to identify patterns, trends, and correlations within the data. These models help to forecast demand, predict lead times, and detect potential disruptions in the supply chain. The insights generated from these analyses are then used to optimize supply chain operations, such as routing and scheduling shipments, managing inventory levels, and selecting the most efficient transportation modes. The causal chain is as follows: data collection leads to data analysis, which informs decision-making, resulting in optimized supply chain operations, and ultimately, improved efficiency and reduced costs. Key performance indicators (KPIs) such as fill rates, inventory turnover, and on-time delivery rates are used to measure the effectiveness of these optimizations and identify areas for further improvement. The next step involves applying analytics tools and techniques, such as predictive modeling, simulation, and optimization algorithms, to analyze the data and identify trends, patterns, and areas for improvement. The insights gained from this analysis are then used to inform decision-making, such as optimizing inventory levels, streamlining transportation routes, and improving supply chain visibility.

## Methods And Frameworks

In logistics supply chain, various methods and frameworks are employed to analyze and optimize supply chain operations. The Economic Order Quantity (EOQ) model is used to determine the optimal order quantity, minimizing total inventory costs. It is applicable when demand is constant and lead time is fixed. However, it fails when demand is variable or lead time is uncertain. The Just-In-Time (JIT) framework focuses on producing and delivering products just in time to meet customer demand, reducing inventory levels. It is suitable for companies with stable demand and reliable suppliers, but fails when demand is unpredictable or suppliers are unreliable. The Total Cost of Ownership (TCO) model calculates the total cost of acquiring and maintaining products or services, considering factors such as procurement, inventory, and transportation costs. It is useful for evaluating supplier performance and negotiating contracts, but may fail to account for intangible costs or external factors. The Six Sigma methodology aims to reduce defects and variations in supply chain processes, using statistical tools and techniques. It is effective for improving quality and efficiency, but may fail if not adapted to the specific supply chain context or if it becomes too bureaucratic. The SCOR (Supply Chain Operations Reference) model provides a framework for evaluating and improving supply chain performance, using metrics such as lead time, inventory turns, and perfect order fulfillment. It is applicable for benchmarking and identifying areas for improvement, but may fail if not tailored to the company's specific supply chain strategy or if metrics are not properly aligned. The SCOR (Supply Chain Operations Reference) model is a widely used framework that categorizes supply chain processes into plan, source, make, deliver, and return. The Six Sigma methodology, specifically DMAIC (Define, Measure, Analyze, Improve, Control), is applied to identify and eliminate defects in supply chain processes. The Economic Order Quantity (EOQ) formula, which calculates the optimal order quantity to minimize total inventory costs, is used when demand is constant and lead times are fixed. The Bullwhip Effect, a phenomenon where small changes in demand lead to large fluctuations in inventory, is mitigated using techniques such as vendor-managed inventory and collaborative planning. The failure mode of EOQ is its assumption of constant demand, which can lead to stockouts or overstocking when demand is variable. The Theory of Constraints (TOC) method, which identifies and manages bottlenecks in supply chain processes, is used when there are constraints in production or distribution. The failure mode of TOC is its focus on a single constraint, which can lead to neglect of other important factors. The Total Cost of Ownership (TCO) framework, which considers all costs associated with a product or service, is used to evaluate supplier performance and make sourcing decisions. The failure mode of TCO is its complexity, which can lead to inaccurate cost estimates and poor decision-making. The Just-In-Time (JIT) framework is utilized to reduce inventory levels and minimize waste, suitable for environments with stable demand and reliable suppliers. Its failure mode arises when supply chain disruptions occur or demand is volatile. The Bullwhip Effect framework is used to analyze the impact of demand variability on supply chain operations, applicable when demand is uncertain and lead time is long. Its failure mode occurs when supply chain visibility is limited or forecasting accuracy is low. It is applicable when procurement decisions need to be made, but its failure mode arises when indirect costs are not accounted for or cost structures are complex. The Six Sigma framework is used to improve supply chain quality and reduce defects, suitable for environments with high variability or complex processes. Its failure mode occurs when cultural or organizational barriers hinder implementation or when metrics are not properly defined.

## Worked Examples

To illustrate the application of supply chain analytics in logistics, consider the following examples. 
1. **Inventory Optimization**: A company has a warehouse with a capacity of 1000 units and currently holds 600 units of a product. The demand forecast for the next period is 400 units, and the lead time is 2 weeks. The holding cost per unit per week is $1, and the stockout cost per unit per week is $5. Using the Economic Order Quantity (EOQ) model, we calculate the optimal order quantity as follows: EOQ = √((2*400*5)/1) = √4000 = 63.25 units. Since the warehouse has sufficient capacity, we can order 63 units to meet the demand and minimize costs.
2. **Transportation Optimization**: A logistics company needs to transport 150 units from a warehouse in City A to a distribution center in City B, which is 200 miles away. The company has two transportation options: using a truck with a capacity of 100 units and a cost of $500 per trip, or using a train with a capacity of 200 units and a cost of $800 per trip. To minimize transportation costs, we calculate the cost per unit for each option: Truck = $500/100 = $5 per unit, Train = $800/200 = $4 per unit. Since the train option has a lower cost per unit, we choose to transport the units by train.
3. **Supply Chain Network Optimization**: A manufacturer has two suppliers, A and B, and two warehouses, X and Y. The transportation costs from suppliers to warehouses are: A to X = $10, A to Y = $15, B to X = $12, B to Y = $8. The demand at warehouse X is 100 units, and at warehouse Y is 150 units. The capacity of supplier A is 120 units, and supplier B is 180 units. To optimize the supply chain network, we use linear programming to minimize the total transportation cost. The optimal solution is to transport 100 units from supplier A to warehouse X, and 150 units from supplier B to warehouse Y, resulting in a total transportation cost of $1000 + $1200 = $2200. 
2. **Transportation Optimization**: A logistics provider needs to transport 1000 units from a warehouse to two retail stores, one requiring 600 units and the other 400 units. The transportation costs from the warehouse to the stores are $10 and $15 per unit, respectively. **Supply Chain Network Optimization**: A company has three manufacturing plants, two warehouses, and five retail stores. The production cost at each plant is $50, $60, and $70 per unit, respectively. The transportation cost from each plant to each warehouse, and from each warehouse to each retail store, varies. Using a network optimization model, such as the Minimum Spanning Tree or the Shortest Path algorithm, the optimal supply chain network can be designed to minimize total costs.

## Common Errors

In logistics supply chain analytics, common mistakes include relying solely on historical data to predict future demand, failing to account for variability in supply chain operations, and neglecting to consider the impact of external factors such as weather, traffic, and economic trends. Another error is the incorrect application of forecasting methods, such as using a single method for all products or locations, rather than selecting methods based on the specific characteristics of each product or location. Additionally, practitioners often overlook the importance of data quality and integrity, leading to inaccurate analysis and decision-making. Furthermore, the failure to consider the trade-offs between different performance metrics, such as cost, service level, and inventory levels, can result in suboptimal decisions. These errors can be attributed to a lack of understanding of the underlying supply chain dynamics, inadequate data analysis, and insufficient consideration of the complexities and uncertainties inherent in logistics supply chain operations.

In logistics supply chain analytics, common errors often stem from incorrect assumptions, inadequate data analysis, and flawed modeling techniques. One mistake is ignoring variability in demand and supply, leading to inaccurate forecasts and inventory management. Practitioners may also fail to account for lead time variability, resulting in stockouts or overstocking. Another error is using simplistic metrics, such as average lead time, without considering the underlying distribution and potential outliers. Additionally, some practitioners rely too heavily on historical data, neglecting to incorporate external factors like seasonality, weather, or economic trends that can impact supply chain performance. Incorrectly applying optimization techniques, such as linear programming, to complex nonlinear problems can also lead to suboptimal solutions. Furthermore, failing to consider the bullwhip effect, where small changes in demand are amplified upstream, can result in inefficient supply chain operations. These errors can be mitigated by using robust analytical techniques, such as stochastic modeling and simulation, and by considering the complexities and uncertainties inherent in logistics supply chain systems.

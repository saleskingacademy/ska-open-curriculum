## Classical Theory

Sun Tzu (Art of War: know yourself and enemy, deception, terrain, winning without fighting). Clausewitz (On War: war as politics by other means, fog of war, friction, center of gravity, culminating point). Jomini (interior/exterior lines, concentration of force). PRINCIPLES OF WAR: Objective, offensive, mass, economy of force, maneuver, unity of command, security, surprise, simplicity. MODERN DOCTRINE: AirLand Battle, maneuver warfare, effects-based operations. COIN (counterinsurgency: protect population, political solution). Multi-domain operations (land, sea, air, space, cyber). LEVELS OF WAR: Strategic (national/theater objectives), operational (campaigns, major operations), tactical (battles, engagements). NUCLEAR STRATEGY: Deterrence, mutually assured destruction (MAD), second-strike capability. Escalation ladder (Kahn). Arms control: NPT, START treaties.

## Foundations

Military strategy refers to the planning and execution of military operations to achieve national or coalition objectives. A practitioner must understand core definitions, first principles, and vocabulary to develop effective strategies. **National Security** is the protection of a nation's interests, values, and institutions from external threats. **Defense** is the act of protecting a nation's territory, population, and interests from attack or aggression. **Deterrence** is a strategy aimed at discouraging an adversary from taking a particular action, by threatening unacceptable consequences. **Military Power** is the ability to apply force to achieve strategic objectives, comprising **Land**, **Sea**, **Air**, and **Space** components. **Strategy** itself is the art and science of employing military power to achieve national objectives, guided by **Principles of War**, such as the **Objective**, **Mass**, **Economy of Forces**, **Maneuver**, **Unity of Command**, **Security**, **Surprise**, and **Simplicity**. Understanding these concepts and their inter relationships is essential for a practitioner to develop effective military strategies.

## Mechanisms

The implementation of military strategy involves a series of mechanisms that facilitate the achievement of strategic objectives. Firstly, the strategic planning process commences with the definition of national or organizational security objectives, which are then translated into military strategic goals. These goals are further broken down into operational and tactical tasks, which are assigned to various military units and formations. The next step involves the allocation of resources, including personnel, equipment, and logistics, to support the execution of these tasks.

A critical mechanism in this process is the establishment of a clear chain of command, which ensures that orders and directives are communicated effectively from higher to lower echelons of command. This chain of command also facilitates the exercise of command and control, which enables military leaders to direct and coordinate the actions of their subordinates.

The operational planning process involves the development of detailed plans and timelines, which outline the sequence of events and actions required to achieve specific objectives. This process also involves the identification of potential risks and threats, as well as the development of contingency plans to mitigate these risks.

The execution of military strategy also relies on effective intelligence, surveillance, and reconnaissance (ISR) mechanisms, which provide critical information on the enemy's disposition, intentions, and capabilities. This information is used to inform decision-making and to adjust military plans and operations accordingly.

Ultimately, the successful implementation of military strategy depends on the ability of military organizations to adapt and evolve in response to changing circumstances and emerging challenges. This requires a culture of innovation and experimentation, as well as a commitment to continuous learning and improvement.

This feedback is used to refine and adjust the tactical plans, ensuring that the military strategy remains effective and adaptive in response to changing circumstances. Throughout this process, the principles of unity of command, unity of effort, and economy of forces are applied to ensure that all military actions are coordinated, focused, and efficient in achieving the strategic objectives.

## Methods And Frameworks

In military strategy, various methods and frameworks are employed to analyze situations, develop plans, and make informed decisions. The OODA Loop (Observe, Orient, Decide, Act) is a widely used model for decision-making, particularly in dynamic and rapidly changing environments. It is most effective when used in situations requiring swift and adaptive responses, such as in counterinsurgency or counterterrorism operations. However, its failure mode lies in the potential for incomplete or inaccurate observation, leading to misguided orientation and decision-making.

The Military Decision-Making Process (MDMP) is a more structured framework, used for planning and executing military operations. It consists of seven steps: receipt of mission, mission analysis, course of action development, course of action analysis, course of action comparison, course of action approval, and orders production. MDMP is best utilized in situations where thorough planning and coordination are essential, such as in large-scale joint operations. Its failure mode can occur if the process becomes overly bureaucratic or time-consuming, leading to delays in decision-making and action.

The CARVER matrix is a targeting methodology used to evaluate and prioritize potential targets based on their criticality, accessibility, return on investment, vulnerability, effect, and recognizability. It is most effective when used in situations where resources are limited, and targeting decisions must be made efficiently. However, its failure mode lies in the potential for subjective weighting of criteria, leading to biased targeting decisions.

The PMESII-PT framework (Political, Military, Economic, Social, Information, Infrastructure, Physical environment, and Time) is used for analyzing the operational environment and understanding the complex interactions between various factors. It is most effective when used in situations where a comprehensive understanding of the environment is necessary, such as in stability operations or counterinsurgency. Its failure mode can occur if the analysis becomes too broad or superficial, failing to identify key factors or relationships.

## Worked Examples

To illustrate the application of military strategy, consider the following scenarios:

1. **Amphibious Assault**: A military force plans to capture a coastal city defended by 5,000 enemy troops. The attacking force has 10,000 troops, 20 tanks, and 50 artillery pieces. Assuming a 3:1 ratio of attackers to defenders is required for a successful assault, what is the minimum number of additional troops needed? 
Solution: The defenders have 5,000 troops, so the attackers need 3 * 5,000 = 15,000 troops. The attacking force currently has 10,000 troops, so they need 15,000 - 10,000 = 5,000 additional troops.

2. **Flanking Maneuver**: A defensive position is anchored on a mountain range, with 2,000 enemy troops deployed along a 10 km front. The defender has 1,500 troops and 10 artillery pieces. If the attacker launches a frontal assault, what is the likelihood of success? 
Solution: A frontal assault against a well-entrenched defender is unlikely to succeed. A more effective approach would be to launch a flanking maneuver, attacking the enemy's vulnerable flank. This requires knowledge of the terrain and the enemy's dispositions.

3. **Logistical Support**: A military force is advancing 300 km into enemy territory, with a supply chain that can support 5,000 troops for 30 days. If the force grows to 10,000 troops, what is the maximum duration of the advance? 
Solution: The supply chain can support 5,000 troops for 30 days, so the total supply is 5,000 * 30 = 150,000 troop-days. With 10,000 troops, the supply will last 150,000 / 10,000 = 15 days. The force must either reduce its size or establish a new supply chain to sustain the advance.

Given: 2,000 defenders, 6,000 attackers, 1,000 defenders pinned at each flank
Solution: Defenders available to defend the gap = 2,000 - 2 * 1,000 = 0. Since the defenders are pinned at the flanks, the attackers can break through the gap with any number of troops, but to ensure success, they should allocate at least a small force to maintain pressure on the pinned defenders.

Solution: Daily transport requirement = 100 tons. Since each asset can make 0.5 trips per day (1 trip / 2 days), each asset can transport 10 tons per day (20 tons/trip * 0.5 trips/day). Minimum assets required = 100 tons/day / 10 tons/day/asset = 10 assets.

## Common Errors

In military strategy, common errors often stem from a lack of understanding of fundamental principles or the misapplication of these principles in dynamic and complex operational environments. One such error is the failure to adhere to the principle of unity of command, where multiple chains of command or unclear lines of authority can lead to confusion, duplication of effort, and ultimately, a lack of cohesion in military operations. Another mistake is underestimating the enemy, which can lead to overconfidence and poor planning, as seen in historical examples where an underestimated adversary has managed to inflict significant casualties or even defeat a more powerful but complacent force. The overreliance on technology without considering the human factor is also a critical error, as it overlooks the psychological and social aspects of warfare, which are crucial for achieving strategic objectives. Furthermore, the failure to adapt strategies in response to changing circumstances on the battlefield is a significant mistake, as military strategy must be flexible to respond effectively to the fluid nature of combat. Lastly, neglecting the principles of economy of forces and mass, where resources are not allocated efficiently or concentrated at the decisive point, can lead to wastage of resources and failure to achieve strategic goals. These errors highlight the importance of a deep understanding of military strategy principles and their flexible application in real-world scenarios.

Misidentifying the center of gravity can lead to misguided efforts and ineffective allocation of resources. Another error is underestimating the importance of logistics and supply chain management, which can severely hinder a military's ability to sustain operations over time. Lastly, the failure to consider the operational and strategic levels of war, and instead focusing solely on the tactical level, can result in a lack of cohesion and overall strategic failure. These errors highlight the importance of a thorough understanding of military strategy principles and the need for adaptability and critical thinking in military planning and execution.

## Foundations

Psychiatry is the medical specialty devoted to the diagnosis, treatment, and prevention of mental, emotional, and behavioral disorders. Rooted in neurobiology, psychology, and social sciences, it integrates biological, psychological, and sociocultural models to understand psychopathology. Core principles include the biopsychosocial model (Engel, 1977), neurochemical and neuroanatomical correlates of mental illness, and evidence-based psychopharmacology and psychotherapy. Psychiatry uniquely combines medical assessment (history, mental status exam, physical/neuro exam) with longitudinal evaluation and multidisciplinary treatment planning.

In the field of psychiatry, a medical specialty concerned with the diagnosis, treatment, and prevention of mental, emotional, and behavioral disorders, several core definitions and principles form the basis of practice. **Mental health** refers to a state of well-being in which an individual realizes their own abilities, can cope with the normal stresses of life, can work productively, and is able to make a contribution to their community. **Psychopathology**, the study of abnormal mental conditions, underpins the understanding of psychiatric disorders. A **psychiatric disorder** is a clinically significant behavioral or psychological syndrome or pattern that occurs in an individual and is associated with present distress or disability or with a significantly increased risk of suffering death, pain, disability, or an important loss of freedom. **Diagnosis** in psychiatry involves identifying and categorizing these disorders, often using standardized classification systems such as the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (DSM) or the International Classification of Diseases (ICD). **Treatment** may include **psychotherapy** (talk therapy), **pharmacotherapy** (medication), or a combination of both, aimed at reducing symptoms, improving quality of life, and enhancing functional capacity. Understanding these foundational concepts is crucial for practitioners to provide effective care and support to individuals with psychiatric disorders.

A **mental disorder**, as defined by the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (DSM), is a clinically significant disturbance in an individual's cognition, emotion regulation, or behavior that reflects a dysfunction in the psychological, biological, or developmental processes underlying mental functioning. **Psychopathology** refers to the study of abnormal mental conditions, which can result from genetic, environmental, or psychological factors. **Psychiatrists**, medical doctors (MDs or DOs) who specialize in psychiatry, use **psychopharmacology** (the study of the use of medications in treating mental disorders) and **psychotherapy** (talking therapies that help individuals understand and change their thoughts, feelings, and behaviors) to manage and treat these conditions. Key principles include understanding the **biopsychosocial model**, which posits that mental health disorders result from the interplay of biological (genetic, neurochemical), psychological (thoughts, emotions), and social (environmental, cultural) factors. Familiarity with the **neurotransmitters** (chemical messengers such as serotonin, dopamine) and their role in mood regulation and other psychological processes is also essential. Vocabulary specific to psychiatry includes terms like **symptom** (a subjective experience reported by the patient), **sign** (an objective observation made by the clinician), and **syndrome** (a set of symptoms that occur together and suggest a specific diagnosis).

## Diagnostic Framework

DSM-5 CRITERIA & STRUCTURED CLINICAL INTERVIEW (SCID)  
The Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition (DSM-5, APA 2013) provides operationalized criteria for over 300 psychiatric diagnoses. Diagnosis requires:  
1) Symptom presence and duration thresholds (e.g., ≥2 weeks of depressed mood or anhedonia for Major Depressive Disorder).  
2) Functional impairment or distress.  
3) Exclusion of medical/organic causes.  
The SCID is a semi-structured interview tool that operationalizes DSM criteria, enhancing diagnostic reliability. Steps:  
- Begin with mood and psychosis screening modules.  
- Use branching logic to explore symptom clusters.  
- Confirm symptom timing, severity, and exclusion criteria.  
- Assign diagnosis per DSM-5 algorithms.

## Neurobiological Models

NEUROTRANSMITTER DYSREGULATION & CIRCUIT DYSFUNCTION  
Psychiatric disorders are conceptualized as dysfunctions in neurotransmitter systems and neural circuits. For example:  
- Major Depression: Monoamine hypothesis implicates serotonin (5-HT), norepinephrine (NE), dopamine (DA) deficits; supported by efficacy of SSRIs (e.g., fluoxetine 20-40 mg/day) and SNRIs.  
- Schizophrenia: Dopamine hypothesis (hyperactivity in mesolimbic pathway) and glutamate NMDA receptor hypofunction.  
- Anxiety Disorders: GABAergic deficits and hyperactive amygdala-prefrontal circuits.  
Neuroimaging (fMRI, PET) and electrophysiology (EEG) provide biomarkers for circuit dysfunction.

## Pharmacotherapy Principles

TARGETS, TITRATION, & MONITORING  
Psychopharmacology relies on receptor pharmacodynamics and pharmacokinetics. Key frameworks:  
- Dose-response curves guide titration (e.g., start sertraline 25-50 mg/day, titrate to 100-200 mg/day).  
- Therapeutic drug monitoring (e.g., lithium serum levels 0.6-1.2 mEq/L).  
- Side effect profiles guide agent selection (e.g., antipsychotics: extrapyramidal symptoms with haloperidol vs metabolic syndrome with olanzapine).  
- Polypharmacy principles: avoid pharmacodynamic antagonism, monitor for QT prolongation (e.g., citalopram max 40 mg/day).  
- Algorithmic treatment sequences (e.g., STAR*D protocol for depression).

## Psychotherapeutic Interventions

CBT & DBT STRUCTURES  
Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT) involves:  
- Assessment of automatic thoughts and cognitive distortions.  
- Behavioral activation and exposure techniques.  
- Structured sessions (~12-20 weekly 50-minute sessions).  
Dialectical Behavior Therapy (DBT) combines cognitive-behavioral techniques with mindfulness:  
- Four modules: Mindfulness, Distress Tolerance, Emotion Regulation, Interpersonal Effectiveness.  
- Skills training groups + individual therapy + phone coaching.  
- Effective in Borderline Personality Disorder and chronic suicidality.

## Risk Assessment & Management

SUICIDE & VIOLENCE  
Structured risk assessment tools include:  
- Columbia-Suicide Severity Rating Scale (C-SSRS): evaluates ideation severity, intent, plan, and behavior.  
- Historical-Clinical-Risk Management-20 (HCR-20): assesses violence risk via historical, clinical, and risk management factors.  
Management steps:  
- Immediate safety planning with means restriction.  
- Hospitalization criteria: active suicidal intent with plan, psychosis, or inability to care for self.  
- Pharmacologic adjuncts (e.g., clozapine reduces suicidality in schizophrenia).  
- Multidisciplinary coordination with social services.

## Neuromodulation Techniques

ECT, TMS, & VNS  
Electroconvulsive Therapy (ECT):  
- Indicated for treatment-resistant depression, catatonia, acute suicidality.  
- Protocol: bilateral or unilateral electrode placement; 6-12 sessions, thrice weekly.  
- Anesthesia and muscle relaxants used; seizure duration 25-60 seconds.  
Transcranial Magnetic Stimulation (TMS):  
- FDA-approved for depression; high-frequency stimulation (10 Hz) over left dorsolateral prefrontal cortex, 3000 pulses/session, 20-30 sessions.  
Vagus Nerve Stimulation (VNS):  
- Implanted device delivering intermittent electrical pulses; adjunct for refractory depression and epilepsy.

## Mechanisms

In psychiatry, the mechanisms underlying mental health disorders involve complex interactions between genetic, environmental, and neurochemical factors. The causal chain begins with genetic predisposition, where inherited traits affect the structure and function of brain regions, such as the amygdala and prefrontal cortex. Environmental factors, including stress, trauma, and social support, then influence gene expression, leading to alterations in neurotransmitter systems, including serotonin, dopamine, and norepinephrine. These neurotransmitters play crucial roles in regulating mood, motivation, and emotional response. Imbalances or dysregulation of these systems can contribute to the development of mental health disorders, such as depression, anxiety, and schizophrenia. The neurochemical changes, in turn, affect neural circuit function, leading to symptoms such as altered mood, cognition, and behavior. Understanding these mechanisms is essential for developing effective treatments, including pharmacological and psychotherapeutic interventions, which target specific components of the causal chain to restore balance and improve mental health outcomes.

## Methods And Frameworks

In psychiatry, several methods and frameworks are employed to diagnose, assess, and treat mental health disorders. The Biopsychosocial Model is a comprehensive framework that considers the interplay between biological, psychological, and social factors contributing to a patient's condition. It is used to guide assessment and treatment planning, and its failure mode lies in oversimplifying the complexity of interactions between these factors. The Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (DSM-5) provides a standardized classification system for psychiatric diagnoses, to be used when establishing a diagnosis. Its failure mode is the risk of overreliance on categorical classification, potentially neglecting the dimensional nature of mental health symptoms. The Mental Status Examination (MSE) is a structured assessment tool used to evaluate a patient's cognitive, emotional, and behavioral functioning, and is indicated when a comprehensive assessment of mental state is required. Its failure mode is incomplete or inaccurate documentation, which can lead to misdiagnosis or inappropriate treatment. The Risk-Need-Responsivity (RNR) model is used to guide treatment planning, particularly in forensic psychiatry, by identifying an individual's risk of recidivism, criminogenic needs, and responsiveness to treatment. Its failure mode lies in inadequate assessment of these factors, leading to ineffective or even counterproductive interventions. Additionally, the Cognitive-Behavioral Therapy (CBT) model is a problem-focused approach used to treat various mental health conditions, and is indicated when patients present with maladaptive thought patterns and behaviors. Its failure mode is the therapist's inadequate training or failure to establish a strong therapeutic alliance, which can limit the effectiveness of the treatment. Each of these methods and frameworks has its own failure mode, such as the DSM-5's potential for cultural bias, the MSE's reliance on patient self-report, and CBT's limited effectiveness in treating complex or severe mental health conditions. Understanding these methods, models, and formulas, as well as their limitations, is essential for effective psychiatric practice.

## Worked Examples

Calculating the dose of antidepressant medication for a patient with major depressive disorder: A 35-year-old patient with a diagnosis of major depressive disorder requires treatment with fluoxetine. The recommended starting dose is 20mg per day. If the patient has a history of seizures, the dose should be reduced by 25%. What is the adjusted dose? 
Adjusted dose = 20mg * (1 - 0.25) = 20mg * 0.75 = 15mg per day.

A score of 15 indicates a moderate to high risk of suicide, requiring close monitoring and possibly hospitalization.

A YMRS score of 25 indicates manic symptoms, and a PHQ-9 score of 18 indicates depressive symptoms, supporting a diagnosis of bipolar disorder.

## Applications

In medicine, psychiatry has numerous applications in the diagnosis, treatment, and management of mental health disorders. Psychiatrists use various techniques, including psychotherapy, pharmacotherapy, and psychosocial interventions, to treat conditions such as depression, anxiety, schizophrenia, and bipolar disorder. They also work with patients to develop coping strategies and provide education on mental health and wellness. In clinical practice, psychiatrists conduct thorough diagnostic evaluations, including physical exams, laboratory tests, and psychological assessments, to determine the underlying causes of symptoms. They then develop personalized treatment plans, which may involve medication, therapy, or a combination of both. Additionally, psychiatrists often collaborate with other healthcare professionals, such as psychologists, social workers, and primary care physicians, to provide comprehensive care to patients. In hospitals, psychiatrists may work in inpatient units, emergency departments, or consultation-liaison services, providing urgent care and stabilizing patients in crisis. In outpatient settings, they may work in private practices, community mental health centers, or specialty clinics, providing ongoing care and support to patients with chronic mental health conditions. Overall, the application of psychiatry in medicine is focused on improving patient outcomes, reducing symptoms, and enhancing quality of life for individuals with mental health disorders.

In medical practice, psychiatry is applied through various diagnostic and therapeutic techniques to manage mental health disorders. Psychiatrists use the biopsychosocial model to assess patients, considering biological factors such as genetics and neurochemistry, psychological factors like cognition and emotion, and social factors including environment and relationships. This comprehensive approach guides the development of individualized treatment plans, which may involve psychotherapy, pharmacotherapy, or a combination of both. Psychotherapy techniques, such as cognitive-behavioral therapy (CBT) and psychodynamic therapy, help patients modify maladaptive thought patterns and behaviors. Pharmacotherapy involves the use of medications like antidepressants, antipsychotics, and mood stabilizers to alleviate symptoms of conditions such as depression, schizophrenia, and bipolar disorder. Additionally, psychiatrists often work in multidisciplinary teams with other healthcare professionals, including psychologists, social workers, and nurses, to provide holistic care and support to patients and their families. Electroconvulsive therapy (ECT) and other somatic therapies may also be employed in severe or treatment-resistant cases. Overall, the application of psychiatry in medical practice aims to improve mental health outcomes, enhance quality of life, and promote recovery and rehabilitation for individuals with mental health disorders. Diagnosis involves a comprehensive clinical interview, mental status examination, and sometimes, neuropsychological testing. Psychiatrists use standardized classification systems, such as the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (DSM-5) or the International Classification of Diseases (ICD-11), to identify and categorize mental health conditions. Treatment approaches include psychotherapy (e.g., cognitive-behavioral therapy, psychodynamic therapy), pharmacotherapy (e.g., antidepressants, antipsychotics), and other interventions like electroconvulsive therapy (ECT) or transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS). They also play a crucial role in addressing mental health issues in various populations, including children, adolescents, adults, and older adults, and in managing mental health aspects of chronic medical conditions, such as diabetes, cardiovascular disease, and cancer. Additionally, psychiatrists are involved in mental health promotion, prevention, and education, aiming to reduce stigma and improve overall well-being.

## Common Errors

In psychiatric practice, common errors include misdiagnosis due to inadequate history taking, failure to consider comorbidities, and overreliance on psychotropic medications. Practitioners may misattribute physical symptoms to psychiatric conditions, such as misdiagnosing hypothyroidism as depression. Another error is the failure to monitor and adjust treatment plans, leading to inadequate management of side effects and poor patient outcomes. Overlooking cultural and social factors can also result in misdiagnosis and ineffective treatment. Furthermore, not staying updated with current guidelines and research can lead to outdated and ineffective treatment approaches. These errors can be avoided by maintaining a thorough and nuanced approach to diagnosis and treatment, considering the complex interplay of biological, psychological, and social factors that contribute to psychiatric conditions. Regular peer review, ongoing education, and a commitment to evidence-based practice can also help mitigate these errors.

Open questions in the field include the development of personalized treatment approaches, incorporating genetic and environmental factors to tailor interventions to individual patients. The integration of technology, such as artificial intelligence and machine learning, is also being explored to enhance diagnosis, treatment, and patient outcomes. Furthermore, the field is moving towards a greater understanding of the social determinants of mental health, including the impact of socioeconomic status, trauma, and social support on psychiatric disorders. Additionally, there is a growing recognition of the need for culturally sensitive and inclusive care, acknowledging the diverse experiences and perspectives of patients from diverse backgrounds. As the field continues to evolve, it is likely that advances in neuroscience, genetics, and technology will play a crucial role in shaping the future of psychiatry.

# Nursing

## Foundations

Nursing is the autonomous and collaborative care of individuals of all ages, families, groups, and communities, sick or well, and in all settings. It encompasses the promotion of health, prevention of illness, and the care of ill, disabled, and dying people. Rooted in holistic principles, nursing integrates biological, psychological, sociological, and environmental sciences to optimize patient outcomes. The discipline is grounded in the nursing process—a systematic, evidence-based approach comprising assessment, diagnosis, planning, implementation, and evaluation (ADPIE)—which operationalizes clinical judgment and critical thinking in care delivery.

Nursing, as a discipline in medicine and health, is grounded in the provision of care to individuals, families, and communities to promote health, prevent illness, and manage disease. A **nurse** is a healthcare professional who has undergone formal education and training in nursing, licensed to practice by a regulatory authority. The core of nursing practice is based on the **nursing process**, a systematic, patient-centered approach that involves assessing the patient's physical, psychological, and social needs, diagnosing actual or potential health problems, planning care, implementing interventions, and evaluating the effectiveness of care. **Health**, in this context, refers to a state of complete physical, mental, and social well-being, not merely the absence of disease or infirmity. **Patient-centered care** is an approach to care that focuses on the patient's unique needs, values, and preferences, and involves treating patients with dignity and respect. **Evidence-based practice** is the use of current best evidence in making decisions about the care of individual patients, which integrates clinical expertise and patient values with the best available research information. Understanding these foundational concepts is crucial for nursing practice, as they guide the delivery of high-quality, effective care.

## Section

NURSING PROCESS FRAMEWORK  
The nursing process (ADPIE) is a cyclical, iterative method:  
1. Assessment: Collect comprehensive data via subjective (patient interview) and objective (physical exam, labs) means. Utilize frameworks like Gordon’s Functional Health Patterns (11 categories) for holistic data gathering.  
2. Diagnosis: Formulate nursing diagnoses using NANDA-I taxonomy, distinguishing actual, risk, and health-promotion diagnoses. Example: “Impaired Gas Exchange related to alveolar-capillary membrane changes.”  
3. Planning: Develop measurable, patient-centered goals using SMART criteria (Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Relevant, Time-bound). Prioritize interventions with Maslow’s Hierarchy of Needs or urgency frameworks (ABC—Airway, Breathing, Circulation).  
4. Implementation: Execute evidence-based interventions from standardized protocols or clinical practice guidelines (e.g., CDC infection control). Document care meticulously.  
5. Evaluation: Measure outcomes against goals using validated scales (e.g., Braden Scale for pressure ulcer risk). Revise care plans based on findings.

PATIENT SAFETY AND QUALITY IMPROVEMENT  
The Swiss Cheese Model (Reason, 1990) illustrates system failures leading to errors. Nurses employ Root Cause Analysis (RCA) post-adverse events to identify latent conditions and active failures. The Plan-Do-Study-Act (PDSA) cycle operationalizes continuous quality improvement:  
- Plan: Identify a problem and devise a test of change.  
- Do: Implement the change on a small scale.  
- Study: Analyze data and outcomes.  
- Act: Adopt, adapt, or abandon the change.  
Utilize standardized tools like SBAR (Situation, Background, Assessment, Recommendation) for effective communication and handoffs to reduce errors.

PHARMACOKINETICS AND PHARMACODYNAMICS IN NURSING  
Understanding drug absorption, distribution, metabolism, and excretion (ADME) is critical. Nurses calculate dosages using formulas such as:  
Dose (mg) = (Desired Dose × Body Weight (kg)) / Concentration (mg/mL)  
Monitor therapeutic ranges and toxicity thresholds (e.g., Digoxin therapeutic range: 0.5–2 ng/mL). Apply knowledge of half-life (t½) to time dosing intervals, ensuring steady-state plasma concentrations. Use the Six Rights of Medication Administration (Right patient, drug, dose, route, time, documentation) to prevent errors.

CLINICAL ASSESSMENT AND PHYSICAL EXAMINATION  
Employ the Head-to-Toe Assessment framework, systematically evaluating:  
- Neurological (Glasgow Coma Scale, 3–15)  
- Cardiovascular (Heart rate 60–100 bpm, BP 120/80 mmHg)  
- Respiratory (Respiratory rate 12–20 breaths/min, SpO2 > 95%)  
- Gastrointestinal, Musculoskeletal, Skin, and others.  
Utilize validated tools such as the Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) for cognitive screening and the Morse Fall Scale for fall risk stratification.

ETHICS AND LEGALITY IN NURSING PRACTICE  
Apply the Four Principles of Biomedical Ethics: Autonomy, Beneficence, Nonmaleficence, and Justice. Use the ANA Code of Ethics as a guiding framework. Understand legal precedents, including informed consent, confidentiality (HIPAA compliance), and scope of practice defined by state Nurse Practice Acts. Employ ethical decision-making models such as the Four-Topic Approach (Medical Indications, Patient Preferences, Quality of Life, Contextual Features).

HEALTH PROMOTION AND DISEASE PREVENTION  
Implement the Health Belief Model (HBM) to assess patient readiness for behavior change by evaluating perceived susceptibility, severity, benefits, and barriers. Use the Transtheoretical Model (Stages of Change: Precontemplation, Contemplation, Preparation, Action, Maintenance) to tailor interventions. Apply epidemiological data (incidence, prevalence rates) to prioritize community health initiatives.

LEADERSHIP AND MANAGEMENT IN NURSING  
Utilize transformational leadership principles to inspire and motivate teams. Apply the Five Rights of Delegation (Right task, circumstance, person, direction/communication, supervision/evaluation). Employ the Lewin’s Change Theory (Unfreeze-Change-Refreeze) to manage organizational transitions. Use staffing formulas such as nurse-to-patient ratios (e.g., 1:4 on med-surg units) to optimize workload and patient safety.

## Mechanisms

Nursing mechanisms involve a complex interplay of cognitive, affective, and psychomotor processes. The nursing process, a systematic approach to care, is initiated when a patient's needs are assessed, and a nursing diagnosis is formulated. This diagnosis identifies the patient's health problems, risks, or strengths, and guides the development of a care plan. The care plan outlines specific, measurable, achievable, relevant, and time-bound (SMART) goals, and the interventions required to achieve them. Interventions may include medication administration, wound care, education, and emotional support. The nurse implements these interventions, and evaluates their effectiveness, using a continuous cycle of assessment, diagnosis, planning, implementation, and evaluation. This cyclical process enables nurses to refine their care, and make adjustments as needed, to optimize patient outcomes. The nurse-patient relationship, built on trust, empathy, and effective communication, is a critical component of nursing mechanisms, as it facilitates patient engagement, and enhances the effectiveness of care.

## Methods And Frameworks

Nursing practice utilizes various methods and frameworks to guide care delivery. The Nursing Process, a problem-solving approach, involves assessment, diagnosis, planning, implementation, and evaluation. Use it for individualized patient care. Failure mode: inadequate assessment leading to incorrect diagnosis. The ABCDE approach (Airway, Breathing, Circulation, Disability, Exposure) is used in emergency situations to prioritize care. Apply it in acute care settings, such as cardiac arrest or trauma. Failure mode: omitting a critical step. The Maslow's Hierarchy of Needs model prioritizes patient needs, with physiological needs first. Use it to guide care planning, especially for patients with multiple needs. Failure mode: neglecting basic needs. The Orem's Self-Care Deficit Theory focuses on patient self-care abilities. Apply it for patients requiring education on self-care. Failure mode: overestimating patient abilities. The Braden Scale and Norton Scale are used to assess pressure ulcer risk. Use them for patients at risk of pressure ulcers. Failure mode: inaccurate assessment leading to inadequate prevention.

## Worked Examples

Calculating medication dosages is a critical skill in nursing. For example, if a patient requires 250mg of amoxicillin orally every 8 hours, and the medication is available in 500mg capsules, the nurse must calculate the correct dose. To do this, the nurse would divide the required dose (250mg) by the available dose (500mg), resulting in 0.5 capsules per dose.

Another example involves calculating intravenous (IV) fluid rates. If a patient requires 2000ml of IV fluid over 8 hours, the nurse would calculate the hourly rate by dividing the total volume (2000ml) by the total time (8 hours), resulting in 250ml/hour.

In assessing patient nutritional status, a nurse might calculate a patient's body mass index (BMI). For a patient who is 170cm tall and weighs 60kg, the nurse would first convert the height to meters (1.7m), then calculate the BMI by dividing the weight (60kg) by the square of the height (1.7m^2 = 2.89m^2), resulting in a BMI of 20.8.

Calculating Medication Dosage: A patient weighing 70 kg requires a dose of 10 mg/kg of a medication. To calculate the total dose, multiply the patient's weight by the dose per kilogram: 70 kg * 10 mg/kg = 700 mg.
Assessing Fluid Balance: A patient has a fluid intake of 2000 mL and a fluid output of 1500 mL over 8 hours. To calculate the fluid balance, subtract the output from the intake: 2000 mL - 1500 mL = 500 mL. This indicates a positive fluid balance of 500 mL.
Determining Oxygen Therapy: A patient requires an oxygen flow rate of 2 L/min. If the oxygen tank contains 680 L and the flow rate is constant, to calculate the time the tank will last, divide the total volume by the flow rate: 680 L / 2 L/min = 340 min. This indicates the tank will last approximately 5.67 hours.

## Common Errors

In nursing practice, common errors include medication administration mistakes, such as incorrect dosing, wrong medication, or wrong route of administration. These errors can occur due to distractions, lack of attention to detail, or inadequate knowledge of pharmacology. Another error is inadequate patient assessment, leading to missed diagnoses or delayed interventions. Insufficient documentation and poor communication among healthcare team members can also lead to errors, such as misunderstandings about patient care plans or allergies. Additionally, failure to follow infection control protocols can result in hospital-acquired infections. These errors are wrong because they can cause harm to patients, compromise patient safety, and lead to adverse outcomes. Furthermore, errors can also lead to increased healthcare costs, prolonged hospital stays, and decreased patient satisfaction. Nurses must prioritize patient safety, stay vigilant, and adhere to evidence-based practices to minimize the risk of errors. By recognizing the potential for errors and taking proactive steps to prevent them, nurses can provide high-quality care and promote optimal patient outcomes. Another common error is inaccurate documentation, which can lead to misinformation and inappropriate care. Insufficient hand hygiene and improper use of personal protective equipment (PPE) are also prevalent, increasing the risk of healthcare-associated infections. Additionally, failure to properly monitor patients, particularly those with high-risk conditions, can result in delayed detection of complications. These errors are wrong because they can lead to patient harm, prolonged hospital stays, and increased healthcare costs. Furthermore, they often result from deviations from established nursing standards and protocols, highlighting the importance of adherence to evidence-based practices and ongoing education to prevent such mistakes.

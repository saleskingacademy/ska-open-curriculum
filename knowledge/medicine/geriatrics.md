# Geriatrics

> The course assumes prior knowledge of medicine and health sciences, and delves into specialized topics like comprehensive geriatric assessment and polypharmacy management.

## Foundations

Geriatrics is the branch of medicine focused on the health care of older adults, typically those aged 65 and above, emphasizing the prevention, diagnosis, treatment, and management of age-related diseases and syndromes. Rooted in the principles of comprehensive, multidisciplinary care, geriatrics addresses the complex interplay of multimorbidity, polypharmacy, functional decline, cognitive impairment, and psychosocial factors. The core tenet is the preservation of functional independence and quality of life rather than disease-centric paradigms alone. Aging is a heterogeneous process influenced by biological, psychological, and social determinants, necessitating individualized and holistic approaches.

Key terms in geriatrics include **aging**, the process of becoming older, characterized by a decline in physical and physiological functions; **senescence**, the biological process of aging, influenced by genetic, environmental, and lifestyle factors; **functional status**, a person's ability to perform daily activities, such as bathing, dressing, and managing finances; and **geriatric syndromes**, conditions common in older adults, including falls, frailty, and dementia. Understanding these concepts is essential for providing high-quality care to older adults. A **geriatric assessment** is a comprehensive evaluation of an older adult's health, including medical, functional, and social components. This assessment informs the development of a personalized care plan, addressing the unique needs and goals of each patient.

## Comprehensive Geriatric Assessment (Cga)

Framework: Multidimensional, interdisciplinary diagnostic process to evaluate medical, psychological, functional, and social capabilities.  
Steps:  
1. Medical assessment — chronic conditions, medications (focus on Beers Criteria for potentially inappropriate meds), sensory deficits.  
2. Mental health — cognitive screening (Mini-Mental State Examination [MMSE], Montreal Cognitive Assessment [MoCA]), depression scales (Geriatric Depression Scale [GDS]).  
3. Functional status — Activities of Daily Living (ADLs; Katz Index), Instrumental Activities of Daily Living (IADLs; Lawton Scale).  
4. Social evaluation — support networks, living environment, financial resources.  
5. Formulation of a coordinated care plan involving multidisciplinary team (physicians, nurses, social workers, therapists).  
Outcome: Identifies reversible conditions, optimizes medication regimens, and tailors interventions to individual goals.

## Polypharmacy Management

Framework: Systematic medication review using explicit criteria to minimize adverse drug events (ADEs).  
Key tools:  
- Beers Criteria (American Geriatrics Society, updated 2023) — lists drugs to avoid or use with caution in elderly.  
- STOPP/START criteria — Screening Tool of Older Persons’ Prescriptions and Screening Tool to Alert to Right Treatment.  
Steps:  
1. Compile complete medication list including OTC and supplements.  
2. Assess each drug for indication, effectiveness, dosage, interactions, and duplication.  
3. Identify potentially inappropriate medications (PIMs) per Beers/STOPP criteria.  
4. Deprescribe systematically, prioritizing drugs with highest risk-benefit imbalance.  
5. Monitor for withdrawal effects and therapeutic outcomes.  
Goal: Reduce ADEs, hospitalizations, and improve adherence.

## Frailty Assessment And Management

Framework: Frailty as a clinical syndrome of decreased reserve and resistance to stressors, increasing vulnerability.  
Models:  
- Fried Frailty Phenotype (2001): 5 criteria — unintentional weight loss (>4.5 kg/10 lbs in past year), self-reported exhaustion, weakness (grip strength <20th percentile adjusted for sex/BMI), slow walking speed (<0.8 m/s over 4 meters), low physical activity. Frailty defined by ≥3 criteria; pre-frail 1-2.  
- Rockwood Clinical Frailty Scale (CFS): 9-point scale from very fit (1) to terminally ill (9).  
Management: Tailored exercise programs (resistance and balance training), nutritional interventions (protein intake 1.2-1.5 g/kg/day), and minimization of iatrogenic insults.

## Dementia Diagnosis And Staging

Framework: Differentiation of dementia subtypes and staging severity to guide management.  
Diagnostic criteria: DSM-5 and NIA-AA guidelines.  
Common types: Alzheimer’s disease (AD), vascular dementia, Lewy body dementia, frontotemporal dementia.  
Tools:  
- Cognitive testing: MMSE (cutoff <24/30 suggests impairment), MoCA (cutoff <26/30), Clock Drawing Test.  
- Functional staging: Clinical Dementia Rating (CDR) scale (0–3).  
- Neuroimaging: MRI for vascular lesions, hippocampal atrophy.  
Management: Cholinesterase inhibitors (donepezil, rivastigmine) and memantine; non-pharmacologic interventions (cognitive stimulation therapy).

## Delirium Recognition And Prevention

Framework: Acute fluctuating disturbance in attention and cognition, often multifactorial in elderly hospitalized patients.  
Assessment: Confusion Assessment Method (CAM) — requires presence of acute onset/fluctuating course, inattention, and either disorganized thinking or altered consciousness.  
Prevention strategies:  
- Identify and mitigate precipitating factors (infection, hypoxia, electrolyte imbalance).  
- Maintain orientation aids (clocks, calendars).  
- Promote sleep hygiene, early mobilization, hydration, and sensory aids (glasses, hearing aids).  
Treatment: Address underlying causes, minimize psychoactive drugs, and supportive care.

## Functional Rehabilitation And Falls Prevention

Framework: Functional decline and falls are leading causes of morbidity in geriatrics; prevention requires multifactorial intervention.  
Assessment: Timed Up and Go (TUG) test (>12 seconds indicates fall risk), Berg Balance Scale, gait speed (<0.8 m/s predictive of poor outcomes).  
Interventions:  
- Strength and balance training (Otago Exercise Program).  
- Home hazard assessment and modification.  
- Vision correction and medication review.  
- Vitamin D supplementation (800–1000 IU/day) shown to reduce fall risk.  
Outcome: Reduced fall incidence, improved mobility, and independence.

## Advanced Care Planning And Ethics

Framework: Proactive discussions regarding goals of care, advance directives, and end-of-life preferences.  
Process:  
1. Assess decisional capacity using tools like the Aid to Capacity Evaluation (ACE).  
2. Discuss prognosis, treatment options, and patient values.  
3. Document advance directives, POLST (Physician Orders for Life-Sustaining Treatment), or DNR orders.  
4. Involve surrogate decision-makers and ethics consultations as needed.  
Ethical principles: Autonomy, beneficence, non-maleficence, and justice guide decision-making in complex geriatric scenarios.

## Mastery Levels

L1: Identify age ≥65 as the starting point for geriatric considerations.  
L2: Perform basic cognitive screening with MMSE or MoCA.  
L3: Conduct a medication review using Beers Criteria to flag PIMs.  
L4: Apply Fried Frailty Phenotype to classify frailty status.  
L5: Interpret CAM to diagnose delirium in hospitalized elders.  
L6: Develop and implement a multidisciplinary care plan based on CGA findings.  
L7: Lead deprescribing initiatives balancing polypharmacy risks and benefits.  
L8: Integrate ethical, functional, cognitive, and social domains to personalize advanced care planning and optimize quality of life in complex geriatric patients.

## Mechanisms

Geriatrics, as a medical specialty, involves understanding the complex interplay of physiological, pathological, and pharmacological changes that occur in older adults. The mechanisms underlying geriatric care can be broken down into several key steps. Firstly, aging is associated with a decline in physiological reserve, making older adults more susceptible to disease and disability. This decline is characterized by changes in multiple organ systems, including the cardiovascular, renal, and nervous systems. As a result, older adults may experience a range of age-related changes, such as decreased muscle mass, bone density, and immune function. When an older adult presents with a medical condition, the geriatrician must consider the causal chain of events, including the underlying disease process, comorbidities, and polypharmacy. The geriatrician must then evaluate the patient's functional status, cognitive function, and social support network to develop an individualized care plan. This plan may involve pharmacological interventions, non-pharmacological therapies, and lifestyle modifications, all of which are tailored to the patient's unique needs and goals. Ultimately, the goal of geriatric care is to optimize functional ability, manage chronic conditions, and improve quality of life for older adults. By understanding the mechanisms underlying aging and disease, geriatricians can provide high-quality, patient-centered care that addresses the complex needs of this population.

## Methods And Frameworks

In geriatrics, several methods and frameworks are employed to assess and manage the health of older adults. The Comprehensive Geriatric Assessment (CGA) is a multidisciplinary approach used to evaluate an older adult's medical, functional, and social needs. It is typically used for frail or complex patients, and its failure mode is incomplete assessment due to lack of interdisciplinary collaboration. The Timed Up and Go (TUG) test is a simple method used to assess mobility and fall risk, with a failure mode of inaccurate results if not administered correctly. The Mini-Cog is a brief cognitive screening tool, used to detect dementia, with a failure mode of false negatives if the patient has high levels of education or cognitive reserve. The Katz Index of Independence in Activities of Daily Living (ADLs) is used to assess functional status, with a failure mode of underestimating disability if not administered by a trained healthcare professional. The Geriatric Depression Scale (GDS) is used to screen for depression, with a failure mode of false negatives if the patient has cognitive impairment or if the scale is not culturally sensitive. These methods and frameworks are essential in geriatrics to provide comprehensive and patient-centered care.

## Worked Examples

Calculating the risk of falls in elderly patients is crucial in geriatrics. For example, consider an 80-year-old female patient with a history of osteoporosis and arthritis. Her Timed Up and Go (TUG) test score is 15 seconds, indicating a high risk of falls. Using the Morse Fall Scale, which assesses factors such as history of falling, secondary diagnoses, and use of ambulatory aids, her score is 55, categorizing her as a high-risk patient.

Another example involves calculating the creatinine clearance in an elderly patient to assess renal function. For a 75-year-old male patient weighing 70 kg and with a serum creatinine level of 1.2 mg/dL, the creatinine clearance can be estimated using the Cockcroft-Gault equation: CrCl (mL/min) = (140 - age) * weight (kg) / (72 * serum creatinine), which yields approximately 50 mL/min, indicating impaired renal function.

In managing polypharmacy in geriatric patients, consider a 90-year-old patient taking 10 medications, including warfarin, aspirin, and atorvastatin. Using the Beers Criteria, which lists potentially inappropriate medications for elderly patients, warfarin and aspirin are identified as high-risk medications due to the patient's age and increased risk of bleeding. The patient's medication regimen should be carefully reviewed and optimized to minimize adverse effects.

Plugging in the numbers, CrCl = (140 - 75) * 70 / (72 * 1.2) = 65 * 70 / 86.4 = 52.5 mL/min, indicating impaired renal function.

In medication management, calculating the appropriate dose of medications in elderly patients is critical due to age-related changes in pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics. For instance, when prescribing metformin for an elderly patient with diabetes, the dose should be adjusted based on renal function. If the patient's estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) is 45 mL/min/1.73m^2, the metformin dose should be reduced to minimize the risk of lactic acidosis. The maximum recommended dose of metformin in patients with an eGFR between 30-59 mL/min/1.73m^2 is 1000 mg/day.

## Applications

In medical practice, geriatrics is applied through comprehensive geriatric assessments, which evaluate an older adult's medical, functional, and social capabilities to develop individualized care plans. This approach is used in various settings, including hospitals, clinics, nursing homes, and home care. Geriatricians use their knowledge of age-related physiological changes, such as decreased renal function and altered drug metabolism, to optimize medication management and prevent polypharmacy. They also apply principles of geriatrics to manage common geriatric syndromes like frailty, falls, and delirium. Additionally, geriatricians work with interdisciplinary teams, including nurses, physical therapists, and social workers, to address the complex needs of older adults and promote healthy aging. This collaborative approach helps to improve outcomes, reduce hospital readmissions, and enhance the quality of life for older patients. By considering the unique needs and circumstances of older adults, geriatricians can provide personalized care that addresses the physical, emotional, and social aspects of health.

## Common Errors

In geriatric care, common mistakes include overprescription and polypharmacy, which can lead to adverse drug interactions and exacerbate age-related declines in renal function and drug metabolism. Another error is underestimating the impact of comorbidities and functional limitations on treatment outcomes, resulting in unrealistic expectations and inadequate care planning. Failure to consider age-related changes in physiology, such as decreased muscle mass and altered body composition, can lead to incorrect medication dosing and monitoring. Additionally, neglecting to assess for geriatric syndromes like frailty, dementia, and delirium can result in missed opportunities for targeted interventions and support. Overreliance on disease-focused care, rather than patient-centered care, can also lead to neglect of important geriatric considerations, such as maintaining functional independence and addressing social determinants of health. These errors often stem from inadequate training in geriatric principles, lack of time for comprehensive assessments, and insufficient consideration of the unique needs and priorities of older adults.

## Advanced

The field of geriatrics is continually evolving, with ongoing research aimed at improving the health and quality of life of older adults. One key area of focus is the development of personalized medicine approaches, tailored to the unique needs and health profiles of individual older patients. This involves integrating genetic, epigenetic, and environmental factors to inform treatment decisions and optimize health outcomes. Another area of advancement is the study of frailty, a condition characterized by decreased physiological reserve and increased vulnerability to stressors. Researchers are working to develop more effective interventions to prevent or reverse frailty, such as exercise programs, nutritional supplements, and pharmacological therapies. Additionally, there is a growing recognition of the importance of social determinants of health, such as social isolation, housing, and access to healthcare, in shaping the health and wellbeing of older adults. Open questions in the field include the optimal management of multimorbidity, the role of technology in supporting aging in place, and the development of effective strategies to address the healthcare workforce shortage in geriatrics. As the field continues to move forward, it is likely that we will see increased emphasis on interdisciplinary collaboration, innovative models of care delivery, and a greater focus on promoting healthy aging and preventing age-related diseases.

# Single Cell Genomics

> The course assumes prior knowledge of biology and genomics, and focuses on applying principles to real situations and methods.

## Foundations

Single cell genomics is the study of the genome, which is the complete set of DNA (deoxyribonucleic acid, a molecule that contains genetic instructions) in a single cell. A cell is the basic structural and functional unit of living organisms, and genomics is the field of biology that focuses on the structure, function, and evolution of genomes. The genome consists of genes, which are units of heredity that carry information from one generation to the next, and non-coding regions, which are sequences of DNA that do not code for proteins.

Key terms include: transcriptome, the set of all RNA (ribonucleic acid, a molecule that plays a crucial role in protein synthesis) molecules in a cell; epigenome, the set of chemical modifications to DNA and histone proteins that regulate gene expression; and genome variation, which refers to differences in the DNA sequence between individuals or cells.

Understanding these concepts is crucial for analyzing and interpreting single cell genomic data, which can be obtained through various techniques, including single cell DNA sequencing, which involves determining the order of the four chemical building blocks (adenine, guanine, cytosine, and thymine) that make up an organism's DNA, and single cell RNA sequencing, which involves analyzing the transcriptome of a single cell.

## Mechanisms

Single cell genomics involves the analysis of the genome of individual cells, allowing for the understanding of cellular heterogeneity and the identification of rare cell populations. The process begins with cell isolation, where individual cells are separated from a mixture of cells using techniques such as fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS) or microfluidics. Once isolated, the cell's genome is amplified using whole-genome amplification (WGA) methods, such as multiple displacement amplification (MDA) or degenerate oligonucleotide primed PCR (DOP-PCR), to generate sufficient DNA for downstream analysis. The amplified DNA is then sequenced using next-generation sequencing (NGS) technologies, such as Illumina or PacBio, which generate millions of short DNA sequences. These sequences are then aligned to a reference genome and analyzed using bioinformatic tools to identify genetic variants, such as single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) and copy number variations (CNVs). The resulting data provide a comprehensive understanding of the cell's genetic landscape, enabling the identification of cellular subpopulations and the elucidation of cellular heterogeneity. The causal chain of single cell genomics is therefore: cell isolation → genome amplification → DNA sequencing → data analysis → identification of genetic variants and cellular subpopulations. The causal chain of single cell genomics is therefore: cell isolation → genome amplification → DNA sequencing → data analysis → biological interpretation.

## Methods And Frameworks

In single cell genomics, several methods and frameworks are employed to analyze and interpret the genomic data of individual cells. 
1. **Single Cell RNA Sequencing (scRNA-seq)**: This method is used to study the transcriptome of single cells, providing insights into cellular heterogeneity and gene expression profiles. It is particularly useful for identifying cell types and understanding cellular differentiation. Failure mode: scRNA-seq can be limited by the quality of the input RNA, and biases in library preparation can affect the accuracy of the results.
2. **Single Cell DNA Sequencing (scDNA-seq)**: This approach is used to study the genome of single cells, enabling the detection of copy number variations and genomic mutations. It is useful for understanding clonal evolution and tumor heterogeneity. Failure mode: scDNA-seq can be affected by DNA degradation and amplification biases, leading to inaccurate results.
3. **Single Cell ATAC Sequencing (scATAC-seq)**: This method is used to study chromatin accessibility in single cells, providing insights into gene regulation and cellular identity. It is useful for identifying regulatory elements and understanding cellular differentiation. Failure mode: scATAC-seq can be limited by the quality of the input material and biases in library preparation, affecting the accuracy of the results.
4. **Cell Ranger**: This is a software framework used for analyzing scRNA-seq data, providing a pipeline for data processing, visualization, and downstream analysis. It is useful for identifying cell types and understanding cellular heterogeneity. Failure mode: Cell Ranger can be sensitive to the quality of the input data and may not perform well with low-quality or noisy data.
5. **Seurat**: This is an R package used for analyzing scRNA-seq data, providing a framework for data integration, visualization, and downstream analysis. It is useful for identifying cell types and understanding cellular heterogeneity. Failure mode: Seurat can be computationally intensive and may require significant computational resources for large datasets.

Single cell genomics employs various methods to analyze individual cells, including single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq), which measures gene expression levels. The Smart-seq2 protocol is used for full-length mRNA sequencing, while the 10X Genomics Chromium platform is utilized for high-throughput scRNA-seq. To identify cell types, the Seurat framework is applied, which uses a graph-based approach to cluster cells. The t-distributed Stochastic Neighbor Embedding (t-SNE) algorithm is used for dimensionality reduction and visualization of high-dimensional data. The Monocle method is employed for pseudotime analysis, which reconstructs cellular trajectories. Failure modes include batch effects, which can be mitigated using the ComBat algorithm, and dropouts, where genes are not detected due to low sequencing depth. The choice of method depends on the research question, with scRNA-seq suitable for studying cell heterogeneity and pseudotime analysis for understanding cellular differentiation.

## Worked Examples

To illustrate the application of single cell genomics, consider the following examples. 
1. **Cellular Heterogeneity Analysis**: Suppose we have a population of 100 cells from a tumor sample, and we want to identify the proportion of cancer stem cells. Using single cell RNA sequencing, we find that 20 cells express the stem cell marker CD133, with an average of 500 transcripts per cell. If the total number of transcripts for CD133 across all 100 cells is 15,000, what is the average expression level of CD133 in the non-stem cells? 
Let's denote the average expression level in non-stem cells as x. Since 80 cells are non-stem cells, the total number of CD133 transcripts in non-stem cells is 80x. We know that 20 cells have an average of 500 transcripts, contributing 20 * 500 = 10,000 transcripts. The remaining 5,000 transcripts (15,000 - 10,000) are distributed among the 80 non-stem cells, so 80x = 5,000, giving x = 5,000 / 80 = 62.5 transcripts per non-stem cell.
2. **Genomic Variation Detection**: A single cell genome sequencing experiment reveals a mutation frequency of 1.2 * 10^-5 per base pair in a sample of 50 cells. If the human genome has approximately 3.2 billion base pairs, how many mutations would we expect to find in a single cell? 
Using the mutation frequency, we can calculate the expected number of mutations per cell as 3,200,000,000 * 1.2 * 10^-5 = 38.4. Since we cannot have a fraction of a mutation, we round to the nearest whole number, expecting approximately 38 mutations per cell.
3. **Cell Lineage Inference**: In a single cell DNA sequencing study, we identify 5 distinct clones in a population of 200 cells, with each clone characterized by a unique set of single nucleotide variations (SNVs). If clone A has 10 cells, clone B has 15 cells, clone C has 20 cells, clone D has 30 cells, and clone E has 125 cells, what proportion of cells belong to the most abundant clone? 
The most abundant clone is clone E, with 125 cells. To find the proportion, divide the number of cells in clone E by the total number of cells: 125 / 200 = 0.625. Thus, 62.5% of the cells belong to clone E.

If the gene of interest is 10 kb long, and we observe 500 reads mapping to this gene, we can estimate the CNV as follows: first, calculate the average coverage depth (500 reads * 100 bp / 10,000 bp = 5x). 2. A researcher wants to analyze the transcriptome of a single neuron. She uses smart-seq2, a protocol for full-length mRNA sequencing, to generate a library of 200,000 reads. If 80% of these reads map to known genes, and the remaining 20% are unmapped, what percentage of the transcriptome is represented by unknown transcripts? The answer is 20%, indicating that a significant portion of the neuron's transcriptome consists of novel or unannotated genes. 
3. In a single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) experiment, we want to determine the cell type of a single cell based on its gene expression profile. Suppose we have a reference dataset of 100 cells from 5 different cell types (20 cells per type), and we use a clustering algorithm (e.g., k-means) to group the cells based on their expression profiles. If our single cell clusters with 18 out of 20 cells from a specific cell type (e.g., T-cells), we can infer that our single cell is likely a T-cell, with a probability proportional to the clustering similarity (e.g., 90%).

## Common Errors

In single cell genomics, common errors include inadequate cell isolation, resulting in contamination with neighboring cells or debris, which can lead to incorrect conclusions about gene expression profiles. Another mistake is insufficient consideration of batch effects, where variations in experimental conditions, such as sequencing depth or library preparation, can introduce systematic biases. Failure to account for dropouts, where genes are not detected due to low expression or technical limitations, can also lead to misinterpretation of data. Additionally, incorrect normalization methods can distort the true biological signal, and neglecting to validate findings using orthogonal methods, such as qRT-PCR or immunofluorescence, can result in false positives or false negatives. These errors can be mitigated by careful experimental design, rigorous quality control, and thorough data analysis, highlighting the importance of attention to detail and methodological rigor in single cell genomics research.

In single cell genomics, several common errors can lead to misleading results and incorrect conclusions. One of the primary mistakes is inadequate consideration of cell viability and quality control. This can result in the inclusion of dead or dying cells in the analysis, which can skew the data and lead to incorrect interpretations. Another error is the failure to account for batch effects and technical variability, which can arise from differences in sample preparation, sequencing protocols, and instrumentation. This can lead to the misidentification of cell subpopulations and the incorrect assignment of cellular phenotypes. Additionally, the incorrect application of bioinformatic tools and algorithms can also lead to errors, such as the misclassification of cell types or the failure to detect rare cell populations. Furthermore, the lack of consideration of the single cell genomics data's inherent noise and variability can lead to over-interpretation of the results. It is essential to be aware of these potential pitfalls and take steps to mitigate them, such as implementing robust quality control measures, using appropriate statistical methods, and carefully validating the results. By understanding these common errors, researchers can design more robust experiments and accurately interpret their results, ultimately advancing our understanding of single cell biology.

# Emergency Medicine

> The course assumes prior knowledge of medicine and health sciences, and teaches specialized concepts and procedures in emergency medicine.

## Foundations

Emergency Medicine (EM) is the medical specialty focused on the immediate decision-making and action necessary to prevent death or any further disability both in pre-hospital and hospital settings. It integrates acute care of undifferentiated patients of all ages and organ systems. Core principles include rapid assessment, stabilization, differential diagnosis under uncertainty, and time-critical interventions. EM operates at the nexus of pathophysiology, resuscitation science, and systems-based practice, emphasizing the “golden hour” and “platinum ten minutes” for trauma and critical illness. The specialty requires mastery of cognitive heuristics, procedural skills, and resource allocation under pressure.

In the field of Emergency Medicine, core definitions and first principles are crucial for effective practice. Emergency Medicine is a medical specialty that focuses on the diagnosis and treatment of acute illnesses and injuries that require immediate attention. A key concept is **triage**, a process of determining the priority of patients' treatments based on the severity of their condition. This is guided by the principle of **urgency**, which refers to the need for immediate medical attention to prevent serious harm or death. 
The **emergency department (ED)** is the primary setting for Emergency Medicine, where patients receive initial evaluation and treatment. EDs are staffed by **emergency medicine physicians**, who are trained to manage a wide range of medical conditions, and **emergency nurses**, who provide critical care and support. 
Understanding **vital signs**, including pulse, blood pressure, respiratory rate, and oxygen saturation, is essential for assessing a patient's condition. The **ABCs (Airway, Breathing, Circulation)** are fundamental principles for evaluating and managing critically ill patients. 
Familiarity with **emergency medical terminology**, such as **hypotension** (low blood pressure), **tachycardia** (rapid heart rate), and **hypoxia** (insufficient oxygen), is necessary for effective communication among healthcare providers. 
A solid foundation in Emergency Medicine requires knowledge of these core concepts and principles, enabling practitioners to provide timely and effective care in high-pressure situations.

## Airway Management

Framework: The Difficult Airway Algorithm (American Society of Anesthesiologists, ASA 2022)  
- Step 1: Assess airway difficulty (Mallampati score III/IV, thyromental distance <6 cm, limited neck mobility).  
- Step 2: Prepare equipment: standard laryngoscope, video laryngoscope (e.g., GlideScope), supraglottic airway devices (LMA Supreme), bougie, surgical airway kit.  
- Step 3: Preoxygenate with 100% O2 for ≥3 minutes or 8 vital capacity breaths.  
- Step 4: Rapid Sequence Intubation (RSI) protocol: induction agent (etomidate 0.3 mg/kg or ketamine 1–2 mg/kg), paralytic (succinylcholine 1.5 mg/kg or rocuronium 1.2 mg/kg).  
- Step 5: Confirm tube placement via waveform capnography (EtCO2 > 35 mmHg), bilateral breath sounds, and chest rise.  
- Step 6: Failed intubation pathway: attempt second intubation with alternate device, then insert supraglottic airway, and if ventilation fails, proceed to emergency cricothyrotomy (scalpel-bougie-tube technique).  
Specifics: Cricothyrotomy landmark is cricothyroid membrane, typically 1–2 cm below thyroid notch.

## Circulatory Resuscitation

Framework: Advanced Trauma Life Support (ATLS) Circulation Algorithm  
- Step 1: Control hemorrhage with direct pressure, tourniquets (windlass type recommended for limb exsanguination).  
- Step 2: Establish two large-bore IV lines (14–16G) or intraosseous access (proximal tibia or humerus).  
- Step 3: Initial fluid resuscitation: balanced crystalloids (Lactated Ringer’s or Plasma-Lyte) at 20 mL/kg bolus; avoid excessive normal saline to prevent hyperchloremic acidosis.  
- Step 4: Massive transfusion protocol (MTP): 1:1:1 ratio of packed RBCs, fresh frozen plasma, and platelets; activate if >10 units RBCs anticipated in 24 hours or >4 units in 1 hour.  
- Step 5: Vasopressors: norepinephrine first-line for septic or cardiogenic shock; titrate to MAP ≥65 mmHg.  
- Step 6: Identify and treat reversible causes of shock (the “4 H’s and 4 T’s”: Hypovolemia, Hypoxia, Hydrogen ion (acidosis), Hypo/Hyperkalemia; Tension pneumothorax, Tamponade, Toxins, Thrombosis).

## Neurologic Emergencies

Framework: The NIH Stroke Scale (NIHSS) for rapid stroke assessment  
- Step 1: Evaluate level of consciousness (0-3 points), gaze palsy (0-2), visual fields (0-3), facial palsy (0-3), motor arm and leg (0-4 each), limb ataxia (0-2), sensory (0-2), language (0-3), dysarthria (0-2), extinction/inattention (0-2).  
- Step 2: Total score 0–42; >4 indicates moderate to severe stroke.  
- Step 3: Time last known well critical; initiate CT head without contrast within 25 minutes of arrival.  
- Step 4: Administer IV alteplase (tPA) 0.9 mg/kg (max 90 mg) within 4.5 hours of symptom onset, excluding contraindications (e.g., recent surgery, INR >1.7).  
- Step 5: For large vessel occlusion, consider mechanical thrombectomy within 6–24 hours using imaging criteria (CT perfusion mismatch).  
- Step 6: Manage blood pressure carefully: keep systolic BP <185 mmHg pre-thrombolysis, permissive hypertension allowed otherwise.

## Toxicology

Framework: The “ABCDE” approach with antidote protocols  
- Step 1: Airway protection, breathing support, circulation stabilization.  
- Step 2: Decontamination: activated charcoal within 1 hour of ingestion (1 g/kg up to 50 g). Avoid if compromised airway or caustic ingestion.  
- Step 3: Antidotes:  
   - Acetaminophen toxicity: N-acetylcysteine (NAC) IV protocol—loading dose 150 mg/kg over 1 hour, then 50 mg/kg over 4 hours, then 100 mg/kg over 16 hours.  
   - Opioid overdose: Naloxone 0.4 mg IV, repeat every 2–3 minutes up to 10 mg.  
   - Beta-blocker overdose: glucagon 5–10 mg IV bolus, then infusion 1–5 mg/hr.  
- Step 4: Enhanced elimination: consider hemodialysis for toxic alcohols (ethylene glycol, methanol) or lithium.  
- Step 5: Monitor labs: anion gap, osmolal gap, arterial blood gases, and specific drug levels.

## Trauma Management

Framework: Primary and Secondary Survey (ATLS)  
- Primary Survey (ABCDE):  
   A – Airway with cervical spine protection  
   B – Breathing and ventilation (look for tension pneumothorax, flail chest)  
   C – Circulation with hemorrhage control  
   D – Disability (Glasgow Coma Scale, pupil size/reactivity)  
   E – Exposure/environmental control (prevent hypothermia)  
- Secondary Survey: head-to-toe examination, adjunct imaging (FAST ultrasound for free fluid), and detailed history (AMPLE: Allergies, Medications, Past illness, Last meal, Events).  
- Specifics: FAST exam positive for free fluid mandates surgical consultation; pelvic binder applied for suspected pelvic fracture to reduce hemorrhage.

## Pediatric Emergencies

Framework: Broselow Tape and Pediatric Advanced Life Support (PALS) algorithms  
- Step 1: Use Broselow Tape for rapid weight estimation and dosing (e.g., epinephrine 0.01 mg/kg IV/IO).  
- Step 2: Airway: smaller diameter endotracheal tubes (cuffed size = (age/4) + 3.5 mm).  
- Step 3: Circulation: fluid bolus 20 mL/kg isotonic crystalloid over 5–10 minutes for shock.  
- Step 4: Respiratory distress management: nebulized racemic epinephrine 0.5 mL/kg (max 5 mL) for croup.  
- Step 5: Seizure management: benzodiazepines first-line (lorazepam 0.1 mg/kg IV).  
- Step 6: Recognize subtle signs of deterioration; pediatric patients compensate well until sudden decompensation.

## Mastery Levels

L1 Beginner: Recognizes life-threatening emergencies and initiates basic ABCs.  
L2 Novice: Performs standard protocols like RSI and ACLS algorithms under supervision.  
L3 Competent: Integrates clinical findings with diagnostic tools to formulate differential diagnoses rapidly.  
L4 Proficient: Manages multi-system trauma and shock with advanced resuscitation techniques independently.  
L5 Expert: Executes complex procedures (e.g., emergency thoracotomy) with precision and leads resuscitations.  
L6 Scholar: Critically appraises emerging evidence to optimize protocols and teaches peers.  
L7 Innovator: Develops new diagnostic or therapeutic approaches improving emergency care outcomes.  
L8 Grandmaster: Shapes global emergency medicine policy, pioneers research, and mentors the next generation of leaders.

## Mechanisms

In emergency medicine, the mechanisms of patient care involve a complex interplay of assessment, diagnosis, and treatment. The process begins with patient presentation, where the emergency medical services (EMS) team or emergency department (ED) staff conduct an initial assessment, typically using the ABCDE (Airway, Breathing, Circulation, Disability, Exposure) approach. This assessment identifies life-threatening conditions, such as airway obstruction or cardiac arrest, which require immediate intervention. The EMS team or ED staff then obtain a brief medical history, including the patient's chief complaint, medical history, and current medications. 
The next step involves a physical examination, where the healthcare provider evaluates the patient's vital signs, performs a head-to-toe examination, and assesses for signs of injury or illness. This information is used to develop a differential diagnosis, which is a list of potential causes for the patient's symptoms. The healthcare provider then orders diagnostic tests, such as laboratory studies or imaging, to confirm or rule out suspected conditions. 
The results of these tests, combined with the patient's history and physical examination findings, are used to develop a treatment plan. This plan may involve pharmacological interventions, such as administering medications to manage pain or stabilize vital signs, or procedural interventions, such as performing endotracheal intubation or thoracostomy. Throughout the process, the healthcare provider continuously monitors the patient's condition, reassessing and adjusting the treatment plan as needed to ensure optimal outcomes.

## Methods And Frameworks

In emergency medicine, several methods and frameworks guide decision-making and patient care. The Airway, Breathing, Circulation (ABC) approach is used to prioritize assessment and management of critically ill patients, focusing on securing the airway, ensuring adequate breathing, and maintaining circulation. The Advanced Trauma Life Support (ATLS) framework provides a structured approach to trauma care, emphasizing rapid assessment, resuscitation, and stabilization. The Pediatric Assessment Triangle (PAT) is used to quickly assess pediatric patients, evaluating appearance, work of breathing, and circulation. The Shock Index (SI) formula, calculated as heart rate divided by systolic blood pressure, helps identify patients at risk of shock. The Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) is used to assess level of consciousness, with scores ranging from 3 to 15. Failure modes include incorrect application of these frameworks, inadequate patient assessment, and delayed recognition of critical conditions. The MIMMS (Major Incident Medical Management and Support) framework guides response to mass casualty incidents, emphasizing scene assessment, triage, and resource allocation. Effective use of these methods and frameworks requires ongoing education, training, and practice to ensure proficient application in high-pressure emergency situations.

## Worked Examples

Calculating the dose of epinephrine for an adult patient in cardiac arrest: A 70kg adult patient is in cardiac arrest, requiring epinephrine administration. The recommended dose is 1mg, which can be calculated based on body weight (0.0143 mg/kg). For this patient, the dose would be 70kg * 0.0143 mg/kg = 1mg, confirming the standard dose.

Assessing the severity of a burn injury: A 30-year-old patient presents with a burn covering 20% of their body surface area (BSA). Using the rule of nines, the BSA can be estimated as 9% for each arm, 18% for the chest, 18% for the back, 9% for the head, and 9% for each leg. The patient's burn covers the entire front of one leg and half of the back, totaling 9% + 9% = 18% BSA, but since it only covers half of the back, the actual BSA is 9% + 4.5% = 13.5%. The patient's burn severity would be classified as moderate.

Determining the Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) score: A patient is intubated and unable to speak, with a motor response of flexion to pain and eye opening to pain. The GCS score is calculated as the sum of the eye, verbal, and motor responses. For this patient, the eye response score is 2 (eye opening to pain), the verbal response score is 1 (intubated, unable to speak), and the motor response score is 3 (flexion to pain). The total GCS score would be 2 + 1 + 3 = 6, indicating a severe brain injury.

## Applications

In medicine, Emergency Medicine is applied in various settings, including emergency departments, urgent care centers, and prehospital care. The primary goal is to provide immediate attention to patients with acute illnesses or injuries, stabilizing them and preventing further harm. Emergency physicians use a range of skills, including rapid assessment, diagnosis, and treatment, often in high-pressure situations. They must be proficient in airway management, cardiac arrest management, and trauma care. Point-of-care ultrasound and other diagnostic tools are frequently used to guide decision-making. Emergency Medicine also involves coordination with other healthcare services, such as surgery, intensive care, and radiology, to ensure seamless patient care. Additionally, emergency physicians play a critical role in public health, responding to mass casualties, infectious disease outbreaks, and other emergencies that impact community health. The application of Emergency Medicine principles is guided by evidence-based guidelines and protocols, which are continually updated to reflect best practices and improve patient outcomes.

## Common Errors

In emergency medicine, common errors include delayed or missed diagnoses, inappropriate treatment, and failure to recognize high-risk patients. One mistake is underestimating the severity of chest pain in younger patients or those with atypical presentations, leading to delayed diagnosis of acute coronary syndrome. Another error is misinterpreting electrocardiogram (ECG) results, such as mistaking a posterior myocardial infarction for a normal ECG. Inadequate assessment of patients with abdominal pain can lead to missed diagnoses of serious conditions like appendicitis or ectopic pregnancy. Overreliance on diagnostic imaging, such as computed tomography (CT) scans, can result in unnecessary radiation exposure and delays in diagnosis. Failure to consider alternative diagnoses, such as pulmonary embolism in patients with dyspnea, can also lead to errors. Additionally, inadequate communication and handoffs between healthcare providers can result in errors, highlighting the importance of clear and concise communication in emergency medicine. These mistakes often occur due to cognitive biases, such as anchoring bias or confirmation bias, which can lead to premature closure and diagnostic errors.

## Advanced

In emergency medicine, advanced topics include the application of point-of-care ultrasound (POCUS) for bedside diagnosis, and the integration of artificial intelligence (AI) and machine learning (ML) algorithms to enhance clinical decision-making. The use of telemedicine and digital health technologies is also expanding, enabling remote consultations and monitoring. Furthermore, the field is moving towards personalized medicine, incorporating genetic and biomarker data to tailor treatment strategies. Open questions remain regarding the optimal management of complex conditions such as sepsis, acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS), and traumatic brain injury. Ongoing research focuses on improving outcomes in these areas, as well as exploring the role of emergency medicine in global health, disaster response, and public health emergencies. Additionally, there is a growing emphasis on physician well-being, burnout prevention, and the development of resilient healthcare systems. The incorporation of simulation-based education and high-fidelity simulation models is also becoming increasingly important for training and assessment in emergency medicine.

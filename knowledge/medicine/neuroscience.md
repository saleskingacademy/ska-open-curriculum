# Neuroscience

> The course covers foundational concepts and principles of neuroscience, including neural structure, neurotransmitters, and brain regions, assuming some basic knowledge of biology.

## Neural Structure

~86 billion neurons in human brain. Neurons: soma, dendrites, axon, synaptic terminals. Glial cells: astrocytes (BBB, neurotransmitter clearance), oligodendrocytes (CNS myelin), Schwann cells (PNS myelin), microglia (immune). NEUROTRANSMITTERS: Excitatory: glutamate (most common), acetylcholine. Inhibitory: GABA (most common), glycine. Modulatory: dopamine (reward, movement), serotonin (mood, sleep), norepinephrine (arousal), endorphins (pain). BRAIN REGIONS: Frontal lobe (executive function, planning, personality — prefrontal cortex). Temporal (memory — hippocampus, language — Wernicke's). Parietal (sensory processing). Occipital (vision). Cerebellum (motor coordination). Brainstem (vital functions). Amygdala (fear, emotion). Basal ganglia (movement initiation). PLASTICITY: Neuroplasticity — synaptic strengthening (LTP: long-term potentiation), pruning, neurogenesis (hippocampus). Hebbian learning: neurons that fire together wire together. CONSCIOUSNESS: Hard problem (Chalmers). Neural correlates of consciousness (NCC). Default mode network. Global workspace theory (Baars). Integrated information theory (Tononi, Φ). MEMORY: Working (prefrontal, 7±2 items), short-term consolidation (hippocampus), long-term (distributed cortical). Declarative (explicit: semantic + episodic) vs procedural (implicit). Encoding, storage, retrieval. SLEEP: Stages — NREM (N1,N2,N3 slow-wave) + REM. Memory consolidation during sleep. Circadian rhythm (suprachiasmatic nucleus, melatonin).

## Foundations

In the life sciences, neuroscience is the study of the structure and function of the nervous system, which comprises the central nervous system (CNS) and the peripheral nervous system (PNS). The CNS includes the brain and spinal cord, while the PNS consists of nerves that connect the CNS to the rest of the body. A fundamental concept in neuroscience is the neuron, a specialized cell that processes and transmits information through electrical and chemical signals. Neurons have distinct components, including dendrites, which receive signals, and axons, which transmit signals to other neurons or to muscles or glands. The synapse, a small gap between two neurons, is the site of chemical transmission, where neurotransmitters, such as dopamine and serotonin, are released by the presynaptic neuron and bind to receptors on the postsynaptic neuron. Neuroglia, or glial cells, provide support and maintenance functions for neurons, including supplying them with oxygen and nutrients. The nervous system operates through the integration of neural circuits, which are networks of interconnected neurons that process and transmit information. Understanding these core components and their interactions is essential for grasping the principles of neuroscience. Key terms include neuroplasticity, the brain's ability to reorganize itself in response to experience, and homeostasis, the ability to maintain a stable internal environment despite changes in external conditions. Neurotransmitters, such as dopamine and serotonin, play crucial roles in various physiological and psychological processes. The study of neuroscience also involves understanding glial cells, which provide support and maintenance functions for neurons. Key terms in neuroscience include neuroplasticity, referring to the brain's ability to reorganize itself in response to experience or injury, and synaptogenesis, the formation of new synapses.

## Mechanisms

The neural signaling process involves a complex series of steps, starting with the transmission of signals from one neuron to another through synapses. This process begins with the release of neurotransmitters from the terminal end of the presynaptic neuron into the synaptic cleft. The binding of these neurotransmitters to specific receptors on the postsynaptic neuron triggers a series of downstream effects, including the opening of ion channels, which alters the electrical properties of the postsynaptic neuron. This can lead to either depolarization, making the neuron more likely to fire, or hyperpolarization, making it less likely. The integration of these signals from multiple synapses determines the overall excitability of the neuron, with the generation of an action potential occurring when the threshold potential is reached. The action potential is then propagated down the length of the neuron through the movement of ions across the cell membrane, allowing the signal to be transmitted to other neurons, muscles, or glands. This complex interplay of electrical and chemical signals underlies all neural functions, from simple reflexes to complex cognitive processes. The causal chain is as follows: neurotransmitter release → binding to receptors → alteration of ion channel activity → change in postsynaptic neuron excitability → generation of action potential → transmission of signal to subsequent neurons or effectors. The neural signaling process involves a complex series of steps. These neurotransmitters are chemical messengers that cross the synapse, the gap between two neurons, and bind to specific receptors on the postsynaptic neuron. This binding causes a change in the postsynaptic neuron's membrane potential, either excitatory or inhibitory. Excitatory signals increase the likelihood of the postsynaptic neuron firing, while inhibitory signals decrease this likelihood. The change in membrane potential is due to the opening or closing of ion channels, which alters the flow of ions into or out of the neuron. If the excitatory signals are strong enough, they can trigger an action potential, a rapid depolarization of the neuron's membrane, which propagates down the length of the neuron. This action potential is generated by the rapid influx of sodium ions, followed by the efflux of potassium ions, and is essential for the transmission of neural signals. This process underlies all neural communication and is fundamental to the functioning of the nervous system.

## Methods And Frameworks

In neuroscience, various methods and frameworks are employed to understand the structure and function of the nervous system. The Hodgkin-Huxley model is used to describe the electrical properties of neurons, particularly the generation and propagation of action potentials. This model is applicable when studying the electrophysiological properties of neurons, but its failure mode lies in oversimplifying the complex interactions between ion channels and the cell membrane. 
The cable theory is utilized to analyze the passive electrical properties of dendrites, allowing researchers to understand how synaptic inputs are integrated. However, its failure mode occurs when dealing with active conductances or non-uniform distributions of ion channels. 
The Hebbian theory, "neurons that fire together, wire together," is a framework for understanding synaptic plasticity, particularly long-term potentiation (LTP) and long-term depression (LTD). This theory is applicable when studying learning and memory, but its failure mode lies in not accounting for other forms of synaptic plasticity, such as homeostatic plasticity. 
The Weber-Fechner law is used to describe the relationship between stimulus intensity and perceived magnitude, which is essential for understanding sensory processing. However, its failure mode occurs when dealing with complex or dynamic stimuli. 
These methods and frameworks provide a foundation for understanding the intricate mechanisms of the nervous system, but it is essential to recognize their limitations and applicability to specific research questions. The cable theory is utilized to model the spread of electrical signals in dendrites, and is suitable for studying the integration of synaptic inputs, but fails to account for non-linear interactions between signals. The leaky integrate-and-fire model is a simplified model of neuronal firing, used to study the response of neurons to inputs, but fails to capture the complex dynamics of real neurons. These methods and frameworks provide a foundation for understanding neural function, but their limitations must be considered when interpreting results.

## Worked Examples

To illustrate key concepts in neuroscience, consider the following examples. 
1. Action Potential: A neuron has a resting membrane potential of -70mV. If the threshold potential is -55mV, and an excitatory postsynaptic potential (EPSP) of 10mV is generated, will the neuron fire? 
The initial membrane potential is -70mV. Adding the EPSP gives -60mV, still below the threshold of -55mV, so the neuron will not fire. 
2. Neurotransmitter Concentration: A synaptic cleft has a volume of 0.1 μm^3 and contains 1000 dopamine molecules. If the cleft is 20 nm wide, what is the concentration of dopamine in μM? 
First, calculate the number of moles of dopamine: 1000 molecules / (6.022 x 10^23 molecules/mole) = 1.66 x 10^-21 moles. Then, calculate the volume of the cleft in liters: 0.1 μm^3 x (1 μm / 10^6 m)^3 x (1 m / 100 cm) x (1 L / 1000 cm^3) = 1 x 10^-16 L. Finally, calculate the concentration: 1.66 x 10^-21 moles / 1 x 10^-16 L = 1.66 x 10^-5 M or 16.6 μM. 
3. Synaptic Plasticity: Long-term potentiation (LTP) is a form of synaptic plasticity that strengthens synaptic connections. If a synapse has an initial strength of 100 pA and undergoes LTP, increasing its strength by 20%, what is the new synaptic strength? 
The initial strength is 100 pA. An increase of 20% means the new strength will be 100 pA + (100 pA x 0.2) = 120 pA.

To illustrate key concepts in neuroscience, let's consider the following examples. 
1. **Action Potential Calculation**: If a neuron has a resting membrane potential of -70mV and the threshold potential is -55mV, what is the amount of depolarization needed to reach the threshold? 
Depolarization needed = Threshold potential - Resting membrane potential = -55mV - (-70mV) = 15mV. 
2. **Synaptic Transmission**: A neurotransmitter binds to its receptor with a binding affinity of 10^-8 M. If the concentration of the neurotransmitter in the synaptic cleft is 10^-6 M, what percentage of receptors are expected to be bound? 
Using the formula for receptor binding: Bound receptors = (Concentration of neurotransmitter * Binding affinity) / (1 + Concentration of neurotransmitter * Binding affinity), we can calculate the bound receptors. 
Bound receptors = (10^-6 M * 10^-8 M) / (1 + 10^-6 M * 10^-8 M) ≈ 0.001 / 1.001 ≈ 0.000999, or approximately 0.1% of receptors are bound. 
3. **Neurotransmitter Release**: If a neuron releases 10,000 vesicles of neurotransmitter per action potential, and each vesicle contains 5,000 molecules of neurotransmitter, how many molecules of neurotransmitter are released per action potential? 
Molecules released per action potential = Number of vesicles released per action potential * Number of molecules per vesicle = 10,000 vesicles * 5,000 molecules/vesicle = 50,000,000 molecules.

## Applications

Neuroscience has numerous applications in life sciences, particularly in the fields of medicine, psychology, and neurology. In clinical settings, understanding of neural mechanisms and functions informs the diagnosis and treatment of neurological and psychiatric disorders, such as Alzheimer's disease, Parkinson's disease, and depression. Neuroimaging techniques like functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) and electroencephalography (EEG) are used to visualize brain activity and structure, aiding in the identification of affected areas and the development of targeted therapies. Additionally, knowledge of neuroplasticity, the brain's ability to reorganize itself in response to injury or experience, guides rehabilitation strategies for patients with stroke, spinal cord injuries, or other forms of brain damage. Neuroscience also underlies the development of neuropharmacological interventions, such as drugs for pain management, anxiety, and attention deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD), by elucidating the neural circuits and neurotransmitter systems involved in these conditions. Furthermore, advances in neuroscience have led to the creation of brain-computer interfaces (BCIs), which enable people to control devices with their thoughts, offering new avenues for assistive technology and prosthetics. The study of neuroscience also informs our understanding of sensory perception, including vision, hearing, and pain, which is crucial for the development of therapies aimed at restoring or improving sensory function in individuals with sensory impairments.

## Common Errors

In neuroscience, common errors often stem from oversimplification or misinterpretation of complex biological processes. One mistake is attributing human-like cognitive functions to brain regions based solely on their structural homology across species. This error neglects the significant differences in neural connectivity and function that can occur between species. Another error is the failure to account for the dynamic and adaptive nature of the brain, assuming that brain function and structure are static. This overlooks neuroplasticity, the brain's ability to reorganize itself in response to new experiences, environments, and learning. Furthermore, practitioners may mistakenly assume that correlation implies causation when interpreting neuroimaging data, such as fMRI scans, without considering alternative explanations or conducting thorough statistical analyses. Additionally, the complexity of neurotransmitter systems is often underestimated, leading to oversimplification of the roles of specific neurotransmitters in various neurological conditions. These errors can lead to misinterpretation of data and incorrect conclusions about brain function and behavior, highlighting the need for a nuanced and multidisciplinary approach to neuroscience research.

In neuroscience, as studied in life sciences, several common errors occur due to misconceptions or oversimplifications of complex biological processes. One mistake is attributing human-like consciousness or self-awareness to non-human animals without considering the distinct neuroanatomical and neurophysiological characteristics of each species. Another error is assuming that brain regions are strictly specialized, ignoring the distributed and interconnected nature of brain function. For instance, the idea that specific brain areas are exclusively responsible for specific functions, such as Broca's area for language, oversimplifies the complex network interactions involved in language processing. Additionally, practitioners may incorrectly assume that neuroplasticity implies unlimited brain adaptability, neglecting the constraints imposed by genetic, developmental, and environmental factors on neural reorganization. These errors stem from oversimplification of the intricate relationships between brain structure, function, and behavior, highlighting the need for nuanced understanding of neuroscience principles.

## Advanced

In graduate-level neuroscience, researchers delve into specialized areas such as neuroplasticity, where they investigate the brain's ability to reorganize itself in response to injury or experience. Another key area is the study of neural oscillations, which are rhythmic patterns of brain activity that underlie various cognitive processes, including attention and memory. The development of new techniques, such as optogenetics and CRISPR-Cas9 gene editing, has enabled precise manipulation of neural circuits and has opened up new avenues for understanding brain function and behavior. Open questions in the field include the neural basis of consciousness, the mechanisms underlying neurodegenerative diseases, and the development of effective treatments for neurological and psychiatric disorders. Current research is also focused on integrating neuroscience with other disciplines, such as artificial intelligence and computer science, to develop more sophisticated models of brain function and to create new technologies for brain-computer interfaces and neuroprosthetics. Furthermore, the increasing use of big data and computational modeling in neuroscience is allowing researchers to analyze complex neural systems and simulate brain function in unprecedented detail, paving the way for major advances in our understanding of the brain and its functions.

In the realm of graduate-level neuroscience, researchers delve into complex topics such as neuroplasticity, synaptic pruning, and the intricacies of neural oscillations. The study of neuroplasticity, for instance, explores the brain's ability to reorganize itself in response to experience, environment, and injury. This concept challenges the long-held idea that the brain is a fixed, unchangeable entity. Furthermore, the investigation of synaptic pruning, a process by which the brain eliminates unnecessary neural connections, sheds light on the mechanisms underlying learning and memory. Additionally, the analysis of neural oscillations, which are rhythmic patterns of brain activity, has led to a deeper understanding of information processing and cognitive functions. Open questions in the field include the neural correlates of consciousness, the development of novel treatments for neurological and psychiatric disorders, and the integration of neuroscience with artificial intelligence and machine learning. The field is moving towards a more interdisciplinary approach, incorporating techniques from physics, computer science, and engineering to develop innovative tools and methods for probing brain function and behavior. The use of optogenetics, for example, allows researchers to manipulate specific neural populations with unprecedented precision, while advances in neuroimaging and brain-computer interfaces are enabling new avenues for exploring brain function and developing novel therapies.

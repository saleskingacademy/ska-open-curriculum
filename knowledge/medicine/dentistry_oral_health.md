## Anatomy

32 adult teeth (8 incisors, 4 canines, 8 premolars, 12 molars including 4 wisdom). Enamel (hardest substance in body, 96% mineral), Dentin, Pulp (nerve/blood supply), Cementum, Periodontal ligament. PREVENTIVE: Brushing (2 min 2× daily, fluoride toothpaste, 45° angle), Flossing (removes interdental plaque), Professional cleaning (scaling, root planing). Linked to cardiovascular disease, diabetes, Alzheimer's). RESTORATIVE: Fillings (composite resin, amalgam — mercury safety debate), Crowns, Bridges, Implants (titanium osseointegration — Brånemark 1965), Dentures. Root canal therapy (pulpectomy — remove infected pulp, fill canals). ORTHODONTICS: Braces (brackets + wire), Invisalign (clear aligners), Retainers. Malocclusion classification (Angle Class I/II/III). MODERN: Digital impressions (intraoral scanners), CAD/CAM (same-day crowns), Laser dentistry, 3D printing, AI-assisted diagnosis (caries detection on X-rays). ORAL-SYSTEMIC: Mouth-body connection. Periodontal bacteria enter bloodstream. Association with heart disease, stroke, diabetes, preterm birth, pneumonia. "The mouth is the gateway to systemic health." APPLIED: Global oral disease burden — 3.5B people affected. Dental market $48B+ US alone.

## Foundations

In the context of Dentistry Oral Health, core definitions and first principles are essential for practitioners to understand. **Oral health** refers to the overall well-being of the mouth, including the teeth, gums, and surrounding tissues. **Dentistry** is the branch of medicine that deals with the diagnosis, treatment, and prevention of diseases and conditions affecting the oral cavity. A **practitioner** is a licensed dentist or dental hygienist who provides oral health care services. **Core definitions** include understanding the structure and function of the oral cavity, comprising the **teeth** (hard, calcified structures used for chewing and biting), **gums** (soft tissues surrounding the teeth), **tongue**, **lips**, and **jaws**. **First principles** involve recognizing the importance of **oral hygiene** (the practice of keeping the mouth clean to prevent disease) and **plaque control** (the removal of bacterial deposits from teeth to prevent **gingivitis**, inflammation of the gums). Key vocabulary includes **caries** (tooth decay), **periodontal disease** (infection of the gums and supporting tissues), and **malocclusion** (improper alignment of the teeth and jaws). Understanding these foundations is crucial for providing effective oral health care and promoting overall health and well-being.

## Mechanisms

The maintenance of oral health in dentistry involves a complex interplay of mechanisms that prevent the onset of oral diseases. The process begins with the formation of dental plaque, a biofilm composed of bacteria, that adheres to the tooth surface. If not removed regularly through brushing and flossing, plaque can lead to the accumulation of acid-producing bacteria, which in turn causes demineralization of tooth enamel, resulting in tooth decay. 
The causal chain is as follows: poor oral hygiene leads to plaque buildup, which facilitates the growth of pathogenic bacteria. These bacteria produce acid as a byproduct of carbohydrate metabolism, lowering the pH in the oral environment. The decreased pH triggers demineralization of hydroxyapatite crystals in the enamel, creating an environment conducive to cavity formation. 
Conversely, good oral hygiene practices, such as regular brushing with fluoride toothpaste and flossing, help remove plaque and prevent the accumulation of pathogenic bacteria, thereby maintaining the integrity of tooth enamel and preventing oral diseases. The fluoride in toothpaste also promotes remineralization of early carious lesions, further protecting the teeth from decay. 
Saliva plays a crucial role in this mechanism, as it helps neutralize acid, remineralize teeth, and facilitate the removal of food particles and plaque. A decrease in salivary flow, as seen in conditions like xerostomia, can disrupt this balance and increase the risk of oral diseases. 
Understanding these mechanisms is essential for the prevention and management of oral health issues, highlighting the importance of regular dental check-ups, good oral hygiene practices, and a balanced diet in maintaining optimal oral health.

## Methods And Frameworks

In dentistry oral health, several methods and frameworks are employed to assess, diagnose, and manage oral health conditions. The DMFT (Decayed, Missing, Filled Teeth) index is used to assess the prevalence of dental caries, with higher scores indicating poorer oral health. The Oral Health Assessment Tool (OHAT) is a comprehensive framework used to evaluate oral health in older adults, incorporating factors such as soft tissue, hard tissue, and overall oral function. The Periodontal Disease Index is used to assess the severity of periodontal disease, with scores ranging from 0 (health) to 8 (severe disease). The Caries Management by Risk Assessment (CAMBRA) model is used to identify individuals at high risk of developing dental caries, allowing for targeted preventive interventions. Failure modes of these methods include incomplete data collection, inaccurate scoring, and failure to account for individual variability and risk factors. The use of these methods and frameworks requires careful consideration of their limitations and potential biases to ensure accurate and effective oral health assessment and management.

## Worked Examples

Calculating the risk of tooth decay in a patient with a high sugar diet: A 30-year-old patient consumes an average of 50 grams of sugar per day. The recommended daily sugar intake is 25 grams. Using the formula: Risk = (Actual sugar intake - Recommended sugar intake) / Recommended sugar intake, calculate the risk of tooth decay. Risk = (50 - 25) / 25 = 1, indicating a high risk of tooth decay. 
Assessing the effectiveness of fluoride toothpaste: A study found that using fluoride toothpaste reduces the incidence of tooth decay by 25%. If a patient uses fluoride toothpaste and has a baseline risk of tooth decay of 0.4, calculate the new risk. New risk = Baseline risk - (Baseline risk * Reduction in risk) = 0.4 - (0.4 * 0.25) = 0.3.
Determining the optimal frequency of dental check-ups: The American Dental Association recommends dental check-ups every 6 months for patients with a low risk of oral health issues. For patients with a high risk, such as those with a history of periodontal disease, check-ups should be scheduled every 3-4 months. If a patient has a history of periodontal disease and the dentist wants to schedule check-ups at a frequency that balances the patient's risk with the cost of visits, a possible frequency could be every 3.5 months, allowing for close monitoring while minimizing costs.

If a patient has a 40% chance of developing tooth decay without fluoride varnish, calculate the patient's chance of developing tooth decay with fluoride varnish. 
Chance of developing tooth decay with fluoride varnish = 40% - (30% of 40%) = 40% - 12% = 28%.
Determining the optimal frequency of dental check-ups for a patient with a history of periodontal disease: A patient with a history of periodontal disease requires more frequent dental check-ups to prevent disease progression. If the patient's current periodontal pocket depth is 4 mm, and the recommended pocket depth is 3 mm, calculate the optimal frequency of dental check-ups using the formula: frequency = 12 / (pocket depth - recommended pocket depth). 
Optimal frequency = 12 / (4 - 3) = 12 months, so the patient should have dental check-ups every 12 months.

## Common Errors

In dentistry oral health, common mistakes made by practitioners include inadequate assessment of patient medical history, failing to recognize signs of systemic diseases with oral manifestations, and not prioritizing preventive measures. Incorrect diagnosis and treatment of periodontal diseases, such as not distinguishing between gingivitis and periodontitis, can lead to ineffective treatment plans. Insufficient consideration of patient factors like smoking, diabetes, and medication use can also impact treatment outcomes. Furthermore, not following proper infection control protocols, such as improper sterilization of instruments and inadequate hand hygiene, can put patients at risk of infection. Additionally, over-reliance on radiographs without clinical correlation can result in misdiagnosis. These errors often stem from inadequate continuing education, poor communication with patients, and insufficient time allocated to patient evaluation. Understanding these common errors is crucial for providing high-quality oral health care and minimizing risks to patients.

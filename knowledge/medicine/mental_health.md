# Mental Health

> The course assumes foundational knowledge and delves into specialized topics such as psychopathology, treatments, and neuroscience of mental illness.

## Disorders (Dsm-5-Tr)

Depressive (MDD: depressed mood + ≥4 symptoms for 2+ weeks — sleep, interest, guilt, energy, concentration, appetite, psychomotor, suicidality). Anxiety (GAD, panic disorder, social anxiety, specific phobias, OCD, PTSD). Bipolar (I: manic episodes, II: hypomanic + depressive). Schizophrenia (positive symptoms: hallucinations, delusions; negative: flat affect, avolition; cognitive: working memory deficits). Personality disorders (Cluster A: paranoid, schizoid, schizotypal; B: antisocial, borderline, histrionic, narcissistic; C: avoidant, dependent, obsessive-compulsive). TREATMENTS: CBT (cognitive behavioral therapy — identify and restructure distorted thoughts), DBT (dialectical behavior therapy — mindfulness, distress tolerance, emotion regulation, interpersonal effectiveness — borderline), psychodynamic, humanistic. MEDICATIONS: SSRIs (fluoxetine, sertraline — increase serotonin), SNRIs (venlafaxine), mood stabilizers (lithium, valproate), antipsychotics (typical: haloperidol D2 blockade; atypical: risperidone, olanzapine 5-HT2A+D2). NEUROSCIENCE OF MENTAL ILLNESS: Monoamine hypothesis (depression = low serotonin/norepinephrine). Dopamine hypothesis (schizophrenia = excessive dopamine). Neuroplasticity-based treatments. SUICIDE PREVENTION: Risk factors (previous attempt strongest predictor, mental illness, substance use, access to means, isolation). Protective factors (social connection, treatment, restricted access to means).

## Foundations

Mental health refers to a state of well-being in which an individual realizes their own abilities, can cope with the normal stresses of life, can work productively, and is able to make contributions to their community. A practitioner must understand the core definitions, including **psychopathology**, the study of abnormal mental conditions, and **psychiatry**, the branch of medicine focused on the diagnosis, treatment, and prevention of mental, emotional, and behavioral disorders. Key terms include **mental disorder**, a clinically significant disturbance in an individual's cognition, emotion regulation, or behavior, and **mental illness**, a more severe mental disorder that significantly impairs an individual's thoughts, feelings, or behaviors. The **biopsychosocial model** is a fundamental principle, recognizing that mental health is influenced by biological, psychological, and social factors. **Neuroplasticity**, the brain's ability to reorganize itself in response to experience, is also crucial. Understanding these concepts is essential for a practitioner to provide effective care and promote mental well-being.

## Mechanisms

The development and maintenance of mental health involve complex interactions between biological, psychological, and environmental factors. At the biological level, neurotransmitters such as serotonin, dopamine, and norepinephrine play crucial roles in regulating mood, motivation, and emotional responses. Imbalances in these neurotransmitters have been linked to various mental health disorders. The causal chain begins with genetic predisposition, which influences the expression and function of neurotransmitters. Environmental factors, including stress, trauma, and social support, then impact the regulation of these neurotransmitters, leading to changes in brain structure and function. This, in turn, affects cognitive processes such as perception, attention, and memory, which are essential for emotional regulation and mental well-being. Psychological factors, including coping mechanisms, learning, and past experiences, further modulate this process, influencing an individual's vulnerability to mental health disorders. The interplay between these factors contributes to the development and progression of mental health conditions, highlighting the importance of a multifaceted approach to understanding and addressing mental health.

## Methods And Frameworks

In medicine health, several methods and frameworks are employed to assess, diagnose, and manage mental health conditions. The Biopsychosocial Model is used to understand the interplay between biological, psychological, and social factors contributing to mental health issues. The Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (DSM-5) provides a standardized framework for diagnosing mental health conditions. The Cognitive-Behavioral Therapy (CBT) model is effective in treating conditions like depression and anxiety by identifying and changing negative thought patterns. The Stress-Vulnerability Model is used to understand the relationship between stress and mental health, highlighting the importance of coping mechanisms and social support. The transtheoretical model (TTM) of behavior change is used to guide interventions, outlining six stages of change: precontemplation, contemplation, preparation, action, maintenance, and termination. Each method has its failure mode, such as the DSM-5's limitations in capturing cultural and contextual factors, CBT's potential for oversimplifying complex issues, and the TTM's assumption of linear progression through stages. Understanding these methods and frameworks, including their applications and limitations, is essential for effective mental health assessment and intervention.

## Applications

In medicine, mental health applications involve the diagnosis, treatment, and prevention of mental health disorders. Clinicians use various assessment tools, such as the Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ-9) and the Generalized Anxiety Disorder 7-item scale (GAD-7), to evaluate patients' mental health status. Treatment approaches include pharmacotherapy, psychotherapy, and psychosocial interventions. For example, selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs) are commonly used to treat depression, while cognitive-behavioral therapy (CBT) is effective in managing anxiety disorders. Mental health professionals also employ prevention strategies, such as stress management and coping skills training, to promote overall well-being. Additionally, mental health applications in primary care settings involve screening for common mental health conditions, providing brief interventions, and referring patients to specialized care when necessary. ## Common Errors

Practitioners in mental health often make mistakes due to incomplete assessments, biases, or lack of updated knowledge. One common error is misdiagnosing or overdiagnosing conditions, such as misattributing symptoms of complex trauma to borderline personality disorder. Another mistake is failing to consider cultural and socioeconomic factors that influence mental health presentations and treatment responses. Overreliance on pharmacological interventions, without adequately exploring psychotherapeutic options, is also a common error. Furthermore, neglecting to monitor and address co-occurring physical health conditions, such as chronic pain or sleep disorders, can exacerbate mental health issues. Additionally, insufficient consideration of patient preferences and values in treatment planning can lead to non-adherence and poor outcomes. These errors can be attributed to inadequate training, insufficient time for patient assessment, and lack of interdisciplinary collaboration. It is essential for practitioners to stay updated with evidence-based practices, engage in reflective practice, and prioritize patient-centered care to minimize these errors.

The role of genetics and epigenetics in mental health is also explored, including the impact of genetic variants on treatment response and the potential for personalized medicine. Additionally, the intersection of mental health and other fields, such as psychoneuroimmunology and neuroendocrinology, is examined. Open questions in the field include the development of more effective and targeted treatments for complex disorders, such as treatment-resistant depression and schizophrenia, as well as the need for more robust and reliable diagnostic biomarkers. The field is moving towards a more integrated and multidisciplinary approach, incorporating insights from psychology, neuroscience, and sociology to develop more comprehensive and effective interventions.

# Microbiology

> The course assumes basic knowledge of biological concepts and builds upon them to introduce microbiology principles.

## Bacteria

Gram-positive (thick peptidoglycan) vs gram-negative (thin + outer membrane with LPS). Shapes: cocci, bacilli, spirilla. Binary fission. Biofilms. Horizontal gene transfer: conjugation, transformation, transduction. VIRUSES: Obligate intracellular parasites. Structure: capsid + nucleic acid (DNA or RNA, ss or ds). Lytic vs lysogenic cycle. Baltimore classification. FUNGI: Eukaryotic, heterotrophic, cell walls (chitin). Yeasts (unicellular), molds (multicellular hyphae), mushrooms. Decomposers, pathogens, symbionts (mycorrhizae). PROTISTS: Diverse eukaryotes. Protozoa (amoeba, paramecium), algae (photosynthetic), slime molds. ANTIMICROBIALS: Antibiotics (β-lactams target cell wall, tetracyclines target ribosomes, fluoroquinolones target DNA gyrase). Resistance mechanisms: β-lactamase, efflux pumps, target modification. STERILIZATION: Autoclave (121°C, 15 min), UV, chemical disinfectants. Aseptic technique.

## Foundations

Microbiology is the study of microorganisms, defined as living organisms that are too small to be seen with the naked eye, typically measuring between 0.1 and 100 micrometers in size. A micrometer (μm) is one-millionth of a meter. Microorganisms include bacteria, archaea, fungi, protists, and viruses. Bacteria are prokaryotic cells, meaning they lack a true nucleus and other membrane-bound organelles. Archaea are also prokaryotic, but have distinct genetic and metabolic characteristics. Fungi are eukaryotic organisms, containing a true nucleus and other membrane-bound organelles, and obtain nutrients by decomposing organic matter or forming symbiotic relationships with other organisms. Protists are a diverse group of eukaryotic microorganisms that do not fit into any other category, and can be either autotrophic or heterotrophic. Viruses are obligate parasites, requiring a host cell to replicate, and consist of genetic material surrounded by a protein coat. The vocabulary of microbiology includes terms such as culture, referring to the growth of microorganisms in a controlled environment, and medium, the substance providing nutrients for microbial growth. Microbial metabolism refers to the chemical reactions that occur within microorganisms, allowing them to grow, maintain homeostasis, and respond to their environment.

## Mechanisms

Microbial mechanisms involve a complex interplay of cellular and molecular processes. The causal chain begins with the interaction between microorganisms and their environment, where they sense and respond to various stimuli, such as nutrients, pH, and temperature. This sensing is mediated by signal transduction pathways, which trigger a cascade of downstream effects, including gene expression and protein synthesis. The resulting proteins can have various functions, including enzymatic activity, transport, and regulation of metabolic pathways. In the case of pathogenic microorganisms, these mechanisms can lead to the production of virulence factors, which enable the microbe to colonize and infect host cells. The host's immune system responds to the infection by activating various defense mechanisms, including the production of cytokines, activation of immune cells, and the initiation of inflammatory responses. The microbe, in turn, can evade or suppress the host's immune response through various strategies, such as antigenic variation, biofilm formation, and production of immunomodulatory molecules. Understanding these mechanisms is crucial for the development of effective therapeutic strategies and prevention of infectious diseases. The study of microbial mechanisms also informs our understanding of the complex interactions between microorganisms and their environments, including the human microbiome.

This breakdown provides nutrients for microbial growth and multiplication, which in turn leads to increased interactions with the environment through quorum sensing and other mechanisms.

Understanding these mechanisms is crucial for appreciating the complex roles that microorganisms play in various ecosystems, including the human body, and for developing strategies to manipulate or control microbial activities for beneficial purposes, such as preventing infection or promoting health.

The immune system also produces antibodies, which are proteins that bind to specific microorganisms and mark them for destruction. The causal chain of immune response involves recognition, activation, and elimination of microorganisms, ultimately leading to the resolution of infection.

## Methods And Frameworks

Microbiological studies employ various methods and frameworks to understand microbial behavior, interactions, and dynamics. The Gram staining method is used to classify bacteria into Gram-positive and Gram-negative groups based on cell wall composition, with Gram-positive bacteria retaining the crystal violet stain and appearing purple under a microscope, while Gram-negative bacteria do not retain the stain and appear pink or red. The streak plate method is used to isolate pure cultures of microorganisms, involving the serial dilution of a sample on an agar plate to obtain individual colonies. 
The Koch's postulates framework is used to establish a causal relationship between a microorganism and a disease, involving four criteria: the microorganism must be found in all organisms suffering from the disease, but not in healthy organisms; the microorganism must be isolated from a diseased organism and grown in a pure culture; the cultured microorganism should cause the disease when introduced into a healthy organism; and the microorganism must be re-isolated from the inoculated, diseased experimental host and identified as being identical to the original specific causative agent. 
The failure mode of these methods can occur due to contamination, inadequate sample preparation, or incorrect interpretation of results. The Arrhenius equation (k = Ae^(-Ea/RT)) is used to model the temperature dependence of microbial growth and inactivation, where k is the rate constant, A is the pre-exponential factor, Ea is the activation energy, R is the gas constant, and T is the temperature in Kelvin. 
The Monod equation (μ = μ_max * (S / (K_s + S))) is used to model microbial growth kinetics, where μ is the specific growth rate, μ_max is the maximum specific growth rate, S is the substrate concentration, and K_s is the half-saturation constant. 
The failure mode of these models can occur due to oversimplification of complex microbial interactions or inadequate parameter estimation.

## Worked Examples

To illustrate key concepts in microbiology, consider the following examples. 
1. Calculating bacterial growth rate: Suppose a bacterial culture of E. coli doubles in population every 20 minutes. If the initial population is 10^4 cells, how many cells will be present after 2 hours? 
Using the formula for exponential growth, N(t) = N0 * 2^(t/Td), where N0 is the initial population, t is time, and Td is the doubling time, we can calculate the population after 2 hours (120 minutes). 
N(120) = 10^4 * 2^(120/20) = 10^4 * 2^6 = 10^4 * 64 = 6.4 * 10^5 cells.
2. Determining antibiotic effectiveness: A certain antibiotic reduces the growth rate of a bacterial population by 50%. If the original growth rate is 0.5 per hour, what is the new growth rate? 
The new growth rate can be calculated as follows: new growth rate = original growth rate * (1 - reduction percentage) = 0.5 * (1 - 0.5) = 0.5 * 0.5 = 0.25 per hour.
3. Calculating the amount of DNA in a bacterial cell: The genome size of E. coli is approximately 4.6 million base pairs, and each base pair weighs about 660 g/mol. If the molecular weight of DNA is 660 g/mol per base pair, how many micrograms of DNA are present in a single E. coli cell? 
First, calculate the total molecular weight of the E. coli genome: 4,600,000 base pairs * 660 g/mol = 3.036 * 10^9 g/mol. 
Then, calculate the number of moles of DNA in a single cell: assuming one copy of the genome per cell, the number of moles is 1. 
Finally, calculate the mass of DNA in a single cell: mass = number of moles * molecular weight = 1 * 3.036 * 10^9 g/mol / (6.022 * 10^23 mol^-1) = 5.04 * 10^-15 g or 5.04 * 10^-12 micrograms (since 1 microgram = 10^-6 grams).

The concentration of CFU in the diluted sample can be calculated as: CFU/mL (diluted) = CFU/mL (original) / dilution factor = 50 CFU/mL / 100 = 0.5 CFU/mL.

## Applications

Microbiology has numerous practical applications in various fields, including medicine, agriculture, and biotechnology. In medicine, microbiology is used to develop vaccines, antibiotics, and diagnostic tests to identify and treat infectious diseases. For instance, microbiological techniques such as culturing and PCR (polymerase chain reaction) are employed to detect and characterize pathogens, enabling targeted treatment and prevention strategies. In agriculture, microbiology is applied to improve crop yields and plant health through the use of microbial pesticides, fertilizers, and soil amendments. Additionally, microbiology plays a crucial role in food safety and quality control, as it helps to detect and prevent foodborne illnesses caused by microorganisms such as Salmonella and E. coli. In biotechnology, microbiology is used to produce biofuels, bioproducts, and pharmaceuticals through microbial fermentation and genetic engineering. Furthermore, microbiology has applications in environmental science, where it is used to monitor and remediate polluted ecosystems, and in forensic science, where it is used to analyze evidence and solve crimes. Overall, the principles and techniques of microbiology are essential for addressing many of the world's most pressing challenges in health, sustainability, and environmental conservation.

## Common Errors

In microbiology, common errors often stem from misunderstandings of microbial physiology, laboratory techniques, and data interpretation. One mistake is the misidentification of microorganisms due to incomplete or inaccurate biochemical testing, leading to incorrect conclusions about the presence or characteristics of specific microbes. Another error is the failure to maintain proper sterile technique, resulting in contamination of cultures and samples, which can lead to false positives or false negatives in diagnostic tests. Additionally, the misuse of antimicrobial agents, such as antibiotics, can lead to the development of resistant microbial strains, reducing the effectiveness of treatment. Furthermore, the incorrect interpretation of microbial growth curves, such as confusing the lag phase with the exponential phase, can lead to errors in understanding microbial population dynamics. These mistakes highlight the importance of rigorous training, attention to detail, and adherence to established protocols in microbiological research and practice.

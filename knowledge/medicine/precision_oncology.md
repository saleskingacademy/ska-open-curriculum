# Precision Oncology

> The course assumes prior knowledge of cancer biology, genetics, and genomics, and delves into specialized concepts and analysis.

## Foundations

Precision oncology refers to an approach to cancer treatment that involves tailoring medical interventions to the individual characteristics of each patient's cancer, leveraging advances in genetics, genomics, and molecular biology. **Genetics** is the study of heredity, genes, and variation, while **genomics** is the study of genomes, the complete set of DNA (including all of its genes) in an organism. **Molecular biology** is the study of biological processes at the molecular level, including the structure, function, and regulation of biomolecules such as DNA, RNA, and proteins. A **biomarker** is a measurable indicator of a biological process or a disease, used to diagnose, predict, or monitor disease progression. **Oncogenesis** refers to the process by which normal cells transform into cancer cells, involving genetic and epigenetic alterations that disrupt normal cellular regulation. **Epigenetics** is the study of heritable changes in gene function that occur without a change in the underlying DNA sequence, influencing gene expression and cellular behavior. **Pharmacogenomics** is the study of how genetic variation affects an individual's response to drugs, enabling personalized treatment strategies. Understanding these core concepts is essential for practitioners to develop effective precision oncology approaches, integrating knowledge of cancer biology, genetics, and genomics to inform treatment decisions.

## Mechanisms

Precision oncology involves a multi-step process that tailors cancer treatment to individual patients based on the unique genetic and molecular characteristics of their tumors. The process begins with tumor biopsy and sample collection, followed by molecular profiling using techniques such as next-generation sequencing (NGS) to identify specific genetic mutations and alterations. This information is then used to classify the tumor into a specific molecular subtype, which informs the selection of targeted therapies. The causal chain is as follows: genetic mutations lead to aberrant cellular signaling, which in turn drives tumor growth and progression. Targeted therapies, such as kinase inhibitors or monoclonal antibodies, are designed to specifically inhibit these aberrant signaling pathways, thereby halting tumor growth. Additionally, immunotherapies, such as checkpoint inhibitors, can be used to enhance the immune system's ability to recognize and attack cancer cells. The use of liquid biopsies and circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) analysis can also provide real-time information on tumor evolution and resistance mechanisms, allowing for adaptive treatment strategies. Overall, the mechanisms underlying precision oncology involve a deep understanding of the complex interplay between genetic alterations, cellular signaling, and the immune system, and the use of this knowledge to develop effective, personalized treatment plans.

## Methods And Frameworks

In precision oncology, several methods and frameworks are employed to tailor treatment to individual patients. The Next-Generation Sequencing (NGS) method is used to identify genetic mutations in tumors, guiding targeted therapy selection. When to use: NGS is ideal for patients with advanced or rare cancers. Failure mode: NGS may not detect all relevant mutations due to tumor heterogeneity. 
The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) framework provides a comprehensive understanding of genomic alterations in various cancer types. When to use: TCGA is useful for identifying potential therapeutic targets and biomarkers. Failure mode: TCGA data may not be representative of individual patient tumors due to intra-tumor heterogeneity. 
The ESMO Scale for Clinical Actionability of molecular Targets (ESCAT) is a model used to prioritize genomic alterations for clinical action. When to use: ESCAT is applied when multiple alterations are identified, and treatment decisions need to be made. Failure mode: ESCAT may not account for emerging evidence or rare alterations. 
The MSK-IMPACT (Memorial Sloan Kettering-Integrated Mutation Profiling of Actionable Cancer Targets) assay is a targeted sequencing panel used to detect mutations in solid tumors. When to use: MSK-IMPACT is suitable for patients with solid tumors, particularly those with limited tissue availability. Failure mode: MSK-IMPACT may not detect all relevant mutations due to its targeted nature. 
These methods and frameworks are crucial in precision oncology, enabling personalized treatment approaches and improving patient outcomes.

## Worked Examples

In precision oncology, targeted therapies are designed to specifically attack cancer cells while minimizing harm to healthy cells. Here are three concrete examples:
1. **HER2-positive breast cancer**: A 45-year-old woman with stage III breast cancer tests positive for HER2 overexpression. Her oncologist prescribes trastuzumab, a monoclonal antibody targeting HER2. The patient's tumor size is 5 cm. After 6 months of treatment, the tumor size decreases to 2 cm, indicating a positive response to targeted therapy.
2. **EGFR-mutated non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC)**: A 60-year-old man with stage IV NSCLC undergoes genetic testing, revealing an EGFR exon 19 deletion. His oncologist prescribes erlotinib, a tyrosine kinase inhibitor targeting EGFR. The patient's initial tumor size is 8 cm. After 3 months of treatment, the tumor size decreases to 4 cm, demonstrating the effectiveness of targeted therapy.
3. **BRAF V600E-mutated melanoma**: A 50-year-old woman with stage III melanoma undergoes genetic testing, revealing a BRAF V600E mutation. Her oncologist prescribes vemurafenib, a BRAF inhibitor. The patient's initial tumor size is 3 cm. After 2 months of treatment, the tumor size decreases to 1 cm, indicating a positive response to targeted therapy. In each example, genetic testing and targeted therapy lead to significant tumor reduction, illustrating the principles of precision oncology.

## Applications

Precision oncology involves the use of genetic and molecular profiling to tailor cancer treatment to individual patients. In practice, this approach is used to identify specific genetic mutations or alterations that drive the growth and progression of a patient's cancer. This information is then used to select targeted therapies that are most likely to be effective against the patient's specific cancer subtype. For example, patients with non-small cell lung cancer who have an EGFR mutation may be treated with EGFR inhibitors, such as erlotinib or afatinib. Similarly, patients with breast cancer who have a HER2 amplification may be treated with HER2-targeted therapies, such as trastuzumab. Precision oncology also involves the use of liquid biopsies, which allow for the detection of circulating tumor DNA in the blood, enabling non-invasive monitoring of cancer progression and response to treatment. Additionally, precision oncology is being used to develop immunotherapies, such as checkpoint inhibitors, which are designed to enhance the body's immune response against cancer cells. Overall, the goal of precision oncology is to provide more effective and personalized cancer treatment, improving patient outcomes and reducing unnecessary toxicity.

## Common Errors

In precision oncology, common mistakes include misinterpretation of genomic data, inadequate consideration of tumor heterogeneity, and failure to integrate clinical and molecular information. Overreliance on a single biomarker or genetic alteration can lead to incorrect treatment decisions, as cancer is a complex and multifactorial disease. Additionally, insufficient attention to the quality and limitations of genomic testing can result in false positives or false negatives, affecting treatment outcomes. Ignoring the principles of germline and somatic mutation distinction can also lead to incorrect assumptions about hereditary cancer risk and targeted therapy selection. Furthermore, not staying updated with the latest clinical trials and guidelines can result in suboptimal treatment choices, highlighting the need for continuous education and multidisciplinary collaboration in precision oncology.

## Advanced

The graduate-level extensions of precision oncology involve the integration of cutting-edge technologies, such as next-generation sequencing, liquid biopsies, and artificial intelligence, to further personalize cancer treatment. One key area of focus is the development of circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) analysis, which enables the non-invasive monitoring of tumor evolution and resistance mechanisms. Additionally, the use of single-cell sequencing and multi-omics approaches is providing new insights into the heterogeneity of cancer cells and the identification of novel therapeutic targets. Open questions in the field include the optimal strategies for combining and sequencing targeted therapies, as well as the development of effective treatments for cancers with high levels of genomic instability. The field is moving towards the incorporation of immunotherapy and combination regimens, with ongoing research aimed at understanding the complex interactions between the tumor microenvironment, the immune system, and cancer cells. Furthermore, the increasing availability of large-scale genomic and clinical datasets is driving the development of precision medicine approaches, such as predictive modeling and machine learning-based patient stratification, to improve treatment outcomes and reduce toxicity.

# Occupational Therapy

> The course teaches foundational concepts and principles of occupational therapy, assuming no prior study.

## Foundations

Occupational therapy (OT) is a healthcare profession that focuses on enabling individuals to achieve independence in all facets of life. **Occupation** refers to any activity that an individual performs to maintain their health, participate in daily life, and engage in meaningful pursuits. The core definition of occupational therapy is based on the concept that occupation has a therapeutic effect on the physical, mental, and emotional well-being of an individual. **Activities of daily living (ADLs)** are fundamental occupations that include tasks such as bathing, dressing, and feeding, which are essential for self-care and daily functioning. **Instrumental activities of daily living (IADLs)** are more complex occupations that require coordination and organization, such as managing finances, cooking, and cleaning. The **occupational therapy process** involves assessing an individual's abilities, identifying limitations, and developing strategies to overcome these limitations to achieve optimal participation in occupations. Key principles of occupational therapy include **client-centered practice**, which prioritizes the individual's goals and needs, and **enablement**, which focuses on empowering individuals to take control of their own health and well-being. ## Mechanisms

Occupational therapy (OT) works by enabling individuals to develop, recover, or maintain the skills needed for daily living and work through a client-centered approach. The process begins with an initial assessment, where the occupational therapist evaluates the client's physical and mental abilities, identifying areas of strength and weakness. This assessment informs the development of a personalized treatment plan, which outlines specific goals and strategies for achieving them. The therapist then applies various interventions, such as adaptive equipment, cognitive training, and physical exercises, to enhance the client's occupational performance. As the client engages in these activities, the therapist provides guidance, support, and feedback, facilitating learning and skill acquisition. Through this process, the client develops the necessary skills and strategies to overcome limitations and participate fully in their desired occupations, ultimately enhancing their overall quality of life and promoting independence. The causal chain is as follows: assessment informs treatment plan, treatment plan guides interventions, interventions enable skill development, and skill development leads to improved occupational performance and increased independence.

Through this process, occupational therapy addresses the complex interplay between the individual, their occupation, and their environment, promoting a holistic approach to health and wellness.

## Methods And Frameworks

Occupational therapy employs various methods and frameworks to facilitate patient care. The Model of Human Occupation (MOHO) is used to understand the volitional, habituation, and performance capacities of individuals, guiding therapists to address barriers to occupation. The Person-Environment-Occupation (PEO) model examines the dynamic interaction between the person, environment, and occupation, informing therapists on how to adapt environments to support occupational participation. The Canadian Model of Occupational Performance and Engagement (CMOP-E) focuses on enabling occupation through a client-centered approach, emphasizing the importance of meaningful activities. The Allen Cognitive Levels (ACL) framework assesses cognitive abilities, providing a structured approach to understanding an individual's capacity for occupational performance. The Perceive, Recall, Plan, and Perform (PRPP) system of task analysis is used to break down tasks into manageable components, facilitating the development of adaptive strategies. Each of these methods and frameworks has its own failure mode, such as overreliance on a single model, neglecting the complexities of human occupation, or failing to consider the dynamic interplay between person, environment, and occupation. Therapists must be aware of these potential pitfalls and adapt their approach to meet the unique needs of each patient.

## Worked Examples

Occupational therapy (OT) aims to enable individuals to participate in daily activities despite physical, cognitive, or mental disabilities. Here are three worked examples:

1. **Stroke Rehabilitation**: A 65-year-old patient, post-stroke, has limited arm mobility (30° shoulder flexion, 20° elbow flexion). The OT goal is to increase independence in dressing. The therapist uses a task-oriented approach, breaking down the dressing process into smaller steps. With adaptive equipment (e.g., reacher, dressing stick) and gradual practice, the patient achieves 60° shoulder flexion and 40° elbow flexion, enabling them to dress independently.

2. **Pediatric Developmental Delay**: A 4-year-old child with developmental delay has difficulty with fine motor skills, specifically using scissors (cannot cut straight line). The OT sets a goal to improve scissor skills to facilitate participation in kindergarten activities. Using a play-based approach, the therapist engages the child in scissor-related play (e.g., cutting playdough) and gradually introduces straight-line cutting tasks. After 6 weeks of therapy, the child demonstrates improved scissor skills, cutting a straight line with 80% accuracy.

3. **Mental Health and Daily Routine**: A 30-year-old patient with depression has difficulty maintaining a daily routine, including self-care activities (e.g., bathing, grooming). The OT sets a goal to establish a consistent morning routine. Using a cognitive-behavioral approach, the therapist helps the patient identify and challenge negative thought patterns, breaks down the routine into manageable steps, and provides visual reminders. After 8 weeks of therapy, the patient reports a 50% increase in self-care activities and improved overall sense of control and motivation.

## Applications

Occupational therapy is applied in various medical settings to enable individuals with physical, cognitive, or mental disabilities to achieve independence in daily activities. In rehabilitation, occupational therapists use goal-oriented interventions to help patients regain skills lost due to injury or illness, such as stroke or spinal cord injury. They assess the patient's functional abilities, identify barriers to participation, and develop personalized plans to improve performance in activities of daily living (ADLs) like bathing, dressing, and feeding. In pediatric settings, occupational therapists work with children to develop skills for play, self-care, and school-related activities, addressing conditions like autism, cerebral palsy, and attention deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD). In mental health settings, occupational therapists use evidence-based practices like cognitive-behavioral therapy and mindfulness to promote emotional regulation, stress management, and social participation. Additionally, occupational therapists collaborate with other healthcare professionals to create accessible environments, recommend adaptive equipment, and provide education on injury prevention and health promotion, ultimately enhancing the overall well-being and quality of life of individuals with diverse needs.

## Common Errors

In occupational therapy, common mistakes include failing to establish clear, client-centered goals, neglecting to incorporate the client's daily activities and routines into the treatment plan, and underestimating the importance of environmental adaptations. Practitioners may also err by not adequately assessing the client's cognitive and perceptual abilities, leading to unrealistic expectations and ineffective interventions. Additionally, insufficient documentation and poor communication with other healthcare professionals can compromise the efficacy of occupational therapy services. Another error is not recognizing the client's emotional and psychological needs, such as anxiety or depression, which can impact their ability to participate in and benefit from therapy. Furthermore, practitioners may mistakenly focus solely on remediation of deficits, rather than emphasizing compensation and adaptation strategies, which can be more effective in promoting functional independence and overall well-being. These errors can be attributed to inadequate training, lack of experience, or insufficient understanding of the client's unique needs and circumstances.

## Advanced

Occupational therapy is evolving with advancements in neuroscience, technology, and personalized medicine. Graduate-level studies delve into the intricacies of occupational science, exploring the complex relationships between occupation, health, and well-being. Emerging areas of research include the application of occupational therapy in primary care, mental health, and community-based settings. The use of telerehabilitation and virtual reality is expanding, enabling therapists to reach remote populations and create immersive, occupation-based interventions. Open questions remain regarding the optimal dosage and timing of occupational therapy interventions, as well as the development of standardized outcome measures to assess occupational performance. The field is moving towards a greater emphasis on prevention, health promotion, and occupational empowerment, with a focus on enabling individuals to participate in meaningful occupations that promote overall well-being and quality of life. Additionally, there is a growing recognition of the importance of addressing health disparities and promoting occupational justice, highlighting the need for culturally sensitive and inclusive practice. As the field continues to evolve, occupational therapists must stay abreast of emerging trends, technologies, and research to provide effective, evidence-based services that address the complex needs of diverse populations.

# Surgery Fundamentals

> The course teaches foundational concepts, key terminology, and basic principles of surgery, assuming no prior study but requiring an understanding of health sciences foundations.

## Foundations

In the context of Surgery Fundamentals, core definitions and first principles are crucial for a practitioner to understand. **Surgery** refers to a medical specialty that involves the manual or instrumental treatment of a bodily part, often involving incision, excision, or manipulation of tissue. A **surgeon** is a medical doctor who specializes in performing surgical operations. **Preoperative** care refers to the preparation and management of a patient before surgery, while **postoperative** care involves the management of a patient after surgery. **Intraoperative** care occurs during the surgical procedure. Key vocabulary includes **incision** (a cut made in the body to access the operative site), **excision** (the removal of tissue or an organ), and **suture** (a stitch or series of stitches used to close an incision). Understanding **sterility** (the state of being free from microorganisms) and **asepsis** (the state of being free from pathogenic microorganisms) is essential for preventing surgical site infections. A **surgical team** typically consists of a surgeon, **anesthesiologist** (a medical doctor who administers anesthesia), **surgical technologist** (a healthcare professional who assists the surgeon), and **nurse** (a healthcare professional who provides patient care). Familiarity with these terms and concepts is essential for effective communication and safe practice in the operating room.

## Mechanisms

Surgical procedures involve a complex interplay of physiological, anatomical, and technical factors. The process begins with preoperative preparation, where the patient is assessed for surgical risk and optimized for the procedure. This involves stabilizing vital signs, correcting coagulopathy, and administering prophylactic antibiotics as needed. The patient is then taken to the operating room, where they are positioned to maximize exposure of the surgical site. Anesthesia is induced, which can be local, regional, or general, depending on the procedure and patient factors. The surgical team, including the surgeon, anesthesiologist, and nurses, work together to maintain a sterile environment and ensure patient safety. The surgeon makes an incision, dissecting through tissue planes to access the target area. Hemostasis is achieved through various methods, including electrocautery, ligation, or topical hemostatic agents. The surgical procedure is then performed, which may involve resection, repair, or reconstruction of tissues. Throughout the procedure, the anesthesiologist monitors the patient's vital signs, managing pain, fluid balance, and blood pressure. After the procedure is completed, the incision is closed, and the patient is taken to the recovery room for postoperative care. The causal chain of events is critical, as each step affects the outcome of the subsequent steps, ultimately impacting patient recovery and outcomes.

## Methods And Frameworks

In surgery, several methods and frameworks guide decision-making and technique. The Airway, Breathing, Circulation (ABC) approach is used in emergency situations to prioritize patient stabilization. The Surgical Care Improvement Project (SCIP) framework focuses on reducing surgical complications through evidence-based practices. The World Health Organization's (WHO) Surgical Safety Checklist is a widely adopted model that ensures critical safety steps are taken before, during, and after surgery. The Halsted principles of surgery, including gentle tissue handling and meticulous hemostasis, provide a foundation for surgical technique. The SWOT analysis (Strengths, Weaknesses, Opportunities, Threats) can be applied to surgical planning, considering patient and procedural factors. Failure modes include inadequate preoperative planning, insufficient patient assessment, and poor communication among the surgical team. The ASA (American Society of Anesthesiologists) Physical Status classification system is used to assess patient risk, guiding anesthetic and surgical management. Each method and framework has its specific application and potential pitfalls, emphasizing the importance of a nuanced understanding of their appropriate use in surgical practice.

## Worked Examples

1. A patient is scheduled for an elective cholecystectomy, and the surgeon needs to determine the required amount of intravenous fluids during the procedure. The patient weighs 70 kg and the estimated blood loss is 500 ml. Using the 4:2:1 rule, calculate the required fluid replacement. 
Reasoning: The 4:2:1 rule states that for every 1 ml of blood loss, 4 ml of crystalloid, 2 ml of colloid, or 1 ml of blood should be replaced. For this patient, the estimated blood loss is 500 ml, so the required crystalloid replacement would be 500 ml x 4 = 2000 ml. 
2. A surgical team is preparing for a laparoscopic appendectomy, and the anesthesiologist needs to determine the required amount of oxygen supply. The patient's oxygen consumption is estimated to be 250 ml/min, and the procedure is expected to last 60 minutes. Calculate the total oxygen requirement. 
Reasoning: To calculate the total oxygen requirement, multiply the patient's oxygen consumption by the duration of the procedure. Total oxygen requirement = 250 ml/min x 60 min = 15000 ml. 
3. A surgeon is performing a skin grafting procedure and needs to determine the required amount of graft material. The wound area is 100 cm², and the mesh expansion ratio is 3:1. Calculate the required amount of donor skin. 
Reasoning: To calculate the required amount of donor skin, divide the wound area by the mesh expansion ratio. Required donor skin = 100 cm² / 3 = 33.33 cm².

The oxygen flow rate can be calculated using the formula: oxygen flow rate = (oxygen consumption + (tidal volume * respiratory rate)) / (1 - (FiO2 / 100)), where FiO2 is the fraction of inspired oxygen. Assuming an FiO2 of 50%, the oxygen flow rate would be (250 + (500 * 12)) / (1 - (50 / 100)).

The required suture size can be calculated using the formula: required suture size = (wound tension * wound length) / tensile strength. For a 10 cm wound with a required tension of 10 N/cm, the required suture size would be (10 * 10) / 50 = 2 mm.

## Common Errors

In surgical practice, common errors include improper patient positioning, leading to nerve damage or respiratory complications. Incorrect surgical site marking is another mistake, resulting in wrong-site surgery. Insufficient hemostasis can cause excessive bleeding, while inadequate wound closure may lead to dehiscence or infection. Failure to adhere to sterile technique and improper use of surgical instruments can also introduce pathogens, causing surgical site infections. Additionally, miscommunication among surgical team members can result in medication errors or omission of critical steps in the surgical procedure. These mistakes often stem from deviations from established protocols, inadequate preoperative planning, or lack of attention to detail. Understanding the principles of surgical safety, including checklists and time-outs, can help mitigate these errors and ensure optimal patient outcomes. Furthermore, recognizing the importance of continuous learning and improvement is crucial in minimizing mistakes and providing high-quality surgical care.

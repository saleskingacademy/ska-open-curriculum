# Public Health

> The course covers foundational concepts and principles of public health, including epidemiology, biostatistics, and social determinants, assuming some basic knowledge of health sci

## Epidemiology

Incidence (new cases/time), prevalence (total cases/population). Mortality rate, morbidity rate. Study designs: cohort (follow exposed/unexposed → outcome), case-control (outcome → look back at exposure), cross-sectional (snapshot), RCT (gold standard — randomized, controlled, double-blind). Measures: relative risk RR=incidence_exposed/incidence_unexposed, odds ratio OR=(a/c)/(b/d). SCREENING: Sensitivity (true positive rate = TP/(TP+FN)), specificity (true negative = TN/(TN+FP)). PPV, NPV depend on prevalence. ROC curves. BIOSTATISTICS: p-value (probability of result under H₀), confidence intervals, power (1-β), sample size calculation. Chi-square, t-test, ANOVA, regression, survival analysis (Kaplan-Meier, Cox regression). INFECTIOUS DISEASE: R₀ (basic reproduction number — measles ~12-18, COVID ~2-3). Herd immunity threshold = 1-1/R₀. Outbreak investigation: confirm cases, establish timeline, identify source, implement control measures. SOCIAL DETERMINANTS: Income, education, housing, food security, access to healthcare, structural racism. Health disparities. GLOBAL HEALTH: Millennium/Sustainable Development Goals. Leading causes of death: ischemic heart disease, stroke, COPD, lower respiratory infections. Burden of disease (DALYs).

## Foundations

Public health refers to the science and art of preventing disease, prolonging life, and promoting health through the organized efforts of society. A key concept is population health, which involves the health outcomes of a group of individuals, including the distribution of health outcomes within the group. Health, as defined by the World Health Organization (WHO), is a state of complete physical, mental, and social well-being, and not merely the absence of disease or infirmity. Disease prevention is a critical component, encompassing measures to prevent the onset of disease, such as health promotion, and specific protection, like vaccination. Health promotion involves enabling people to increase control over, and to improve, their health, through education, social support, and environmental changes. Epidemiology, the study of the distribution and determinants of health-related events, diseases, or health-related characteristics among populations, is a fundamental science underlying public health. A practitioner must understand these core definitions and principles to effectively address health issues at the population level.

## Mechanisms

Public health operates through a multifaceted approach, involving various stakeholders and mechanisms to prevent disease, promote health, and protect populations. The causal chain begins with surveillance and monitoring of health trends, outbreaks, and risk factors, which informs the development of policies, programs, and interventions. Health education and awareness campaigns are implemented to influence individual behaviors and community norms, promoting healthy practices such as vaccination, sanitation, and nutrition. Environmental and occupational health measures are taken to reduce exposure to hazards, including air and water pollution, and workplace injuries. Healthcare systems and services are strengthened to provide accessible, equitable, and quality care, including preventive, curative, and rehabilitative services. Additionally, public health mechanisms involve collaboration with community organizations, schools, and workplaces to promote health and prevent disease through social and economic development. The ultimate goal is to create a supportive environment that enables individuals and communities to make healthy choices, thereby reducing the burden of disease and improving overall well-being.

## Methods And Frameworks

In public health, various methods and frameworks are employed to assess, plan, and evaluate interventions. The Precede-Proceed model is used for planning and evaluation, focusing on the identification of health problems and the design of interventions. The Health Belief model is applied to understand individual health behaviors, considering factors such as perceived susceptibility and severity. The Social Ecological model examines the interplay between individual and environmental factors influencing health behaviors. The Framingham Heart Study's cardiovascular risk assessment formula is utilized to predict the risk of cardiovascular disease. The Disability-Adjusted Life Years (DALYs) formula is used to quantify the burden of disease, taking into account years of life lost and years lived with disability. Failure modes include inadequate consideration of contextual factors, oversimplification of complex health issues, and insufficient community engagement. Effective application of these methods and frameworks requires a comprehensive understanding of the underlying principles and the ability to adapt them to specific public health contexts.

## Worked Examples

Calculating the incidence rate of a disease: Suppose a town with a population of 100,000 has 250 new cases of diabetes reported over a year. The incidence rate per 100,000 population per year is calculated as (250 / 100,000) * 100,000 = 250 per 100,000. 
Assessing the effectiveness of a vaccination program: If 80% of a population of 500,000 is vaccinated against influenza and the number of reported cases decreases from 2,000 to 400, the vaccine effectiveness can be estimated as (2,000 - 400) / 2,000 = 80%. 
Evaluating the risk of a disease outbreak: In a community with 50,000 people, where 120 have been diagnosed with a waterborne illness over 2 weeks, the attack rate can be calculated as (120 / 50,000) * 100 = 0.24%. Understanding these metrics is crucial for public health interventions and policy decisions.

Calculating the incidence rate of a disease: Suppose a community of 10,000 people experiences 25 new cases of influenza over a 1-year period. To calculate the incidence rate, we use the formula: Incidence rate = (Number of new cases / Population at risk) * 100,000. Plugging in the numbers, we get: Incidence rate = (25 / 10,000) * 100,000 = 250 cases per 100,000 people per year.

Assessing the effectiveness of a vaccination program: A vaccination program is implemented in a population of 50,000 people, with 80% of the population receiving the vaccine. If the vaccine is 90% effective, what is the expected number of cases of the disease prevented? First, calculate the number of people vaccinated: 50,000 * 0.8 = 40,000. Then, calculate the number of cases that would have occurred without vaccination, assuming a baseline incidence rate of 500 cases per 100,000 people per year: 50,000 * (500 / 100,000) = 250 cases. With vaccination, the expected number of cases is reduced by 90% among the vaccinated population, so the number of cases prevented is: 250 * 0.9 * (40,000 / 50,000) = 180 cases.

Evaluating the risk of disease transmission: In a hospital setting, the risk of transmission of a bloodborne pathogen from a healthcare worker to a patient is estimated to be 0.3% per procedure. If a healthcare worker performs 500 procedures per year, what is the expected number of transmissions per year? Using the formula: Expected number of transmissions = Number of procedures * Risk per procedure, we get: Expected number of transmissions = 500 * 0.003 = 1.5 transmissions per year.

To illustrate the application of public health principles, consider the following examples. 
1. **Disease Outbreak Investigation**: In a town of 50,000 people, 250 cases of food poisoning were reported over a weekend. If the usual incidence of food poisoning is 2 cases per 100,000 people per day, calculate the attack rate and determine if this is an outbreak. 
Attack rate = (number of cases / population at risk) * 100 = (250 / 50,000) * 100 = 0.5%. 
Given the usual incidence, this is significantly higher, indicating an outbreak. 
2. **Vaccination Coverage**: A new vaccine has been introduced to protect against a specific disease, with a target coverage of 95% of the population. If 40,000 people out of a population of 50,000 have been vaccinated, calculate the coverage percentage and determine if the target has been met. 
Coverage percentage = (number vaccinated / total population) * 100 = (40,000 / 50,000) * 100 = 80%. 
The target has not been met, indicating a need for increased vaccination efforts. 
3. **Mortality Rate Calculation**: In a region, 500 deaths from a specific cause occurred over a year, with a mid-year population of 200,000. Calculate the mortality rate per 100,000 people per year. 
Mortality rate = (number of deaths / mid-year population) * 100,000 = (500 / 200,000) * 100,000 = 250 per 100,000 people per year. 
This rate can be used to compare the burden of this cause of death across different regions or over time.

## Common Errors

In public health practice, common errors include misinterpretation of epidemiological data, failure to consider confounding variables, and inadequate risk communication. Misclassification of disease outcomes or exposures can lead to biased estimates of association, affecting the validity of study findings. Ignoring the ecological fallacy, where associations observed at the group level do not necessarily represent individual-level relationships, can result in misguided interventions. Additionally, neglecting to account for reverse causality, where the outcome influences the exposure, can lead to incorrect conclusions about causation. Furthermore, public health practitioners often underestimate the impact of social determinants of health, such as socioeconomic status and education, on health outcomes, leading to ineffective interventions that do not address the root causes of health disparities. These errors can have significant consequences, including ineffective or even harmful interventions, and highlight the need for rigorous training in epidemiology, biostatistics, and research methods to ensure that public health practitioners can accurately interpret and apply evidence to inform practice.

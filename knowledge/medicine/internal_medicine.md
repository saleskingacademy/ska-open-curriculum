Internal medicine is a medical specialty focused on the diagnosis, treatment, and prevention of adult diseases. A practitioner of internal medicine is called an internist, who applies scientific knowledge and clinical expertise to diagnose and manage various internal diseases. The core definitions in internal medicine include: 
**Pathophysiology**, the study of the changes that occur within an organism as a result of disease, which informs the internist's understanding of disease mechanisms. 
**Epidemiology**, the analysis of the distribution and determinants of health-related events, diseases, or health-related characteristics among populations, is crucial for understanding disease patterns and developing preventive strategies. 
Key vocabulary includes: 
**Symptom**, a subjective experience reported by the patient, such as pain or fatigue, 
**Sign**, an objective finding detected by the physician, such as fever or abnormal laboratory results, 
**Disease**, a particular quality, habit, or disposition regarded as adversely affecting a person or group of people, 
**Diagnosis**, the process of identifying a disease or condition based on its signs, symptoms, and laboratory results. 
First principles in internal medicine involve a thorough patient evaluation, including a detailed medical history, physical examination, and diagnostic testing to establish an accurate diagnosis and develop an effective treatment plan. Key vocabulary includes terms like symptom, a subjective experience reported by the patient, and sign, an objective observation made by the practitioner. A thorough understanding of these foundations is crucial for effective patient care and management.

## Mechanisms

Internal medicine operates through a complex interplay of physiological, pathological, and therapeutic mechanisms. The process begins with the presentation of a patient's symptoms, which are then evaluated through a combination of medical history, physical examination, and diagnostic tests. This initial assessment informs the development of a differential diagnosis, a list of potential causes for the patient's condition. The differential diagnosis guides further investigation, including the selection of laboratory tests, imaging studies, or other diagnostic procedures. As more information becomes available, the differential diagnosis is refined, and a working diagnosis is established. This diagnosis is then used to develop a treatment plan, which may involve pharmacological interventions, lifestyle modifications, or other therapeutic strategies. The treatment plan is implemented, and the patient's response is monitored, allowing for adjustments to the plan as needed. Throughout this process, the internal medicine physician must consider the patient's unique characteristics, including comorbidities, medications, and personal preferences, to provide individualized care. The therapeutic mechanisms of internal medicine interventions, such as medications or lifestyle changes, act on specific physiological pathways to restore health or mitigate disease. For example, medications may act on receptors, enzymes, or ion channels to alter disease-related processes, while lifestyle changes, such as diet or exercise, may influence metabolic or inflammatory pathways. The ultimate goal of these mechanisms is to restore normal physiological function, alleviate symptoms, and improve patient outcomes. The process begins with patient presentation, where symptoms and medical history are evaluated to formulate a differential diagnosis. This step involves the application of knowledge about human physiology and pathophysiology to understand how diseases disrupt normal bodily functions. The next step is diagnostic testing, which may include laboratory tests, imaging studies, or other procedures to gather more information about the patient's condition. These tests help identify the underlying cause of the symptoms, guiding the clinician through a causal chain of disease progression. For example, in the case of hypertension, the mechanism may involve renal sodium retention leading to volume expansion, increased cardiac output, and ultimately, elevated blood pressure. Treatment mechanisms then come into play, such as the use of diuretics to reduce volume overload or ACE inhibitors to decrease peripheral resistance, aiming to interrupt the disease process and restore normal physiological function. Throughout this process, the internal medicine specialist must consider the patient's unique factors, including comorbidities, medications, and lifestyle, to tailor the therapeutic approach and maximize efficacy while minimizing adverse effects. The ongoing monitoring and adjustment of treatment plans further illustrate the dynamic and patient-centered nature of internal medicine mechanisms.

Internal medicine involves the diagnosis, treatment, and prevention of adult diseases, with a focus on the internal organs and systems. The mechanisms of internal medicine can be understood through the following steps: 
1. **Patient Presentation**: A patient presents with symptoms, which are evaluated by the physician to identify patterns and potential underlying causes.
2. **History Taking**: A thorough medical history is taken to gather information about the patient's symptoms, medical history, and lifestyle.
3. **Physical Examination**: A physical examination is performed to gather more information about the patient's condition, including vital signs, organ function, and potential abnormalities.
4. **Diagnostic Testing**: Diagnostic tests, such as laboratory tests and imaging studies, are ordered to confirm or rule out potential diagnoses.
5. **Differential Diagnosis**: The physician generates a list of potential diagnoses, known as a differential diagnosis, based on the patient's symptoms, medical history, and test results.
6. **Treatment Planning**: A treatment plan is developed, taking into account the patient's diagnosis, medical history, and lifestyle.
7. **Therapeutic Intervention**: The treatment plan is implemented, which may involve medications, lifestyle modifications, or other interventions.
8. **Monitoring and Follow-up**: The patient's response to treatment is monitored, and adjustments are made as necessary to achieve optimal outcomes.
The causal chain in internal medicine involves the interplay between the patient's symptoms, medical history, and test results, which inform the diagnosis and treatment plan, ultimately leading to improved patient outcomes.

## Methods And Frameworks

## Worked Examples

1. A 65-year-old patient presents with symptoms of pneumonia, including a fever of 38.5°C and a cough producing yellow sputum. The patient's white blood cell count is 15,000 cells/μL. To determine the severity of the pneumonia, the CURB-65 score is used. This score assigns points for: Confusion (C), Urea > 7 mmol/L (U), Respiratory rate ≥ 30 breaths/min (R), Blood pressure < 90 mmHg systolic or ≥ 60 mmHg diastolic (B), and age ≥ 65 years (65). If the patient has a urea level of 10 mmol/L, is not confused, has a respiratory rate of 32 breaths/min, a blood pressure of 120/80 mmHg, and is 65 years old, what is the CURB-65 score? 
Reasoning: The patient scores points for urea level (1 point), respiratory rate (1 point), and age (1 point), totaling 3 points. 
2. A patient with type 2 diabetes has a hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c) level of 9%. To assess the effectiveness of the current treatment regimen, the HbA1c level needs to be interpreted. HbA1c levels are used to estimate average blood glucose levels over the preceding 2-3 months. An HbA1c level of 9% indicates that the patient's blood glucose levels have been higher than the target range. 
Reasoning: The American Diabetes Association recommends an HbA1c target of < 7% for most adults. This patient's HbA1c level is above the target, indicating the need for adjustment of the treatment plan.
3. A patient with hypertension has a blood pressure reading of 160/100 mmHg. To determine the stage of hypertension, the blood pressure needs to be classified according to the guidelines. Stage 1 hypertension is defined as a systolic blood pressure of 130-139 mmHg or a diastolic blood pressure of 80-89 mmHg. Stage 2 hypertension is defined as a systolic blood pressure of ≥ 140 mmHg or a diastolic blood pressure of ≥ 90 mmHg. 
Reasoning: The patient's blood pressure is 160/100 mmHg, which falls into the category of Stage 2 hypertension due to both the systolic and diastolic values exceeding the thresholds for Stage 1. The score is calculated based on the presence of confusion, urea > 7 mmol/L, respiratory rate > 30 breaths/min, blood pressure < 90 mmHg, and age > 65. The score is 2 (1 point for urea > 7 mmol/L and 1 point for respiratory rate > 30 breaths/min).

1. A 65-year-old patient with a history of hypertension and hyperlipidemia presents with symptoms of chest pain and shortness of breath. The patient's electrocardiogram (ECG) shows ST-segment elevation in leads II, III, and aVL, indicating an acute myocardial infarction (MI). The patient's troponin level is 10 ng/mL, and the creatinine kinase (CK) level is 200 U/L. What is the diagnosis and initial management? 
Diagnosis: Acute ST-segment elevation MI (STEMI). 
Initial management: Administer aspirin 325 mg, clopidogrel 600 mg, and metoprolol 5 mg IV, and arrange for immediate coronary angiography and percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) if possible.

2. A 40-year-old patient with a history of type 2 diabetes mellitus presents with symptoms of polyuria, polydipsia, and weight loss. The patient's fasting blood glucose level is 250 mg/dL, and the hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c) level is 10%. What is the diagnosis and initial management? 
Diagnosis: Uncontrolled type 2 diabetes mellitus. 
Initial management: Initiate metformin 500 mg twice daily, and consider adding a sulfonylurea or a glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonist if necessary. Also, provide patient education on diet, exercise, and glucose monitoring.

3. A 75-year-old patient with a history of chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) presents with symptoms of worsening dyspnea, cough, and sputum production. The patient's oxygen saturation is 88% on room air, and the forced expiratory volume in 1 second (FEV1) is 40% of predicted. What is the diagnosis and initial management? 
Diagnosis: Acute exacerbation of COPD. 
Initial management: Administer oxygen therapy to maintain an oxygen saturation of at least 90%, and initiate bronchodilator therapy with albuterol 2.5 mg and ipratropium 0.5 mg via nebulizer. Consider adding oral corticosteroids and antibiotics if necessary.

2. A patient with type 2 diabetes has a hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c) level of 9.5%. The goal is to reduce the HbA1c level to < 7%. If the patient's current medication regimen includes metformin 1000 mg twice daily, what additional medication could be considered to help achieve the goal? 
Considering the patient's HbA1c level, adding a sulfonylurea or a dipeptidyl peptidase-4 (DPP-4) inhibitor could be an option to enhance glycemic control.

3. A 50-year-old patient with hypertension has a blood pressure reading of 160/95 mmHg. The patient is currently taking an angiotensin-converting enzyme (ACE) inhibitor at a dose of 20 mg daily. To achieve a blood pressure goal of < 140/90 mmHg, what adjustment could be made to the patient's medication regimen? 
The dose of the ACE inhibitor could be increased to 40 mg daily, or an additional antihypertensive agent such as a calcium channel blocker could be added to the regimen.

## Applications

Internal medicine has numerous applications in practice, primarily focusing on the diagnosis, treatment, and prevention of adult diseases. Internists use a wide range of diagnostic tools, including medical history, physical examination, and laboratory tests, to identify and manage complex medical conditions. They often serve as primary care physicians, providing routine check-ups, health screenings, and preventative care to their patients. In the hospital setting, internists work as hospitalists, caring for patients with acute illnesses, such as pneumonia, myocardial infarction, and stroke. They also specialize in various subspecialties, including cardiology, gastroenterology, and nephrology, among others. Additionally, internists play a crucial role in managing chronic diseases, such as diabetes, hypertension, and asthma, through lifestyle modifications, medication management, and ongoing monitoring. The application of internal medicine principles also extends to palliative care, where internists focus on alleviating symptoms, improving quality of life, and providing emotional support to patients with serious illnesses. Overall, the practice of internal medicine requires a deep understanding of the complex interplay between various bodily systems and the ability to integrate this knowledge to provide comprehensive patient care.

## Common Errors

In internal medicine, common errors include misdiagnosis or delayed diagnosis due to inadequate history taking or physical examination. Overreliance on laboratory tests and imaging can lead to missing clinical diagnoses. Failure to consider alternative diagnoses and not ruling out other potential causes of a patient's symptoms can result in incorrect treatment. Insufficient consideration of a patient's medications, including potential interactions and side effects, can also lead to errors. Additionally, not adhering to established guidelines and protocols for managing chronic conditions, such as hypertension and diabetes, can result in suboptimal care. Furthermore, inadequate communication with patients, including poor explanation of diagnoses and treatment plans, can lead to non-adherence and poor health outcomes. These errors can be attributed to various factors, including cognitive biases, such as anchoring bias and confirmation bias, which can influence clinical decision-making. Practitioners must be aware of these potential pitfalls and strive to maintain a high level of vigilance and critical thinking in their practice.

## Advanced

Internal medicine is evolving with advancements in genomics, precision medicine, and digital health. Graduate-level studies focus on complex disease management, such as multimorbidity and polypharmacy, requiring nuanced understanding of pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics. Emerging areas include personalized medicine, where genetic profiles and biomarkers guide treatment decisions, and immunotherapy, which leverages the immune system to combat diseases like cancer and autoimmune disorders. Open questions remain in the management of chronic conditions, such as diabetes and hypertension, where optimal treatment strategies and prevention methods are still being refined. The field is moving towards integrated care models, incorporating behavioral health, social determinants, and patient-centered approaches to improve health outcomes and reduce disparities. Furthermore, the increasing use of artificial intelligence and machine learning in internal medicine is expected to enhance diagnostic accuracy, streamline clinical workflows, and facilitate research in disease prevention and treatment.

The graduate-level extensions of internal medicine involve complex patient management, nuanced diagnostic approaches, and the integration of emerging evidence and technologies.

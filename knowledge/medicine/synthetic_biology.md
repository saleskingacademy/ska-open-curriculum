## Definition

Engineering biology — designing and building new biological systems or redesigning existing ones. Merger of biology + engineering + computer science. FOUNDATIONAL: DNA synthesis (write custom DNA sequences — cost dropped from $10/base to $0.01), Gene editing (CRISPR-Cas9 — Doudna & Charpentier, Nobel 2020 — precise cut-and-paste of DNA), Standard biological parts (BioBricks — standardized genetic components), Genetic circuits (logic gates, switches, oscillators built from DNA). APPLICATIONS: Medicine (CAR-T cell therapy — engineered immune cells that target cancer, gene therapy — Luxturna for blindness, mRNA vaccines — COVID platform), Agriculture (drought/pest-resistant crops, nitrogen fixation engineering, cellular agriculture), Materials (spider silk — Bolt Threads, mycelium leather — MycoWorks, bioplastics), Energy (engineered microbes for biofuel production, artificial photosynthesis), Computing (DNA data storage — 1 gram = 215 petabytes, biological computers). CELLULAR AGRICULTURE: Lab-grown meat (Good Meat, Upside Foods — FDA/USDA approved 2023), Precision fermentation (dairy proteins without cows — Perfect Day, whey/casein from yeast), Cultured materials. BIOSECURITY: Dual-use concern — same tools that cure disease could create bioweapons. DNA synthesis screening. International Biosecurity Innovation Council. Responsible research frameworks. iGEM competition (international student synthetic biology). COMPANIES: Ginkgo Bioworks (organism engineering platform), Twist Bioscience (DNA synthesis), Moderna (mRNA platform), Zymergen, Pivot Bio. MARKET: $30B+ (2024), projected $80B+ by 2030. APPLIED: Synbio is "the next industrial revolution." Biology becomes programmable. Manufacturing shifts from chemical to biological processes.

## Foundations

Synthetic biology is an interdisciplinary field in life sciences that combines engineering principles with biological systems to design, construct, and modify new biological systems, such as genetic circuits, or to redesign existing ones. A **biological system** refers to a network of interacting biological components, including genes, proteins, and cells, that function together to achieve a specific biological function. **Genetic engineering** is the direct manipulation of an organism's genes using biotechnology to introduce desired traits or characteristics. **Biotechnology** is the use of biological systems, living organisms, or derivatives thereof, to develop new products, technologies, and processes. A **genetic circuit** is a set of genes and regulatory elements that interact to control gene expression, analogous to electronic circuits. **Gene expression** is the process by which the information encoded in a gene is converted into a functional product, such as a protein. Synthetic biologists use **standardized biological parts**, such as promoters, terminators, and coding sequences, which are well-characterized and modular components that can be combined to create new biological systems. **Promoters** are regulatory sequences that initiate transcription of a gene, while **terminators** are sequences that stop transcription. Understanding these core concepts and vocabulary is essential for practitioners in the field of synthetic biology.

## Mechanisms

Synthetic biology operates through the design and construction of new biological systems, such as genetic circuits, to achieve specific functions. The process begins with the identification of a biological problem or opportunity, followed by the design of a genetic circuit to address it. This involves the use of computational models and simulations to predict the behavior of the circuit. The designed circuit is then constructed using DNA synthesis and assembly techniques, such as Gibson assembly or CRISPR-Cas9 genome editing. The constructed circuit is then introduced into a host organism, such as E. coli, using transformation or transfection methods. The host organism expresses the genetic circuit, which interacts with the host's native biological processes to produce the desired output. The output is then measured and characterized using various biochemical and biophysical techniques, such as fluorescence microscopy or mass spectrometry. The results are used to refine the design and construction of the genetic circuit, creating a feedback loop that allows for iterative improvement. This design-build-test cycle is the core mechanism of synthetic biology, enabling the development of novel biological systems with precise and predictable functions.

## Methods And Frameworks

In synthetic biology, several methods and frameworks are employed to design, construct, and test new biological systems. The Rational Design method involves a top-down approach, where a biological system is designed based on its desired function and then constructed using genetic engineering techniques. This method is useful when the desired function is well-defined, but can fail if the system's behavior is highly nonlinear or uncertain. 
The Directed Evolution method, on the other hand, uses an iterative process of mutation, selection, and amplification to evolve a biological system towards a desired function. This method is useful when the desired function is not well-defined or when the system's behavior is highly complex, but can fail if the evolutionary process is not properly guided. 
The Model-Guided Design framework combines mathematical modeling with experimental design to predict and test the behavior of biological systems. This framework is useful when the system's behavior can be accurately modeled, but can fail if the model is incomplete or inaccurate. 
Key formulas, such as the Hill equation and the Michaelis-Menten equation, are used to model gene regulation and enzyme kinetics, respectively. These formulas are useful for predicting the behavior of biological systems, but can fail if the underlying assumptions are not met. 
Overall, the choice of method or framework depends on the specific goals and constraints of the synthetic biology project, as well as the complexity and uncertainty of the biological system being designed.

## Worked Examples

1. Designing a genetic circuit: Suppose we want to engineer a bacterial strain to produce a therapeutic protein when induced by a specific sugar. We can use a promoter that is activated by the sugar to drive the expression of the protein. For example, the lac operon promoter in E. coli can be induced by lactose. If we want to produce 100 mg/L of protein, and the promoter has a basal expression level of 10% and an induced expression level of 90%, what percentage of cells need to be induced to achieve the desired protein production? 
Let's assume the protein production rate per cell is 1 mg/L/h. With 10^9 cells/L, 90% induction will produce 90 mg/L/h, and 10% basal expression will produce 1 mg/L/h. To achieve 100 mg/L, we need 100 mg/L / (90 mg/L/h * 1h) = 1.11 * 10^9 cells/L to be induced, which is approximately 111% of the total cells, indicating that nearly all cells need to be induced.

2. Optimizing metabolic pathways: Consider a metabolic pathway that converts glucose to a valuable chemical, with an intermediate step converting glucose-6-phosphate to 6-phosphogluconate. The reaction is catalyzed by an enzyme with a Km of 1 mM and Vmax of 100 μmol/min/mg. If we want to increase the production rate of the chemical by 20%, what should be the concentration of glucose-6-phosphate? 
Using the Michaelis-Menten equation, v = Vmax * [S] / (Km + [S]), where v is the reaction rate, [S] is the substrate concentration, we can calculate the required substrate concentration. Let's assume the current production rate is 80 μmol/min/mg, and we want to increase it to 96 μmol/min/mg. Rearranging the equation to solve for [S], we get [S] = Km * v / (Vmax - v). Plugging in the numbers, [S] = 1 mM * 96 μmol/min/mg / (100 μmol/min/mg - 96 μmol/min/mg) = 24 mM.

3. Gene expression tuning: Suppose we want to fine-tune the expression of a gene by adjusting the ribosome binding site (RBS) strength. The gene has a current expression level of 1000 molecules/cell, and we want to reduce it to 500 molecules/cell. If the RBS strength is currently 0.1, what should be the new RBS strength? 
Using the principle of gene expression tuning, the expression level is proportional to the RBS strength. Let's assume the relationship is linear. We can set up a proportion: 1000 molecules/cell / 0.1 = 500 molecules/cell / x, where x is the new RBS strength. Solving for x, we get x = 0.05. Therefore, the new RBS strength should be 0.05, which is half of the original strength.

## Common Errors

In synthetic biology, several common errors can occur due to the complexity of designing and constructing new biological systems. One mistake is the failure to consider the context and interactions of the host organism, leading to unintended consequences such as off-target effects or metabolic burden. Another error is the assumption that a genetic circuit will function as predicted, without accounting for variability in gene expression, noise, and feedback loops. Additionally, practitioners may overlook the importance of characterizing and standardizing biological parts, such as promoters, ribosome binding sites, and terminators, which can lead to inconsistent performance and difficulties in scaling up designs. Furthermore, the use of inadequate or incomplete modeling and simulation tools can result in flawed designs that do not account for the dynamic and nonlinear behavior of biological systems. These errors can be mitigated by adopting a rigorous and iterative design-build-test cycle, incorporating experimental validation and refinement, and considering the principles of biological robustness, evolvability, and modularity.

Another error is the failure to consider the kinetic parameters of enzymatic reactions, leading to metabolic pathway imbalances and reduced productivity. These errors can be attributed to a lack of understanding of the underlying biological principles, inadequate modeling and simulation, and insufficient experimental validation. By recognizing these common errors, synthetic biologists can take a more holistic approach to design, considering the complex interactions between genetic and environmental factors, and iteratively test and refine their designs to achieve more predictable and reliable outcomes.

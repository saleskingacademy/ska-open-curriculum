## Foundations

In model theory, a **theory** is a set of sentences in a formal language, typically expressed using **predicate logic**, which is a system for expressing and reasoning about logical statements using **predicates** (functions that assign properties to objects) and **quantifiers** (symbols that express the scope of a predicate, such as "for all" or "there exists"). A **model** of a theory is a mathematical structure that satisfies all the sentences of the theory, where a **structure** consists of a non-empty set of objects, called the **domain** or **universe**, together with interpretations of the predicates and functions in the language. The **vocabulary** of a theory consists of the set of symbols used to express the theory, including **constant symbols** (which represent specific objects in the domain), **function symbols** (which represent functions from the domain to itself), and **relation symbols** (which represent relations among objects in the domain). A **sentence** is a formula with no free variables, and a **formula** is a string of symbols in the language that can be either true or false in a given model. The **semantics** of a theory provide a way of assigning meaning to the sentences of the theory in terms of the models, where a sentence is **true** in a model if it is satisfied by all possible assignments of values to the variables in the sentence.

## Common Errors

In model theory, a common mistake is confusing the notions of elementary equivalence and isomorphism. Elementary equivalence refers to the idea that two structures satisfy the same first-order sentences, whereas isomorphism requires a bijective mapping between the structures that preserves all relations. Practitioners may incorrectly assume that if two structures are elementarily equivalent, they must be isomorphic, which is not necessarily true. Another error is neglecting to consider the distinction between a theory and its models, leading to confusion between syntactic properties of the theory (e.g., consistency, completeness) and semantic properties of its models (e.g., satisfiability, validity). Additionally, some may mistakenly believe that a theory's categoricity (i.e., having a unique model up to isomorphism) implies its completeness, when in fact these are separate concepts. These errors often arise from a lack of careful attention to the definitions and distinctions fundamental to model theory, highlighting the importance of precise understanding of the subject's core concepts.

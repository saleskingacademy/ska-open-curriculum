# Zero Knowledge Proofs

> The course assumes basic knowledge of discrete mathematics and cryptography, but still introduces core concepts and key terminology.

## Foundations

Zero-knowledge proofs (ZKPs) rely on discrete mathematics and cryptography. A **proof** is a mathematical demonstration that a statement is true. In ZKPs, this involves a **prover** (P) convincing a **verifier** (V) of the validity of a statement without revealing any underlying information. The **statement** refers to a claim about a mathematical relationship or property. A **witness** is a value or set of values that demonstrates the truth of the statement. 
The core principle of ZKPs is that they are **computationally zero-knowledge**, meaning that the verifier learns nothing beyond the validity of the statement, even if they have unlimited computational power. This is achieved through **probabilistic** methods, where the proof is repeated multiple times to minimize the probability of error. 
Key concepts include **completeness** (the prover can convince the verifier if the statement is true), **soundness** (the prover cannot convince the verifier if the statement is false), and **zero-knowledge** (the verifier learns nothing beyond the validity of the statement). ZKPs can be **interactive** (requiring multiple rounds of communication between prover and verifier) or **non-interactive** (where the prover sends a single proof to the verifier).

## Mechanisms

Zero Knowledge Proofs (ZKPs) are interactive systems that enable one party, the prover, to demonstrate the truth of a statement to another party, the verifier, without revealing any information beyond the validity of the statement itself. The mechanism involves a series of steps: 
1. **Common Reference String**: Both parties agree on a common reference string, which is a publicly available, randomly generated string used to ensure the proof's randomness and unpredictability.
2. **Commitment**: The prover commits to a value or statement without revealing it, using a commitment scheme such as a hash function or homomorphic encryption.
3. **Challenge**: The verifier generates a random challenge, which is sent to the prover. This challenge is used to test the prover's knowledge and prevent cheating.
4. **Response**: The prover responds to the challenge by generating a proof that demonstrates their knowledge of the committed value or statement, without revealing any information about it.
5. **Verification**: The verifier checks the proof to ensure it is valid and that the prover has demonstrated the required knowledge. This is done using a verification algorithm that checks the proof against the committed value and the challenge.
The causal chain is as follows: the prover's commitment and the verifier's challenge causally influence the prover's response, which in turn is verified by the verifier. The use of randomness and cryptographic primitives ensures that the prover cannot cheat or reveal any information beyond the validity of the statement, thus maintaining the zero-knowledge property.

Failure mode: if the prover's secret is compromised, the proof is no longer secure.

## Common Errors

In the implementation and application of Zero Knowledge Proofs (ZKPs), several common errors can compromise the security and validity of the proof system. One mistake is incorrectly assuming that the zero-knowledge property implies perfect secrecy of the witness. While ZKPs ensure that no information about the witness is revealed beyond the validity of the statement, they do not guarantee that an adversary cannot infer any information about the witness through side channels or other means. Another error is failing to properly define the statement to be proven, leading to ambiguities in what is being proven and potentially allowing an adversary to exploit these ambiguities. Additionally, practitioners may incorrectly implement the proof protocol, such as using an insecure commitment scheme or failing to ensure the soundness and completeness of the proof system. Soundness ensures that a false statement cannot be proven, while completeness ensures that a true statement can be proven. If these properties are not carefully established, the ZKP may be vulnerable to attacks. Furthermore, errors in parameter selection, such as key sizes and security parameters, can also compromise the security of the ZKP. It is crucial for practitioners to carefully follow established protocols and rigorously test their implementations to avoid these common errors and ensure the security and validity of the Zero Knowledge Proof system. One mistake is incorrectly assuming that the zero-knowledge property implies the proof is also sound, which is not necessarily the case. Soundness ensures that a dishonest prover cannot convince the verifier of a false statement, whereas zero-knowledge only guarantees that the verifier learns nothing beyond the validity of the statement. Another error is failing to ensure the completeness of the proof system, where an honest prover may not be able to convince the honest verifier due to flaws in the protocol. Additionally, practitioners may overlook the importance of choosing appropriate parameters and cryptographic primitives, such as hash functions and elliptic curves, which can lead to vulnerabilities in the proof system. A common pitfall is also misunderstanding the difference between simulation-based and extraction-based definitions of zero-knowledge, leading to incorrect security proofs or assumptions about the protocol's security.

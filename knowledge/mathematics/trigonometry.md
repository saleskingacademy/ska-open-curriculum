## Foundations

Trigonometry is the branch of mathematics that studies the relationships between angles and lengths in triangles, primarily right-angled triangles, extending to circular and periodic functions. At its core, it formalizes the connection between linear and angular measures via ratios of sides, enabling the analysis of geometric configurations and periodic phenomena. The foundational elements rest on the unit circle definition, where an angle θ corresponds to a point (cos θ, sin θ) on the circle of radius 1 centered at the origin in the Cartesian plane. This links algebraic functions with geometric intuition and underpins the six fundamental trigonometric functions: sine (sin), cosine (cos), tangent (tan), cotangent (cot), secant (sec), and cosecant (csc). These functions satisfy key identities such as the Pythagorean identity sin²θ + cos²θ = 1, and their periodicity and symmetry properties enable extensive applications in analysis, physics, and engineering.

In mathematics, trigonometry is the study of the relationships between the sides and angles of triangles, particularly triangles with right angles (90 degrees). A **right triangle** is a triangle with one angle equal to 90 degrees. The side opposite the right angle is called the **hypotenuse**, and the other two sides are referred to as the **legs** of the triangle. The **angles** of a triangle are measured in **degrees**, with 360 degrees in a full circle.

The core definitions in trigonometry involve the ratios of the lengths of the sides of a right triangle. The **sine** (sin) of an angle is defined as the ratio of the length of the leg opposite the angle to the length of the hypotenuse. The **cosine** (cos) of an angle is defined as the ratio of the length of the leg adjacent to the angle to the length of the hypotenuse. The **tangent** (tan) of an angle is defined as the ratio of the length of the leg opposite the angle to the length of the leg adjacent to the angle.

These ratios are the foundation of trigonometric functions, which describe the relationships between the angles and side lengths of triangles. Understanding these definitions and the relationships between the sides and angles of triangles is crucial for applying trigonometric principles to solve problems in mathematics and other fields.

## Right Triangle Ratios

Given a right triangle with acute angle θ, the primary trigonometric ratios are defined as:  
- sin θ = opposite/hypotenuse  
- cos θ = adjacent/hypotenuse  
- tan θ = opposite/adjacent = sin θ / cos θ  
- cot θ = adjacent/opposite = cos θ / sin θ  
- sec θ = hypotenuse/adjacent = 1 / cos θ  
- csc θ = hypotenuse/opposite = 1 / sin θ  
These ratios allow for the determination of unknown sides or angles using the Law of Sines and Law of Cosines in oblique triangles, extending trigonometry beyond right-angled cases.

## Unit Circle And Radian Measure

The unit circle framework defines trigonometric functions for all real θ by associating θ with a point on the circle x² + y² = 1, where cos θ = x and sin θ = y. Angles are measured in radians, where 2π radians correspond to 360°, and 1 radian equals the angle subtending an arc equal in length to the radius. This approach generalizes trigonometric functions to negative angles and angles greater than 2π, enabling periodicity analysis: sin(θ + 2π) = sin θ, cos(θ + 2π) = cos θ. Key values include sin(0) = 0, sin(π/2) = 1, cos(0) = 1, cos(π/2) = 0, and the symmetry relations sin(−θ) = −sin θ, cos(−θ) = cos θ.

## Trigonometric Identities And Formulas

- Pythagorean identities: sin²θ + cos²θ = 1; 1 + tan²θ = sec²θ; 1 + cot²θ = csc²θ  
- Angle sum and difference:  
  sin(α ± β) = sin α cos β ± cos α sin β  
  cos(α ± β) = cos α cos β ∓ sin α sin β  
  tan(α ± β) = (tan α ± tan β) / (1 ∓ tan α tan β)  
- Double angle formulas:  
  sin 2θ = 2 sin θ cos θ  
  cos 2θ = cos²θ − sin²θ = 2 cos²θ − 1 = 1 − 2 sin²θ  
  tan 2θ = 2 tan θ / (1 − tan²θ)  
- Half-angle formulas:  
  sin²(θ/2) = (1 − cos θ)/2  
  cos²(θ/2) = (1 + cos θ)/2  
- Product-to-sum and sum-to-product identities facilitate integration and simplification in analysis.

## Laws Of Triangles

- Law of Sines: a / sin A = b / sin B = c / sin C = 2R, where R is the circumradius  
- Law of Cosines: c² = a² + b² − 2ab cos C, generalizing the Pythagorean theorem to any triangle  
These laws enable solving arbitrary triangles given partial data (SSS, SAS, ASA, AAS), crucial for navigation, surveying, and physics.

## Trigonometric Equations And Inverse Functions

Solving equations like sin x = a, cos x = b, or tan x = c involves understanding the periodicity and principal values of inverse functions arcsin, arccos, and arctan. For example, sin x = 1/2 has solutions x = π/6 + 2kπ and x = 5π/6 + 2kπ, k ∈ ℤ. Inverse functions are defined with restricted domains to ensure single-valuedness: arcsin: [−1,1] → [−π/2, π/2], arccos: [−1,1] → [0, π], arctan: ℝ → (−π/2, π/2).

## Fourier And Analytic Extensions

Trigonometric functions form the basis of Fourier analysis, decomposing periodic functions into sums of sines and cosines with coefficients computed via integrals over a period. The Euler formula e^{iθ} = cos θ + i sin θ connects trigonometry with complex exponentials, enabling the expression of trigonometric identities and solutions to differential equations in a compact form. This complex analysis perspective leads to the generalization of trigonometric functions to complex arguments and the study of analytic continuations.

## Mechanisms

Trigonometry operates through the relationships between the sides and angles of triangles, particularly right-angled triangles. The core mechanisms involve the definitions of the sine, cosine, and tangent functions. The sine of an angle in a right triangle is defined as the ratio of the length of the side opposite the angle to the length of the hypotenuse. The cosine of an angle is defined as the ratio of the length of the side adjacent to the angle to the length of the hypotenuse. The tangent of an angle is defined as the ratio of the length of the side opposite the angle to the length of the side adjacent to the angle. These ratios are the foundation of trigonometric calculations.

The mechanism of solving trigonometric problems often involves using these ratios to find unknown sides or angles in a triangle. For instance, given the lengths of two sides of a right triangle, one can use the Pythagorean theorem to find the length of the third side. With the sides known, the sine, cosine, or tangent can be used to find the angles. Conversely, if an angle and the lengths of two sides are known, the remaining side and angles can be determined using trigonometric identities and formulas, such as the law of sines and the law of cosines for non-right triangles.

The causal chain in trigonometry is based on the geometric properties of triangles and the circular functions that define the relationships between angles and side lengths. Understanding and applying these mechanisms allow for the solution of a wide range of problems in mathematics, physics, engineering, and other fields where spatial relationships and periodic phenomena are involved.

## Methods And Frameworks

Trigonometry employs various methods and frameworks to solve problems. The Law of Sines is used to find unknown sides and angles in a triangle when two angles and a side, or two sides and an angle, are known. It is expressed as a/r = b/s = c/t, where a, b, and c are sides and r, s, and t are the corresponding opposite angles. The Law of Cosines is used to find the third side of a triangle when two sides and the included angle are known, given by c² = a² + b² - 2ab * cos(C). The Pythagorean identity, sin²(x) + cos²(x) = 1, is used to find missing trigonometric ratios in a right triangle. 
The sum and difference formulas for sine and cosine, sin(a ± b) = sin(a)cos(b) ± cos(a)sin(b) and cos(a ± b) = cos(a)cos(b) ± sin(a)sin(b), are used to simplify trigonometric expressions. 
Failure modes include using the Law of Sines with insufficient information, resulting in ambiguous solutions, and using the Law of Cosines without considering the range of the cosine function, leading to incorrect results. Additionally, incorrect application of the sum and difference formulas can lead to sign errors and incorrect simplification of expressions.

## Worked Examples

To illustrate the application of trigonometric principles, consider the following problems.

1. In a right-angled triangle, the length of the hypotenuse is 10 cm and one of the angles is 30 degrees. Find the length of the side opposite the 30-degree angle. 
Using the sine function, sin(30) = opposite side / hypotenuse, we can rearrange to find the length of the opposite side: opposite side = sin(30) * hypotenuse. Since sin(30) = 0.5, the length of the opposite side is 0.5 * 10 = 5 cm.

2. A surveyor measures the angle of elevation to the top of a building as 45 degrees and the distance from the building as 20 meters. Find the height of the building. 
Using the tangent function, tan(45) = height / distance, we can rearrange to find the height: height = tan(45) * distance. Since tan(45) = 1, the height of the building is 1 * 20 = 20 meters.

3. In a triangle, the length of two sides are given as 8 cm and 10 cm, and the included angle is 60 degrees. Find the length of the third side using the cosine rule. 
The cosine rule states that c^2 = a^2 + b^2 - 2ab * cos(C), where c is the length of the third side, a and b are the lengths of the given sides, and C is the included angle. Substituting the given values, c^2 = 8^2 + 10^2 - 2 * 8 * 10 * cos(60). Since cos(60) = 0.5, c^2 = 64 + 100 - 2 * 8 * 10 * 0.5 = 64 + 100 - 80 = 84, and c = sqrt(84) = sqrt(4 * 21) = 2 * sqrt(21) cm.

## Applications

Trigonometry has numerous applications in various fields, including physics, engineering, navigation, and surveying. In physics, trigonometry is used to describe the motion of objects in terms of displacement, velocity, and acceleration. The concepts of sine, cosine, and tangent are used to resolve vectors into their components, allowing for the calculation of quantities such as force, torque, and energy. For example, in the study of simple harmonic motion, trigonometric functions are used to model the motion of a pendulum or a spring-mass system. In engineering, trigonometry is used in the design of structures, such as bridges and buildings, to calculate stresses and loads. It is also used in the field of optics to calculate the angles of refraction and reflection. In navigation, trigonometry is used to determine the position of a ship or an aircraft, using the angles of elevation and azimuth to calculate distances and directions. Surveyors use trigonometry to calculate the heights of buildings and the distances between landmarks, using techniques such as triangulation and trilateration. Additionally, trigonometry is used in computer graphics to create 3D models and animations, and in medical imaging to reconstruct images of the body. The principles of trigonometry are also used in music theory to analyze the frequencies and harmonics of sound waves. Overall, trigonometry provides a powerful tool for solving problems that involve right triangles and periodic phenomena, and its applications continue to expand into new areas of science and technology.

## Common Errors

In trigonometry, several common errors arise from misunderstandings of fundamental concepts and incorrect application of formulas. One prevalent mistake is the incorrect use of the inverse trigonometric functions. For instance, many practitioners incorrectly assume that the range of the inverse sine function is (-∞, ∞), when in fact, its range is limited to [-π/2, π/2]. This error stems from a lack of understanding of the restricted domains of inverse trigonometric functions, which are designed to ensure a unique output for each input. 
Another error occurs when solving trigonometric equations, where practitioners may forget to check for extraneous solutions. This is particularly important when using identities or formulas that involve squaring or taking the square root of expressions, as these operations can introduce additional solutions that do not satisfy the original equation. 
Additionally, confusion often arises when applying the sum and difference formulas for sine and cosine. Many practitioners incorrectly apply these formulas by forgetting to switch the signs of the terms when moving from sum to difference or vice versa. This mistake can be avoided by carefully examining the formulas and recognizing the pattern of sign changes. 
Lastly, incorrect unit conversions and neglecting to consider the quadrant in which an angle lies are also common pitfalls. When working with trigonometric functions, it is essential to ensure that all angles are in the correct units (degrees or radians) and to consider the quadrant to determine the correct sign of the result. By being aware of these common errors and taking steps to avoid them, practitioners can improve their accuracy and proficiency in trigonometry.

## Advanced

The graduate-level extensions of trigonometry involve the application of trigonometric concepts to more complex and abstract mathematical structures. One such extension is the study of elliptic functions, which are functions defined on the complex plane that satisfy certain trigonometric-like properties. These functions are used to solve elliptic integrals, which arise in the study of elliptic curves and have numerous applications in number theory and algebraic geometry. Another area of research is the study of trigonometric functions on non-Euclidean spaces, such as hyperbolic space and spherical space, which has connections to differential geometry and topology. The field of trigonometry is also closely related to the study of representation theory, where trigonometric functions are used to construct representations of Lie groups and algebras. Open questions in the field include the development of new trigonometric identities and formulas, particularly in the context of higher-dimensional spaces and non-commutative geometry. Researchers are also exploring the application of trigonometric concepts to other areas of mathematics, such as quantum mechanics and signal processing. The use of computational methods and machine learning algorithms to solve trigonometric problems and optimize trigonometric functions is also an active area of research. Additionally, the study of the geometric and topological properties of trigonometric functions, such as their singularities and periodicity, is a rich area of ongoing research.

# Algebraic Geometry

> The course introduces core concepts and principles of algebraic geometry, assuming no prior study but requiring an understanding of abstract algebra and geometry.

## Foundations

Algebraic geometry is a branch of mathematics that combines techniques from abstract algebra and geometry to study geometric objects, such as curves, surfaces, and varieties. A fundamental concept is the ring, which is a set equipped with two binary operations, addition and multiplication, that satisfy certain properties, including distributivity, associativity, and commutativity. A field is a special type of ring where every non-zero element has a multiplicative inverse. The most common fields used in algebraic geometry are the real numbers (ℝ) and the complex numbers (ℂ).

An affine space is a set equipped with a free transitive action of a vector space, and an affine variety is the zero set of a set of polynomials in an affine space. Projective space is the set of lines through the origin in a vector space, and a projective variety is the zero set of a set of homogeneous polynomials in a projective space. Morphisms between varieties are maps that are locally defined by polynomials, and an isomorphism is a bijective morphism with a polynomial inverse. The dimension of a variety is the length of the longest chain of irreducible subvarieties.

A crucial concept is the scheme, which is a locally ringed space that generalizes varieties and provides a framework for studying geometric objects using algebraic tools. Schemes consist of a topological space together with a sheaf of rings, and they are used to define more general geometric objects, such as algebraic stacks. The vocabulary of algebraic geometry includes terms like smooth, singular, and birational, which describe properties of varieties and morphisms. Understanding these core definitions and principles is essential for practitioners of algebraic geometry.

## Common Errors

In algebraic geometry, practitioners often make mistakes when working with fundamental concepts. One common error is confusing the Zariski topology with the usual Euclidean topology. The Zariski topology is defined on the set of prime ideals of a ring, and its closed sets are the sets of prime ideals containing a given ideal. This topology is crucial for defining the geometry of algebraic varieties, but it does not behave like the Euclidean topology, where closed sets are defined as the complement of open sets. Another mistake is neglecting to check the characteristic of the field when working with algebraic curves or varieties. For example, the properties of elliptic curves differ significantly between characteristic 0 and positive characteristic. Additionally, practitioners may incorrectly assume that a morphism of varieties is an isomorphism if it is bijective, forgetting that it must also be an isomorphism of the underlying sheaves of rings. Furthermore, when computing cohomology groups, one must be careful to use the correct type of cohomology, such as étale cohomology or de Rham cohomology, depending on the context. These errors can lead to incorrect conclusions and highlight the importance of carefully applying the definitions and theorems of algebraic geometry.

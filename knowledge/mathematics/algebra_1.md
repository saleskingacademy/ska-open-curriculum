## Foundations

Algebra 1 is based on the concept of variables and constants. A variable is a symbol, often a letter, that represents a value that can change, whereas a constant is a symbol that represents a fixed value. The most common variables are x, y, and z, while constants are often represented by numbers. An expression is a combination of variables, constants, and mathematical operations, such as addition, subtraction, multiplication, and division. For example, 2x + 3 is an expression where 2 is a coefficient, x is a variable, and 3 is a constant. 
A equation is a statement that expresses the equality of two expressions, such as 2x + 3 = 5. The goal of solving an equation is to isolate the variable, which means to get the variable by itself on one side of the equation. This is achieved through inverse operations, which are operations that undo each other, such as addition and subtraction, or multiplication and division. 
The order of operations is a set of rules that dictates the order in which operations should be performed when there are multiple operations in an expression. The acronym PEMDAS is often used to remember the order: Parentheses, Exponents, Multiplication and Division, and Addition and Subtraction. 
Algebraic properties, such as the commutative, associative, and distributive properties, are essential in manipulating and simplifying expressions. The commutative property states that the order of the numbers being added or multiplied does not change the result, while the associative property states that the order in which numbers are grouped when adding or multiplying does not change the result. The distributive property states that a single operation can be applied to multiple numbers, such as multiplying a number by a sum or difference. 
Understanding these core definitions, first principles, and vocabulary is crucial for a practitioner to successfully work with algebraic expressions and equations.

## Methods And Frameworks

In Algebra 1, various methods and frameworks are employed to solve equations and manipulate expressions. The Substitution Method is used to solve systems of linear equations by substituting one equation into another, useful when one equation is easily solvable for one variable. The Elimination Method is applied when the coefficients of one variable in two equations are additive inverses, allowing for elimination of that variable. The Graphing Method involves plotting equations on a coordinate plane to visualize the solution, useful for illustrating relationships between variables. The Quadratic Formula, x = (-b ± √(b² - 4ac)) / 2a, is used to solve quadratic equations of the form ax² + bx + c = 0, when factoring is not straightforward. Failure modes include attempting to use the Substitution Method with complex equations, using the Elimination Method with non-additive inverse coefficients, and applying the Quadratic Formula to non-quadratic equations. Additionally, the Slope-Intercept Form, y = mx + b, is a framework for linear equations, where m represents the slope and b represents the y-intercept, useful for modeling linear relationships. The method of Factoring is used to solve quadratic equations by expressing them as a product of two binomials, useful when the equation can be easily factored. Each method and framework has its specific application and limitations, and understanding these is crucial for effective problem-solving in Algebra 1.

## Worked Examples

To illustrate the application of algebraic concepts, consider the following problems.

1. Solve for x: 2x + 5 = 11. 
First, subtract 5 from both sides: 2x = 11 - 5, so 2x = 6. 
Then, divide both sides by 2: x = 6 / 2, so x = 3.

2. Find the value of y: y - 3 = 7. 
Add 3 to both sides: y = 7 + 3, so y = 10.

3. Solve for z: z / 4 = 9. 
Multiply both sides by 4: z = 9 * 4, so z = 36. 
These examples demonstrate the use of inverse operations to isolate variables and solve linear equations.

## Common Errors

In Algebra 1, common errors often arise from misunderstandings of fundamental concepts and operations. One prevalent mistake is the incorrect application of the order of operations, often resulting in errors when simplifying expressions with multiple operations. For instance, failing to recognize that multiplication and division should be performed before addition and subtraction can lead to incorrect simplifications. Another error is the misuse of properties of equality, such as adding or multiplying both sides of an equation by a value that is not valid for all possible solutions, which can lead to the loss of solutions or introduction of extraneous ones. Additionally, students often struggle with the concept of negative numbers, incorrectly assuming that two negative signs make a positive in all contexts, which is not true when dealing with subtraction or the multiplication of negative numbers by negative numbers in expressions involving more than one operation. The incorrect handling of absolute value equations and inequalities is also a common mistake, where students may forget to consider both the positive and negative case when solving equations involving absolute values. These errors highlight the importance of carefully applying algebraic principles and checking work to ensure accuracy and validity of solutions.

# Numerical Analysis

> The course teaches core principles and mechanisms of numerical analysis, assuming some basic mathematical knowledge.

## Foundations

Numerical analysis is a field of study that involves the development and application of numerical methods to solve mathematical problems. A **mathematical problem** is a statement that poses a question or describes a situation, often involving **variables**, which are quantities that can take on different values, and **constants**, which are quantities that remain unchanged. **Algorithms**, which are step-by-step procedures for solving a problem, are a crucial component of numerical analysis. These algorithms often rely on **approximations**, which are estimates or close representations of exact values. The **accuracy** of an approximation refers to how close it is to the true value, while **precision** refers to the level of detail or exactness of the approximation. **Round-off error** occurs when a numerical value is approximated by a nearby value, often due to limitations in computer arithmetic. **Truncation error**, on the other hand, occurs when an infinite process, such as a series, is approximated by a finite process. Understanding these fundamental concepts, including **convergence**, which refers to the ability of a sequence of approximations to approach a limit, and **stability**, which refers to the ability of an algorithm to produce accurate results despite small changes in input, is essential for a practitioner of numerical analysis.

## Mechanisms

Numerical analysis relies on various mechanisms to approximate mathematical solutions. The process begins with problem formulation, where a mathematical model is constructed to describe the problem. This model is then discretized, which involves approximating the continuous problem with a finite set of equations. The discretization process can be achieved through methods such as the finite difference method, finite element method, or spectral method. Once discretized, the resulting system of equations is solved using numerical methods, including direct methods like Gaussian elimination or iterative methods like the conjugate gradient method. The choice of numerical method depends on the size and complexity of the system, as well as the desired level of accuracy. The solution is then refined through techniques such as interpolation, extrapolation, or defect correction to improve accuracy. Error analysis is also crucial, as it involves estimating the difference between the exact and approximate solutions, allowing for the assessment of the numerical method's performance. Throughout this process, the interplay between the mathematical model, discretization, numerical method, and error analysis forms the causal chain that underlies numerical analysis.

Throughout these mechanisms, the causal chain is evident: the mathematical model dictates the choice of discretization and numerical method, which in turn influences the iteration process and error analysis. The computational implementation then affects how these processes are executed, ultimately leading to the approximate solution of the original mathematical problem.

## Methods And Frameworks

Numerical analysis employs various methods and frameworks to approximate solutions to mathematical problems. The Bisection Method is used to find roots of a function, suitable when the function is continuous and changes sign over a given interval. The Newton-Raphson Method is an iterative technique for finding roots, effective when the initial guess is close to the root and the function is differentiable. The Euler Method and Runge-Kutta Methods are used for solving ordinary differential equations, with the latter being more accurate but computationally expensive. The Gaussian Elimination and LU Decomposition are methods for solving systems of linear equations, with the latter being more efficient for large systems. The Monte Carlo Method is used for approximating solutions to problems involving randomness and uncertainty. Failure modes include division by zero, non-convergence, and accumulation of round-off errors. The choice of method depends on the problem's characteristics, such as the presence of non-linearity, the need for high accuracy, and computational constraints. Understanding the strengths and limitations of each method is crucial for effective numerical analysis.

## Worked Examples

To illustrate the application of numerical analysis techniques, consider the following problems.

1. **Bisection Method**: Find the root of the equation f(x) = x^2 - 2 = 0 using the bisection method, given the initial interval [1, 2]. 
The bisection method requires the function to have opposite signs at the endpoints of the interval. Here, f(1) = -1 and f(2) = 2, satisfying this condition. 
The midpoint of the interval is x = (1 + 2)/2 = 1.5. Evaluating f(1.5) = (1.5)^2 - 2 = 0.25, which is positive. 
Since f(1) and f(1.5) have opposite signs, the root lies in the interval [1, 1.5]. Repeating the process, the midpoint of [1, 1.5] is x = (1 + 1.5)/2 = 1.25. 
Evaluating f(1.25) = (1.25)^2 - 2 = -0.4375, which is negative. Thus, the root lies in the interval [1.25, 1.5].

2. **Trapezoidal Rule**: Approximate the integral of f(x) = x^2 from x = 0 to x = 2 using the trapezoidal rule with n = 4 subintervals. 
The width of each subinterval is h = (2 - 0)/4 = 0.5. The function values at the subinterval points are f(0) = 0, f(0.5) = 0.25, f(1) = 1, f(1.5) = 2.25, and f(2) = 4. 
The trapezoidal rule formula is: (h/2)(f(x0) + 2f(x1) + 2f(x2) + 2f(x3) + f(x4)). 
Substituting the values, we get: (0.5/2)(0 + 2*0.25 + 2*1 + 2*2.25 + 4) = (0.25)(0 + 0.5 + 2 + 4.5 + 4) = (0.25)(11) = 2.75.

3. **Newton-Raphson Method**: Find the root of the equation f(x) = x^3 - 2x - 5 = 0 using the Newton-Raphson method, given an initial guess x0 = 2. 
The Newton-Raphson formula is: x(n+1) = x(n) - f(x(n))/f'(x(n)). 
Here, f'(x) = 3x^2 - 2. Evaluating f(2) = (2)^3 - 2(2) - 5 = 8 - 4 - 5 = -1 and f'(2) = 3(2)^2 - 2 = 12 - 2 = 10. 
Thus, x1 = 2 - (-1)/10 = 2 + 0.1 = 2.1. Repeating the process, f(2.1) = (2.1)^3 - 2(2.1) - 5 = 9.261 - 4.2 - 5 = -0.039 and f'(2.1) = 3(2.1)^2 - 2 = 13.23 - 2 = 11.23. 
So, x2 = 2.1 - (-0.039)/11.23 = 2.1 + 0.0035 = 2.1035.

## Common Errors

Numerical analysis, a branch of mathematics focused on the development and analysis of algorithms for solving mathematical problems numerically, is not immune to common errors that can significantly impact the accuracy and reliability of the results. One of the primary mistakes practitioners make is the improper handling of round-off errors. Round-off errors occur when a numerical algorithm is implemented using finite precision arithmetic, leading to approximations that can accumulate and affect the final result. Ignoring or underestimating these errors can lead to incorrect conclusions, especially in iterative processes or when dealing with ill-conditioned problems.

Another common error is the misuse of numerical methods for solving differential equations, such as the Euler method or Runge-Kutta methods, without considering the stability and convergence properties of these methods. For instance, applying an explicit method to a stiff differential equation can result in unstable solutions, while an implicit method might be more appropriate.

Furthermore, the choice of numerical method without considering the conditioning of the problem can lead to significant errors. For example, using the Gaussian elimination method without pivoting for solving systems of linear equations can result in large round-off errors if the system is ill-conditioned.

Additionally, not validating the results of numerical computations through comparison with known solutions, when available, or through the use of different numerical methods, can lead to undetected errors. This is particularly important in complex simulations where analytical solutions are not available for verification.

Lastly, neglecting to consider the limitations and assumptions underlying numerical algorithms can lead to their misuse. For example, assuming that a numerical method is universally applicable without considering its limitations in terms of problem size, complexity, or specific characteristics of the problem at hand can result in incorrect or unreliable results.

Understanding and avoiding these common errors are crucial for the effective application of numerical analysis techniques in solving mathematical problems.

Furthermore, the incorrect application of convergence criteria for iterative methods is a frequent mistake. Understanding the convergence properties of algorithms and properly implementing stopping criteria are crucial for obtaining reliable numerical results.

Lastly, ignoring the conditioning of a problem is a critical error. The condition number of a problem, which measures how sensitive the solution is to small changes in the input data, is often overlooked. For ill-conditioned problems, small errors in the data can result in large errors in the solution, making it essential to use appropriate numerical methods that are robust against such sensitivities. Recognizing the potential for conditioning issues and selecting algorithms accordingly is vital for the accurate numerical solution of mathematical problems.

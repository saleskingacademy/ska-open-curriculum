# Derived Algebraic Geometry

> The course assumes advanced knowledge of algebraic geometry, homotopy theory, and model categories.

## Foundations

Derived algebraic geometry is founded upon the concept of a **derived scheme**, which is a pair consisting of a topological space and a **derived ringed space**, comprising a sheaf of **commutative differential graded algebras (CDGAs)**. A CDGA is a **graded algebra** equipped with a **differential**, satisfying the **Leibniz rule**. The **homotopy theory of CDGAs** provides the framework for **derived geometric** constructions, where **weak equivalences** are quasi-isomorphisms, and **homotopy limits** and **colimits** are used to construct **derived fiber products** and **derived intersections**. The **model category structure** on CDGAs, introduced by **Quillen**, enables the use of **homotopical algebra** to study **derived moduli spaces**, which are **functors** from the category of CDGAs to the category of **simplicial sets**, representing **moduli problems** in derived algebraic geometry. Key vocabulary includes **affine derived schemes**, **derived affine schemes**, and **etale morphisms**, which are defined in terms of **etale maps** of CDGAs, satisfying the **lifting property**.

Derived algebraic geometry is a branch of mathematics that combines techniques from algebraic geometry and homotopy theory to study geometric objects in a more refined and flexible way. The core definition of derived algebraic geometry revolves around the concept of a **derived stack**, which is a geometric object that encodes information about the deformations and degenerations of algebraic varieties. A **stack** is a functor from the category of schemes to the category of groupoids, which assigns to each scheme a groupoid of objects and morphisms between them. In the context of derived algebraic geometry, a **scheme** is a locally ringed space that is locally isomorphic to an affine scheme, where an **affine scheme** is the spectrum of a commutative ring. The **spectrum** of a commutative ring is the set of prime ideals of the ring, equipped with a topology and a sheaf of rings. A **commutative ring** is a ring in which the multiplication operation is commutative, meaning that the order of the factors does not change the result. The **category of schemes** is the category whose objects are schemes and whose morphisms are morphisms of schemes, which are continuous maps that preserve the ring structure. A **functor** is a map between categories that preserves the composition of morphisms. The **category of groupoids** is the category whose objects are groupoids and whose morphisms are functors between groupoids, where a **groupoid** is a category in which every morphism is invertible. The **derived** aspect of derived algebraic geometry refers to the use of **homotopical algebra**, which is a branch of mathematics that studies the properties of algebraic structures up to homotopy equivalence, rather than up to isomorphism. This allows for a more flexible and refined study of geometric objects, and is based on the concept of a **model category**, which is a category equipped with a notion of weak equivalence and fibration, that satisfies certain axioms. The core definitions and first principles of this field rely on a deep understanding of several key concepts.

A **scheme** is a locally ringed space that is locally isomorphic to an affine scheme, which is the spectrum of a commutative ring. Schemes serve as the fundamental geometric objects in algebraic geometry.

A **derived scheme** or **derived stack** is an enhancement of the classical notion of a scheme, where the structure sheaf is replaced by a sheaf of differential graded algebras (DGAs) or E-infinity algebras. This allows for the incorporation of homotopical information and the study of derived geometric objects.

The **cotangent complex** is a fundamental invariant of a scheme or derived scheme, which encodes its infinitesimal deformations. It is defined as a quasi-coherent sheaf on the scheme, and its properties are crucial in understanding the geometry and topology of the underlying space.

The **tangent complex** is the dual of the cotangent complex and plays a key role in the study of deformations and obstruction theory.

A **perfect complex** is a complex of sheaves that is locally quasi-isomorphic to a bounded complex of finite free modules. Perfect complexes are essential in derived algebraic geometry, as they provide a framework for studying the homotopy theory of schemes and derived schemes.

The **derived category** of a scheme or derived scheme is a triangulated category that encodes the homotopy theory of complexes of sheaves on the space. It is a fundamental tool in derived algebraic geometry, allowing for the study of geometric objects and their deformations in a homotopy-invariant way.

Understanding these core definitions and first principles is essential for practitioners of derived algebraic geometry, as they provide the foundation for the field and are used to study a wide range of geometric and homotopical phenomena.

The concept of derived stacks, in particular, enables the construction of moduli spaces for objects that are not necessarily representable by classical algebraic stacks. This has significant applications in the study of mirror symmetry, where derived algebraic geometry is used to establish equivalences between categories of coherent sheaves on Calabi-Yau manifolds.

In number theory, derived algebraic geometry has connections to the study of motivic Galois groups and the arithmetic of algebraic curves. The use of derived algebraic geometry in this context allows for a more refined understanding of the algebraic and geometric structures underlying number theoretic phenomena. 
The applications of derived algebraic geometry are diverse and continue to expand, with ongoing research exploring its connections to other areas of mathematics and physics, such as representation theory, non-commutative geometry, and the geometric Langlands program. ## Common Errors

In Derived Algebraic Geometry, several common mistakes arise from misunderstandings of the underlying concepts. One such error is the failure to distinguish between the ordinary and derived perspectives on algebraic geometry. Practitioners may incorrectly apply classical geometric intuition to derived geometric objects, such as derived schemes and stacks, which can lead to incorrect conclusions about their properties and behavior. For instance, the derived intersection of two subschemes may not be what one expects from classical algebraic geometry, as it involves not just the intersection itself but also the higher homotopical information. Another mistake is neglecting the importance of simplicial commutative rings and their role in defining the derived structure sheaf of a scheme. This neglect can result in incorrect calculations of derived invariants, such as the derived cohomology and K-theory of algebraic varieties. Furthermore, confusion can arise from the improper use of model categories and their associated homotopy theories, which are fundamental to the development of derived algebraic geometry. Specifically, the distinction between weak equivalences and fibrations in a model category must be carefully observed to avoid incorrect identifications of derived geometric objects. Lastly, the error of not considering the size issues related to the universe of sets used in the foundations of derived algebraic geometry can lead to inconsistencies and paradoxes, highlighting the need for a careful handling of categorical and homotopical constructions within a well-defined set-theoretic framework. Practitioners may incorrectly apply classical geometric intuition to derived geometric constructions, neglecting the derived category's homotopical nature. This oversight can lead to incorrect computations of cohomology groups or misunderstandings of the behavior of quasi-coherent sheaves in the derived setting.

Another mistake is the improper handling of simplicial commutative rings and their relationship to derived schemes. The derived affine scheme, for instance, is not simply a set of points but rather an object that encodes higher homotopical information. Misinterpreting this can result in errors when working with derived geometric stacks and their descent properties.

Furthermore, confusion often arises regarding the difference between the cotangent complex and the ordinary tangent space. The cotangent complex is a more refined invariant that captures not just the first-order information (as in classical geometry) but also higher-order deformations. Incorrectly treating it as if it were the classical cotangent bundle can lead to mistakes in calculating obstruction theories for moduli problems.

Lastly, a common error involves neglecting the nuances of ∞-categories, particularly in the context of functoriality and homotopy limits. Derived algebraic geometry heavily relies on ∞-categorical machinery, and a lack of precision in handling these structures can lead to incorrect conclusions about the existence and properties of derived geometric objects. Understanding and correctly applying the principles of ∞-category theory is crucial for avoiding these pitfalls.

The integral, denoted as ∫f(x)dx, is a measure of the accumulation of a quantity over a defined interval. The definite integral, denoted as ∫[a,b] f(x)dx, is a measure of the accumulation of a quantity over a defined interval [a,b].

Key concepts in Calculus 2 include the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus, which relates the derivative and integral of a function, and the Mean Value Theorem, which states that a continuous function on a closed interval must attain its maximum and minimum values.

The mechanism of integration works by first defining a function f(x) and an interval [a, b] over which the area is to be calculated. The interval is then divided into n subintervals, each of width Δx, and the function value at a point within each subinterval is used to calculate the area of the corresponding rectangle. The sum of these areas is then taken as the approximation of the area under the curve. As the number of subintervals increases, the approximation becomes more accurate, and in the limit as n approaches infinity, the sum approaches the definite integral of f(x) from a to b, denoted as ∫[a, b] f(x) dx. This mechanism allows for the calculation of areas, volumes, and other quantities, and is the foundation for more advanced techniques in calculus, such as substitution, integration by parts, and integration by partial fractions.

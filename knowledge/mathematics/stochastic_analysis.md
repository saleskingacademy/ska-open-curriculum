## Foundations

Stochastic analysis is based on probability theory, which involves the study of random events and their likelihood of occurrence. The **expected value** of a random variable is a measure of its central tendency, calculated as the sum of each possible value multiplied by its probability.

A **stochastic process** is a collection of random variables, where each variable represents a random event at a particular point in time or space. Stochastic processes can be **discrete** or **continuous**, depending on whether the random variables take on discrete or continuous values. A **filtration** is a sequence of increasing sigma-algebras, which represents the information available at each point in time.

The **sigma-algebra** is a mathematical structure used to define probability measures, consisting of a collection of sets that are closed under countable unions and complementation. A **probability measure** is a function that assigns a non-negative real number to each set in the sigma-algebra, representing the probability of the set. The **sample space** is the set of all possible outcomes of a random experiment, and an **event** is a subset of the sample space.

The **Borel sigma-algebra** is the smallest sigma-algebra containing all open sets in a topological space, and is used to define probability measures on continuous spaces. A **martingale** is a stochastic process whose expected value at any future time is equal to its current value, given the current information. These concepts form the foundation of stochastic analysis, and are used to model and analyze random phenomena in various fields.

## Methods And Frameworks

Stochastic analysis employs various methods and frameworks to model and analyze random phenomena. The Martingale method is used to study stochastic processes with fair games, where the expected value of the process at any future time is equal to its current value. The Itô calculus framework is applied to stochastic differential equations, providing a way to compute derivatives and integrals of stochastic processes. The Feynman-Kac formula is utilized to solve partial differential equations (PDEs) with stochastic components, relating the solution of a PDE to the expectation of a stochastic process. The Girsanov theorem is used to change the measure of a stochastic process, allowing for the transformation of one stochastic process into another. The failure mode of these methods often arises from incorrect application of assumptions, such as neglecting non-stationarity or non-Markovian behavior in the underlying process. The Euler-Maruyama method is a numerical technique used to approximate the solution of stochastic differential equations, but it may fail to converge for certain types of equations or initial conditions. The stochastic Taylor expansion is a method for approximating stochastic processes, but its failure mode can occur when the expansion is truncated too early, leading to inaccurate results.

Using the properties of Brownian motion, we know that $B_t$ is normally distributed with mean $\mu t$ and variance $\sigma^2 t$. Hence, $B_1$ is normally distributed with mean $0.5$ and variance $1.2^2 = 1.44$. The probability that $B_1 > 1$ can be calculated using the standard normal distribution: $P(B_1 > 1) = P\left(\frac{B_1 - 0.5}{\sqrt{1.44}} > \frac{1 - 0.5}{\sqrt{1.44}}\right) = P(Z > 0.416)$, where $Z$ is the standard normal variable.

2. **Poisson Process**: A Poisson process $N_t$ has a rate parameter $\lambda = 2.5$. Find the probability that $N_2 = 3$, i.e., exactly 3 events occur in the time interval $[0, 2]$. The probability mass function for a Poisson process is given by $P(N_t = k) = \frac{(\lambda t)^k e^{-\lambda t}}{k!}$. Substituting the given values, we get $P(N_2 = 3) = \frac{(2.5 \cdot 2)^3 e^{-2.5 \cdot 2}}{3!} = \frac{5^3 e^{-5}}{3!}$.

The expected value of $X_1$ is $E[X_1] = X_0 e^{(\mu - \frac{\sigma^2}{2}) \cdot 1} = 10 e^{(0.1 - \frac{0.2^2}{2}) \cdot 1}$.

## Common Errors

In stochastic analysis, common mistakes often arise from misunderstandings of the underlying mathematical concepts. One prevalent error is the confusion between the notions of almost sure convergence and convergence in probability. Almost sure convergence implies that the sequence of random variables converges pointwise to a limit, except on a set of measure zero, whereas convergence in probability only requires that the probability of the sequence not converging to the limit decreases to zero. Practitioners may incorrectly assume that convergence in probability implies almost sure convergence, leading to incorrect conclusions about the behavior of stochastic processes. 
Another mistake is the incorrect application of the Ito's lemma, which is a fundamental tool in stochastic calculus. Ito's lemma provides a formula for the differential of a function of a stochastic process, but it requires that the function be twice differentiable and that the stochastic process be a semimartingale. If these conditions are not met, the lemma cannot be applied, and incorrect results may be obtained. 
Additionally, the mistake of neglecting the distinction between a local martingale and a martingale can lead to errors. A local martingale is a stochastic process that is a martingale when stopped at a sequence of increasing stopping times, whereas a martingale is a process that satisfies the martingale property at all times. Failing to recognize that a local martingale is not necessarily a martingale can result in incorrect calculations and conclusions. 
These errors often stem from a lack of understanding of the subtle differences between various stochastic concepts and the failure to carefully verify the conditions required for the application of stochastic analysis techniques.

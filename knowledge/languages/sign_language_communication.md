# Sign Language Communication

> The course assumes prior knowledge of linguistics and communication principles, and delves into specialized topics such as Deaf culture and sign language specifics.

## Not Universal

300+ distinct sign languages worldwide. ASL (American Sign Language), BSL (British Sign Language — NOT mutually intelligible with ASL), LSF (French Sign Language — ASL's ancestor), DGS (German), JSL (Japanese), etc. LINGUISTICS: Full natural languages with grammar, syntax, morphology, phonology (handshape, location, movement, orientation, non-manual markers). NOT signed English. ASL grammar: topic-comment structure, spatial grammar, classifier predicates. William Stokoe (1960) — proved ASL is a true language. DEAF CULTURE: Capital-D "Deaf" = cultural identity, not disability. Deaf gain (not hearing loss). Rich artistic tradition — Deaf poetry, theater (National Theatre of the Deaf), visual art, storytelling. Residential schools as cultural centers. Gallaudet University (world's only university for Deaf students). HISTORY: Milan Conference (1880) — oralism imposed, sign languages banned in schools worldwide for ~100 years. Devastating impact on Deaf education and culture. Gradually reversed late 20th century. COCHLEAR IMPLANTS: Electronic device providing sense of sound. Controversial within Deaf community — seen by some as cultural erasure. Medical vs cultural model of deafness. Personal choice. INTERPRETING: ASL-English interpreters (RID certification). Video Relay Service (VRS). Communication Access Realtime Translation (CART). Legal requirements (ADA). Haptic communication (DeafBlind — tactile sign, Pro-Tactile movement). BABY SIGN: Hearing infants can learn signs before speech (motor control develops earlier). Reduces frustration, may boost language development. APPLIED: 70M Deaf people worldwide. Accessibility compliance. Inclusive design benefits everyone.

## Foundations

In the context of languages, Sign Language Communication refers to the use of visual-gestural languages, such as American Sign Language (ASL) or British Sign Language (BSL), to convey meaning and facilitate communication between individuals. A **sign** is a manual or non-manual signal, including handshapes, orientations, locations, and movements, that conveys a specific meaning. **Manual signs** are made with the hands, while **non-manual signs** involve facial expressions, head movements, and body language. **Iconicity** refers to the degree to which a sign resembles its referent, with **iconic signs** bearing a visual resemblance to the concept they represent. **Sign language phonology** is the study of the soundless, visual-gestural units of sign language, including **handshapes**, **orientations**, **locations**, and **movements**. A **sign language lexicon** is the collection of signs that comprise a sign language, with each sign having a specific **meaning** and **usage**. **Morphology** in sign language refers to the study of how signs are formed and combined to convey meaning, including **compounding**, where two or more signs are combined to create a new sign. **Syntax** in sign language refers to the rules governing the combination of signs to form sentences, including **word order** and **non-manual markers**. Understanding these core concepts is essential for effective Sign Language Communication.

## Mechanisms

Sign language communication involves a complex interplay of physical and cognitive processes. The process begins with the signer's intention to convey a message, which is then encoded into a visual-gestural representation. This encoding involves the retrieval of linguistic information from the signer's mental lexicon, including handshapes, orientations, locations, and movements. The signer's brain then sends signals to the relevant motor neurons, which control the muscles of the hands, arms, and face to produce the desired signs. As the signer articulates the signs, the addressee perceives them through visual attention, processing the manual and non-manual cues, such as facial expressions and body language. The addressee's brain then decodes the visual information, using their own knowledge of the sign language's grammar, vocabulary, and pragmatics to interpret the message. This decoding process involves the activation of the addressee's mental lexicon, as well as the application of contextual information and inference. The addressee may also respond with their own signs, creating a dialogue that involves the reciprocal exchange of visual-gestural information. Throughout this process, both the signer and the addressee rely on their shared knowledge of the sign language and its conventions to ensure effective communication. The causal chain of sign language communication can be summarized as: intention → encoding → articulation → perception → decoding → interpretation → response.

## Methods And Frameworks

In sign language communication, several methods and frameworks facilitate effective interaction. The Rochester Method emphasizes the importance of visual-gestural communication, using a combination of signs, facial expressions, and body language to convey meaning. This approach is useful in one-on-one interactions or small group settings, but may be less effective in large groups or noisy environments, where visual cues can be easily missed. 
The Linguistics of Visual-Gestural (LVG) model analyzes sign language as a linguistic system, breaking down signs into phonological, morphological, and syntactic components. This framework is useful for understanding the structural properties of sign languages, but may not account for the nuances of individual signers' styles or regional variations. 
The Conceptual Metaphor (CM) framework explores how sign languages convey abstract concepts through metaphorical extensions of concrete signs. This approach is useful for understanding how signers create and negotiate meaning, but may be limited by its reliance on pre-existing linguistic and cultural knowledge. 
The Sign Language Interpreting (SLI) model provides a framework for interpreters to analyze and render sign language messages into spoken language, taking into account factors such as context, register, and cultural references. This approach is useful for facilitating communication between signers and non-signers, but may be vulnerable to errors or misinterpretations if the interpreter lacks sufficient training or cultural competence.

The Bill Stokoe's Notation System is used to transcribe and analyze sign languages, breaking down signs into handshape, orientation, location, movement, and non-manual markers. This method is useful for linguistic research and sign language documentation, but its failure mode lies in its complexity, making it challenging for non-experts to master. 
The Signed Language Discourse Analysis (SLDA) framework examines the structure and organization of sign language narratives, focusing on cohesion, coherence, and turn-taking. This framework is applied when investigating sign language storytelling and conversation, but its limitation is that it may not account for cultural variations in sign language use. 
The Cognitive Linguistics approach views sign language as a cognitive process, emphasizing the role of conceptual metaphors, metonymy, and mental spaces in shaping sign language structure and use. This approach is useful for understanding the relationship between sign language and thought, but its failure mode is that it may overlook the social and cultural context of sign language communication. 
The Sociolinguistic framework considers the social factors influencing sign language variation, such as regional dialects, age, and social status. This framework is applied when studying sign language in its social context, but its limitation is that it may not fully capture the complexities of sign language use in diverse communities. 
The Lexical Functional Grammar (LFG) model describes the grammatical structure of sign languages, focusing on the relationship between lexical items and grammatical functions. This model is useful for analyzing sign language syntax, but its failure mode lies in its inability to account for the iconic and gestural aspects of sign language.

## Applications

## Common Errors

In sign language communication, common errors often stem from a lack of understanding of the linguistic structure and cultural nuances of the language. One prevalent mistake is the incorrect use of non-manual markers (NMMs), such as facial expressions, head movements, and body language, which are essential for conveying grammatical information and emotional tone. For instance, using a neutral face while signing a question can change the intended meaning, as sign languages often rely on raised eyebrows or tilted heads to indicate interrogative sentences. Another error is the misapplication of finger spelling, where signers may incorrectly spell out words or use finger spelling in place of signs, disrupting the natural flow of the language. Additionally, practitioners may overlook the importance of spatial relationships and orientation in sign language, leading to confusion in storytelling and dialogue. Furthermore, the adoption of a dominant hand for signing without considering the recipient's handedness can also lead to errors, as some signs require specific hand dominance to convey the correct meaning. These mistakes highlight the need for comprehensive training and practice in sign language communication to ensure effective and respectful interaction with the Deaf and hard of hearing community.

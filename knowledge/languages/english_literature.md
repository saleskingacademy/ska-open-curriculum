Discourse analysis is also a crucial mechanism, as it examines how texts are structured and how they create meaning through cohesion, coherence, and genre. By applying these mechanisms, students can critically evaluate literary texts, identify the author's intentions, and develop a deeper understanding of the language and its role in literature.

## Methods And Frameworks

In the study of English Literature as a language subject, several methods and frameworks are employed to analyze and interpret literary texts. The Stylistics method, for instance, is used to examine the linguistic features of a text, such as syntax, semantics, and pragmatics, to understand the author's style and its effect on the reader. This method is particularly useful when analyzing poetry or prose with complex linguistic structures. However, its failure mode lies in overemphasizing the linguistic aspects, potentially neglecting the historical, cultural, or social contexts of the text.

The Discourse Analysis framework is utilized to study the relationships between language, power, and ideology in literary texts. This framework is effective when examining texts that reflect social issues, such as inequality or injustice. Nevertheless, its failure mode occurs when the analysis becomes too focused on the power dynamics, overlooking the literary merit or aesthetic value of the text.

The Systemic Functional Linguistics (SFL) model, developed by Michael Halliday, is applied to investigate the relationship between language and context. SFL is useful when analyzing texts that exhibit complex relationships between language, culture, and society. However, its failure mode arises when the model is applied too rigidly, failing to account for the nuances and complexities of human communication.

The Critical Discourse Analysis (CDA) framework, inspired by the work of Norman Fairclough, is employed to examine the ways in which language is used to shape and reflect social relationships and power structures. CDA is particularly effective when analyzing texts that reflect dominant ideologies or social injustices. Nevertheless, its failure mode occurs when the analysis becomes too biased towards a particular ideology, neglecting the complexity and multiplicity of human experiences.

## Common Errors

In the study of English Literature as a language subject, several common errors occur due to misunderstandings of linguistic and literary concepts. One prevalent mistake is the confusion between the terms "Old English" and "Middle English", with some practitioners incorrectly assuming they refer to the same period or style of English. However, Old English (c. 450-1100 AD) is characterized by highly inflected grammar and a distinct vocabulary, whereas Middle English (c. 1100-1500 AD) is marked by a simplification of grammar and the influence of French and Latin on the English language. Another error is the misattribution of literary devices, such as metaphor and simile, which are often used interchangeably despite their distinct definitions. A metaphor is a comparison between two unlike things without "like" or "as," whereas a simile uses these words to make a comparison. Furthermore, the analysis of literary texts often suffers from a lack of attention to historical and cultural context, leading to anachronistic interpretations that neglect the social, political, and cultural conditions in which the texts were written. Additionally, the overreliance on translation software and lack of proficiency in close reading skills can result in misinterpretations of literary works, highlighting the importance of linguistic competence and critical thinking in the study of English Literature.

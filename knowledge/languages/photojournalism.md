# Photojournalism

> The course teaches applied practice of photojournalism, including techniques, equipment, and editing skills.

## Ethics

Accuracy, fairness, independence. No staging, no manipulation (NPPA code). Consent issues, photographing minors, vulnerable subjects. TECHNIQUE: Decisive moment (Cartier-Bresson), storytelling through sequences, establishing/medium/close, peak action, reaction shots. EQUIPMENT: Fast lenses (f/2.8 or wider), available light skills, flash journalism. Camera settings for speed (shutter priority, auto-ISO). EDITING: Selection (editing eye), basic processing (crop, exposure, white balance), ethical boundaries (no content manipulation, minor adjustments only). RAW workflow. DISTRIBUTION: Wire services (AP, Reuters, AFP), editorial submissions, photo essays. Metadata (IPTC), captioning, photo credits. GENRES: News, feature, sports, documentary, conflict/war photography. SAFETY: Hostile environment training, protective equipment, PTSD awareness.

## Foundations

In media communications, photojournalism refers to the practice of using photographs to tell news stories and convey information to the public. A photojournalist is a professional who captures and edits photographs to communicate news, events, and social issues. The core definition of photojournalism involves the use of images to document reality, with the goal of informing, educating, and engaging audiences. Key principles include objectivity, accuracy, and fairness. Vocabulary essential to the practitioner includes terms such as caption (a brief description accompanying a photograph), byline (the photojournalist's name accompanying their work), and credit line (acknowledging the source of a photograph). Other fundamental concepts include the rule of thirds (a compositional principle dividing an image into thirds both horizontally and vertically), leading lines (visual elements guiding the viewer's eye), and framing (using elements within the scene to create a border around the subject). Understanding these foundations is crucial for effective visual storytelling in media communications.

## Mechanisms

In media communications, photojournalism operates through a series of mechanisms that facilitate the creation, dissemination, and consumption of photographic news content. The process begins with news gathering, where photojournalists identify and capture newsworthy events or stories through photographs. These images are then edited and selected based on their news value, relevance, and visual impact. The selected photographs are subsequently transmitted to news organizations or media outlets, where they are further edited, captioned, and contextualized with accompanying text or other multimedia elements. The final product is then published or broadcast through various media channels, such as newspapers, magazines, online news websites, or social media platforms. The causal chain is as follows: the occurrence of a newsworthy event triggers the photojournalist's capture of the event, which in turn leads to the editing and selection of images, followed by transmission, publication, and ultimately, consumption by the audience. Throughout this process, photojournalism relies on technological mechanisms, such as camera equipment and digital editing software, as well as social mechanisms, including journalistic ethics and professional standards, to ensure the accuracy, authenticity, and impact of the photographic news content.

## Worked Examples

1. Calculating the cost of a photojournalism assignment: A photographer is assigned to cover a news event in a remote location. The photographer's daily rate is $800, and they will need to travel for 2 days to reach the location. The cost of transportation is $500, and accommodation is $200 per night for 3 nights. What is the total cost of the assignment? 
Total cost = (daily rate * number of days) + transportation + (accommodation * number of nights) 
Total cost = ($800 * 5) + $500 + ($200 * 3) 
Total cost = $4000 + $500 + $600 
Total cost = $5100.

2. Determining the optimal image size for online publication: A photojournalist has taken a photo with a resolution of 4000 x 3000 pixels. The online publication requires images to be a maximum of 1000 x 750 pixels. What is the optimal image size, and what percentage of the original image will be retained? 
Optimal image size = 1000 x 750 pixels, 
Percentage retained = (optimal size / original size) * 100 
Percentage retained = ((1000 * 750) / (4000 * 3000)) * 100 
Percentage retained = (750000 / 12000000) * 100 
Percentage retained = 6.25%.

3. Evaluating the effectiveness of a photojournalism campaign: A photojournalism campaign has been launched to raise awareness about a social issue. The campaign consists of 10 images published on social media, and it has received 1000 likes, 500 shares, and 200 comments. What is the engagement rate of the campaign? 
Engagement rate = (likes + shares + comments) / number of images 
Engagement rate = (1000 + 500 + 200) / 10 
Engagement rate = 1700 / 10 
Engagement rate = 170.

Number of photos = data limit / photo size = 10 GB / 0.005 GB = 10,000 / 5 = 2000 photos.

Editing time = total photos / photos edited per hour = 10 photos / 2 photos per hour = 5 hours.

## Common Errors

In photojournalism, practitioners often make mistakes that compromise the integrity and impact of their work. One common error is the manipulation of images, such as excessive editing or staging, which can misrepresent the truth and undermine the credibility of the photograph. Another mistake is the lack of contextual understanding, where photographers fail to research and comprehend the complexities of the story they are covering, leading to oversimplification or misrepresentation of the issue. Additionally, photojournalists may prioritize sensationalism over sensitivity, capturing images that exploit or disrespect their subjects, rather than approaching them with dignity and respect. Furthermore, the failure to consider the power dynamics at play, such as the relationship between the photographer and the subject, can result in images that perpetuate stereotypes or reinforce existing social inequalities. These errors can be attributed to a range of factors, including inadequate training, tight deadlines, and the pressure to produce attention-grabbing images. Ultimately, they can damage the reputation of the photographer and the media organization, and undermine the trust of the audience.

# Sports Journalism

> The course assumes foundational knowledge of journalism and media communications, and focuses on applying principles to real situations in sports journalism.

## Coverage

Game reporting (leads — feature, straight, delayed), feature writing, enterprise/investigative. Beat reporting (source development, access). DIGITAL: Real-time coverage (live-tweeting, live blogs), podcasting, video (highlights, analysis), social media strategy. Mobile journalism. ETHICS: Access and relationships, objectivity, dealing with PR, off-the-record, embargoes. Covering sensitive topics (injuries, PEDs, misconduct). ANALYSIS: Statistical analysis, film study, tactical writing. Data-driven storytelling. BUSINESS: Media landscape (ESPN, The Athletic, local papers), freelancing, credentialing (press passes, media rights). Broadcast: play-by-play, color commentary, studio analysis. HISTORY: Red Smith, Grantland Rice, Howard Cosell, modern: Wright Thompson, Shea Serrano.

## Foundations

In media communications, sports journalism refers to the specialized field of reporting, writing, and broadcasting news and information about sports events, teams, and athletes. A practitioner in this field must understand core definitions, first principles, and key vocabulary. **Journalism** is the practice of researching, writing, and disseminating news and information to the public through various media channels. **Sports journalism** is a subset of journalism that focuses on sports-related content. Key terms include **reporting**, which involves gathering and verifying information, and **broadcasting**, which refers to the transmission of audio or video content via electronic media. **News** is information about current events, while **feature** stories provide in-depth analysis or human-interest profiles. **Media channels** include print (newspapers, magazines), electronic (television, radio), and digital (online, social media) platforms. A **practitioner** in sports journalism must be familiar with these concepts and terminology to effectively communicate with audiences and produce high-quality content. Understanding the principles of **objectivity**, **balance**, and **fairness** is also crucial in sports journalism, as is knowledge of **media ethics** and **copyright law**.

## Mechanisms

In sports journalism, the mechanisms involve a series of steps that facilitate the gathering, production, and dissemination of sports news and information to the public. The process begins with research and planning, where journalists identify potential story leads, conduct interviews with athletes, coaches, and other relevant individuals, and gather background information. Next, journalists attend sports events, press conferences, and other related activities to collect firsthand information and quotes. They then write and edit their stories, incorporating audio, video, and still images as necessary, to create engaging and informative content. The stories are then reviewed and approved by editors, who ensure accuracy, fairness, and adherence to journalistic standards. Once approved, the content is published or broadcast through various media channels, such as newspapers, magazines, television, radio, and online platforms. The causal chain is as follows: event occurrence → journalist attendance and research → story creation → editorial review → publication/broadcast → audience consumption. Throughout this process, journalists must adhere to ethical principles, such as objectivity, transparency, and respect for sources, to maintain credibility and trust with their audience. Additionally, the rise of digital media has introduced new mechanisms, including social media engagement, live tweeting, and online commentary, which have expanded the scope and reach of sports journalism.

## Methods And Frameworks

In sports journalism, several methods and frameworks are employed to gather, analyze, and present information. The News Angle Formula is used to determine the most relevant and attention-grabbing aspect of a story, by considering the 5 Ws (who, what, where, when, and why) and the impact on the audience. The Inverted Pyramid Model is applied to structure articles, placing the most critical information at the beginning and supporting details later. The Chronological Structure is utilized for reporting on events, organizing information in a timeline. The Cause-and-Effect Analysis is used to examine the underlying reasons and consequences of a sports-related issue. Each method has its failure mode: the News Angle Formula can oversimplify complex issues, the Inverted Pyramid Model can lead to burying important context, the Chronological Structure can become overly focused on play-by-play details, and the Cause-and-Effect Analysis can be limited by incomplete or biased data. Understanding these methods and their limitations is crucial for effective sports journalism.

## Worked Examples

In sports journalism, calculating the audience reach and engagement is crucial. Here are three worked examples:
1. A sports journalist writes an article about a local football match, which is published in a newspaper with a circulation of 50,000. If the article is also posted online and receives 20,000 unique views, what is the total audience reach? 
Total audience reach = newspaper circulation + online unique views = 50,000 + 20,000 = 70,000.
2. A sports radio show has 30,000 listeners per episode. If the show is broadcast three times a week, what is the total weekly audience reach? 
Total weekly audience reach = listeners per episode * number of episodes per week = 30,000 * 3 = 90,000.
3. A sports journalist's Twitter post about a basketball game receives 500 likes, 200 retweets, and 100 comments. What is the total engagement? 
Total engagement = likes + retweets + comments = 500 + 200 + 100 = 800.

In sports journalism, reporters often need to calculate statistics and analyze data to create engaging stories. Here are three worked examples:

1. Calculating a team's winning percentage: Suppose a football team has won 12 games and lost 4 games in a season. To calculate their winning percentage, divide the number of wins by the total number of games played, then multiply by 100. So, (12 / (12 + 4)) * 100 = (12 / 16) * 100 = 0.75 * 100 = 75%. This means the team has a 75% winning percentage.

2. Determining the most valuable player: A sports journalist wants to determine the most valuable player in a basketball league based on points scored per game. If Player A has scored 250 points in 10 games and Player B has scored 200 points in 8 games, calculate their points per game. For Player A, 250 points / 10 games = 25 points per game. For Player B, 200 points / 8 games = 25 points per game. Since both players have the same points per game, the journalist may need to consider other statistics, such as rebounds or assists, to determine the most valuable player.

3. Analyzing attendance figures: A sports journalist is writing a story about the attendance at a baseball stadium. If the stadium has a capacity of 40,000 and the average attendance for the season is 32,000, what percentage of capacity is the average attendance? To calculate this, divide the average attendance by the capacity, then multiply by 100. So, (32,000 / 40,000) * 100 = 0.8 * 100 = 80%. This means the average attendance is 80% of the stadium's capacity.

Total viewers = 200,000 * 0.3 = 60,000 viewers.

## Common Errors

In sports journalism, practitioners often make mistakes that compromise the accuracy, fairness, and engaging quality of their reporting. One common error is the failure to verify facts and quotes, leading to the dissemination of misinformation. This can occur due to tight deadlines, overreliance on secondary sources, or inadequate fact-checking. Another mistake is the lack of balance and objectivity, where journalists allow personal biases or allegiances to influence their reporting, potentially misleading readers or viewers. Additionally, the use of sensationalized headlines or lead-ins can be misleading, prioritizing attention-grabbing over accuracy. Furthermore, neglecting to provide context and background information can result in reports that are incomplete or difficult to understand. The overemphasis on controversy or scandal, at the expense of more substantive issues, is also a common error, as it can create an unbalanced narrative and detract from the core of the story. Lastly, the failure to respect athletes' and teams' privacy, and to adhere to media ethics guidelines, can lead to unethical reporting practices and damage the credibility of sports journalists.

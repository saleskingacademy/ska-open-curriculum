# Human Factors Aviation

> The course assumes foundational knowledge and applies principles to real situations, requiring analysis and specialisation within the field.

## Foundations

Human Factors in Aviation is the multidisciplinary scientific study of the interactions among humans, machines, and the environment within the aviation system. It seeks to optimize human performance and safety by understanding cognitive, physiological, and organizational constraints and designing systems accordingly. Rooted in ergonomics, cognitive psychology, and systems engineering, its first principles include the recognition of human limitations (e.g., attention, memory, decision-making), the inevitability of human error, and the necessity of designing resilient systems that anticipate and mitigate these errors. The SHELL model (Software, Hardware, Environment, Liveware) frames these interactions, emphasizing the Liveware-Liveware interface as critical in crew resource management (CRM). Human Factors aims to reduce the accident chain by addressing latent conditions and active failures through systemic interventions.

In Human Factors Aviation, core definitions are crucial for understanding the complex interactions between humans, machines, and the environment. **Human Factors** refers to the scientific discipline concerned with understanding the interactions among humans, machines, and the environment, with the goal of optimizing human performance, safety, and well-being. **Aviation** encompasses the operation of aircraft, including commercial airlines, general aviation, and military aviation. **Aerospace** refers to the atmosphere and the space beyond, including the vehicles and systems that operate within these environments. 
Key vocabulary includes **crew resource management (CRM)**, which refers to the set of skills and principles that enable crew members to work effectively together to manage resources, make decisions, and maintain situational awareness. **Situational awareness** is the perception and comprehension of the environment, including the aircraft, other traffic, weather, and air traffic control. **Decision-making** is the process of selecting a course of action from available options, and **risk management** is the systematic process of identifying, assessing, and mitigating potential hazards. **Fatigue** refers to the physiological and psychological state of being tired, which can impair cognitive and motor performance. **Error** is an unintended action or decision that can lead to adverse consequences. Understanding these definitions and principles is essential for practitioners to analyze and mitigate the factors that contribute to errors and accidents in aviation.

## Section

Situational Awareness (SA) and Endsley’s Model  
Situational Awareness is the perception of environmental elements within time and space, comprehension of their meaning, and projection of their status in the near future. Endsley’s three-level model defines SA as:  
1. Level 1 – Perception of critical elements (e.g., aircraft status, weather, traffic).  
2. Level 2 – Comprehension of current situation by integrating Level 1 data (e.g., recognizing a stall condition).  
3. Level 3 – Projection of future status (e.g., anticipating trajectory deviations).  
Measurement techniques include SAGAT (Situation Awareness Global Assessment Technique), which intermittently freezes simulation to query pilots, and SPAM (Situation Present Assessment Method), which probes SA during tasks. Maintaining high SA is critical to decision-making and error prevention in dynamic aviation environments.

Human Error Taxonomy – Reason’s Swiss Cheese Model  
James Reason’s model categorizes errors as active failures (unsafe acts by operators) and latent conditions (organizational weaknesses). The Swiss Cheese Model visualizes layers of defense (training, procedures, technology) as slices of cheese with holes representing weaknesses. When holes align, hazards pass through leading to accidents. Error types include:  
- Slips (attention failures)  
- Lapses (memory failures)  
- Mistakes (rule-based or knowledge-based decision errors)  
- Violations (deliberate deviations)  
Mitigation strategies involve error management training, non-punitive reporting systems, and designing error-tolerant cockpits (e.g., automation with appropriate alerting).

Crew Resource Management (CRM) Framework  
CRM is a set of training procedures developed in the late 1970s (NASA’s Wind Tunnel Study, 1979) to enhance interpersonal communication, leadership, and decision-making in multi-crew cockpits. The core components include:  
- Communication: Closed-loop communication protocols to ensure message receipt and confirmation.  
- Leadership and Followership: Defined roles and assertiveness training to empower junior crew members.  
- Situation Monitoring: Cross-checking and mutual support to maintain SA.  
- Decision Making: Use of structured models like FOR-DEC (Facts, Options, Risks & benefits, Decision, Execution, Check).  
CRM training has reduced controlled flight into terrain (CFIT) and mid-air collision incidents by improving team coordination.

Workload Assessment – NASA-TLX  
The NASA Task Load Index (NASA-TLX) is a validated subjective workload assessment tool measuring six dimensions: Mental Demand, Physical Demand, Temporal Demand, Performance, Effort, and Frustration. Each dimension is rated on a 0-100 scale, weighted by pairwise comparisons to produce an overall workload score. In aviation, NASA-TLX is applied during simulator sessions and line operations to assess pilot workload under varying conditions (e.g., automation modes, weather complexity). Optimal workload management is essential to prevent overload (leading to errors) or underload (leading to complacency).

Automation and Human-Machine Interaction – Levels of Automation (LOA)  
Parasuraman, Sheridan, and Wickens (2000) define LOA on a 10-level scale from complete manual control (Level 1) to full automation with no human involvement (Level 10). Aviation systems like autopilots and flight management systems operate typically between Levels 4-7, where automation suggests options, executes tasks, or supervises human actions. Human factors challenges include automation complacency, mode confusion, and loss of manual flying skills. Mitigation involves adaptive automation, transparency of automation state, and training emphasizing mode awareness.

Fatigue Risk Management Systems (FRMS)  
FRMS are data-driven, scientifically based approaches to managing fatigue in aviation personnel beyond prescriptive flight time limitations. Components include:  
- Predictive modeling (e.g., SAFTE model) to estimate alertness based on sleep-wake history.  
- Fatigue reporting systems and biomathematical modeling integrated into rostering.  
- Countermeasures such as strategic napping, controlled caffeine use, and circadian-friendly scheduling.  
ICAO Doc 9966 and FAA AC 120-100 provide regulatory frameworks for FRMS implementation. Effective fatigue management reduces performance decrements linked to microsleeps, slowed reaction times, and impaired judgment.

Human Factors in Accident Investigation – HFACS Framework  
The Human Factors Analysis and Classification System (HFACS), developed by Wiegmann and Shappell (2003), is a hierarchical framework to classify human error in aviation accidents across four levels:  
1. Unsafe Acts (errors and violations)  
2. Preconditions for Unsafe Acts (environmental factors, condition of operators)  
3. Unsafe Supervision (inadequate supervision, planning)  
4. Organizational Influences (resource management, culture)  
HFACS guides investigators to identify systemic contributors beyond frontline errors, enabling targeted safety interventions.

## Mechanisms

In Human Factors Aviation, the mechanisms refer to the complex interplay of cognitive, physical, and environmental factors that influence human behavior and performance in aviation. The causal chain can be broken down into several key steps: 
1. **Perception**: The process by which pilots and air traffic controllers gather and interpret information from their environment, including visual, auditory, and tactile cues. 
2. **Attention**: The allocation of mental resources to focus on specific tasks or stimuli, which can be affected by factors such as workload, fatigue, and stress. 
3. **Decision-making**: The process of selecting a course of action based on perceived information, which can be influenced by biases, experience, and training. 
4. **Action**: The physical execution of a task, which can be impacted by factors such as manual dexterity, physical fatigue, and equipment design. 
5. **Feedback**: The receipt of information about the outcome of an action, which can be used to adjust subsequent behavior. 
These mechanisms are interconnected and can be influenced by various factors, including pilot training, aircraft design, and environmental conditions. Understanding these mechanisms is crucial for identifying potential human factors hazards and developing strategies to mitigate them.

In Human Factors Aviation, the mechanisms refer to the complex interplay of psychological, physiological, and social factors that influence human behavior and performance in the aviation environment. The causal chain can be broken down into several key steps: 
1. **Stimulus**: An event or situation occurs, such as an alarm sounding or a change in weather conditions, which captures the attention of the pilot or air traffic controller. 
2. **Perception**: The individual perceives the stimulus, which involves interpreting and processing the information through their senses and cognitive biases. 
3. **Information Processing**: The perceived information is then processed in the brain, where it is evaluated, and a decision is made based on past experiences, training, and mental models. 
4. **Decision-Making**: The individual makes a decision in response to the stimulus, which may involve selecting an action, solving a problem, or communicating with others. 
5. **Action**: The decision is then translated into an action, such as adjusting the aircraft's course or communicating with air traffic control. 
6. **Feedback**: The outcome of the action is then evaluated, providing feedback to the individual, which can influence future decisions and behaviors. 
This causal chain is influenced by various factors, including individual differences, team dynamics, and organizational culture, which can either enhance or degrade human performance in the aviation environment.

## Methods And Frameworks

In Human Factors Aviation, several methods and frameworks are employed to analyze and mitigate human error in aviation systems. The Hierarchical Task Analysis (HTA) is used to decompose complex tasks into smaller, manageable components, identifying potential error points and informing design improvements. The Failure Modes and Effects Analysis (FMEA) is applied to identify potential failure modes, their causes, and effects on the system, allowing for proactive mitigation strategies. The Human Error Assessment and Reduction Technique (HEART) provides a quantitative approach to estimating human error probabilities, taking into account factors such as task complexity and operator experience. The Crew Resource Management (CRM) framework focuses on enhancing teamwork, communication, and decision-making skills among crew members to reduce errors and improve safety. The Swiss Cheese Model illustrates how multiple layers of defense can be breached by an error, highlighting the importance of redundant safety measures. Each of these methods has its own strengths and limitations, and their application depends on the specific context and goals of the analysis. For instance, HTA is particularly useful for designing new systems, while FMEA is more suited for evaluating existing systems. Understanding the appropriate application and potential failure modes of these methods is crucial for effective human factors analysis in aviation.

## Worked Examples

To illustrate the application of human factors in aviation, consider the following examples. 
1. A pilot's workload is affected by the number of tasks they must perform during a critical phase of flight. Suppose a pilot has 10 tasks to complete during takeoff, each requiring 2 minutes to complete. If the takeoff phase lasts 5 minutes, what is the pilot's workload ratio? 
Workload ratio = (number of tasks * time per task) / available time = (10 * 2) / 5 = 4. This indicates the pilot is overloaded, as the workload ratio exceeds 1. 
2. The design of a cockpit can impact pilot performance. For instance, if the distance between the pilot's seat and the control yoke is 30 inches, and the pilot's arm length is 25 inches, what is the potential for pilot error due to reach distance? 
Using the principle of ergonomic design, a reach distance of 5 inches (30 - 25) may lead to pilot stretching, potentially causing fatigue and increasing error risk. 
3. In a crew resource management (CRM) scenario, a first officer fails to cross-check the captain's altitude setting, resulting in a 1,000-foot deviation from the assigned altitude. If the aircraft's altitude deviation is limited to 200 feet, what is the likelihood of a controlled flight into terrain (CFIT) accident? 
Applying the principle of threat and error management, the likelihood of a CFIT accident increases when the altitude deviation exceeds the allowed limit, highlighting the importance of effective crew communication and cross-checking.

1. A pilot's workload assessment: Suppose a pilot is tasked with flying a Boeing 737 from New York to Los Angeles. The flight requires navigating through multiple air traffic control zones, managing fuel consumption, and monitoring weather conditions. Using the NASA Task Load Index (TLX) scale, which ranges from 0 to 100, we can assess the pilot's workload. If the pilot rates their mental demand at 60, physical demand at 20, temporal demand at 40, performance at 80, effort at 50, and frustration at 30, the overall workload score would be the average of these ratings, which is (60+20+40+80+50+30)/6 = 46.67. This score indicates a moderate level of workload, suggesting the pilot may require additional support or resources to ensure safe flight operations.
2. Crew resource management (CRM) in emergency situations: In an emergency, such as an engine failure, effective communication and decision-making are critical. For instance, if a first officer notices an engine oil pressure drop, they should immediately inform the captain, who then assesses the situation and decides on the course of action. Using the DECIDE model (Detect, Estimate, Choose, Identify, Do, Evaluate), the captain can systematically approach the problem, ensuring that all relevant factors are considered, and a safe outcome is achieved.
3. Fatigue risk management: A flight crew is scheduled to fly a red-eye flight from Paris to Tokyo, spanning 11 hours. To assess the fatigue risk, we can use the Fatigue Risk Management System (FRMS) framework. Assuming the crew had 6 hours of sleep before the flight and will have 2 hours of rest during the flight, their fatigue score can be calculated based on the sleep-wake cycle and duty period. If the score exceeds a certain threshold (e.g., 70), it may indicate a high risk of fatigue, prompting the airline to consider adjusting the crew's schedule or providing additional rest periods to mitigate the risk.

## Applications

In aviation aerospace, Human Factors (HF) is applied to optimize the performance and safety of aviation systems, including aircraft, air traffic control, and airports. HF principles are used to design and evaluate cockpit interfaces, ensuring that controls, displays, and warning systems are intuitive and effective. For example, the design of flight decks incorporates HF considerations such as ergonomics, workload management, and situational awareness. HF is also applied in the development of crew resource management (CRM) training programs, which focus on communication, decision-making, and teamwork skills to enhance safety and efficiency. Additionally, HF is used in the investigation of aviation accidents and incidents, where it helps identify root causes related to human performance and organizational factors. In air traffic control, HF is applied to optimize controller workload, communication protocols, and decision-support systems. Furthermore, HF informs the design of airport facilities, such as signage, wayfinding, and security screening processes, to minimize passenger stress and errors. By considering the capabilities, limitations, and behaviors of humans in the aviation system, HF applications aim to reduce errors, enhance safety, and improve overall system performance.

## Common Errors

In Human Factors Aviation, common errors made by practitioners include inadequate consideration of crew resource management (CRM) principles, insufficient analysis of human-machine interface (HMI) design, and failure to account for the impact of fatigue on pilot performance. A significant mistake is neglecting to assess the cognitive workload of pilots during critical phases of flight, leading to increased error rates. Another error is not providing adequate training on threat and error management (TEM), resulting in pilots being unprepared to respond to unexpected events. Additionally, practitioners often overlook the importance of standard operating procedures (SOPs) in reducing errors, and may not adequately consider the effects of automation on pilot vigilance and situational awareness. These errors can be attributed to a lack of understanding of human factors principles, inadequate application of risk management strategies, and insufficient consideration of the complex interactions between humans, machines, and the environment in aviation systems. By recognizing and addressing these common errors, practitioners can improve the safety and efficiency of aviation operations.

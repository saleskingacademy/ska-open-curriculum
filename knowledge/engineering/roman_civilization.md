## Founding To Republic (753-27 Bce)

Legendary founding by Romulus (753 BCE). Etruscan kings expelled (509 BCE). Republic: Senate + consuls + tribunes. Twelve Tables (450 BCE) — first written Roman law. PUNIC WARS: vs Carthage. First (264-241 — Sicily). Second (218-201 — Hannibal crosses Alps with elephants, Cannae disaster, Scipio Africanus wins at Zama). Third (149-146 — Carthage destroyed, salted). EXPANSION: Greece (146 BCE), Gaul (58-50 BCE, Caesar), Egypt (30 BCE), Britain (43 CE). Peak territory 117 CE under Trajan — 5M km², 70M people. ENGINEERING: Roads (80,000+ km, lasted 2000 years), aqueducts (11 serving Rome, 500km+ total), concrete (opus caementicium — Pantheon dome still largest unreinforced concrete dome), Colosseum (50,000 capacity), sewers (Cloaca Maxima). LAW: Ius civile, ius gentium, ius naturale. Jurisprudence (Gaius, Ulpian, Justinian's Corpus Juris Civilis 529 CE — foundation of Western legal systems). EMPIRE (27 BCE-476 CE): Augustus (Pax Romana), Nero, Trajan, Hadrian, Marcus Aurelius. Crisis of Third Century. Constantine (Christianity legalized 313, Constantinople 330). Theodosius (Christianity state religion 380). FALL: Multiple causes — economic (inflation, taxation), military (Germanic invasions, Visigoths sack Rome 410, Vandals 455), political (corruption, civil wars), social (population decline, plagues). Last Western emperor Romulus Augustulus deposed 476 CE. Eastern Empire (Byzantine) continues to 1453.

## Mechanisms

The Roman civilization's mechanisms can be understood through the lens of its social, political, and economic structures. At the core of Roman society was the concept of citizenship, which granted individuals certain rights and privileges. The process of becoming a Roman citizen involved a series of steps, including birth or manumission, followed by registration in the census, and finally, the granting of citizenship by the Roman state. This citizenship was the foundation upon which the Roman system of governance was built, with citizens participating in the assemblies and electing representatives to the Senate. The Senate, composed of patrician and plebeian members, advised the consuls, who were the chief executives of the Roman state. The consuls, in turn, were responsible for implementing the decisions of the Senate and overseeing the administration of the Roman territories. The Roman economy was characterized by a mix of agriculture, trade, and commerce, with the state playing a significant role in regulating economic activities. The Roman system of law, based on the Twelve Tables, provided a framework for resolving disputes and governing social and economic interactions. The mechanisms of Roman civilization were also influenced by its system of social hierarchy, which included the aristocracy, the equestrian order, and the plebeians, each with their own distinct roles and responsibilities. The interplay between these mechanisms allowed the Roman civilization to function and maintain its complex social, political, and economic systems.

The stability and functionality of this system were further reinforced by Roman law, which provided a framework for governance, commerce, and social relations, ensuring a degree of predictability and order within the complex societal structure.

Another error is the oversimplification of the Roman Republic's transition to the Empire, often portrayed as a sudden, drastic change rather than a gradual process influenced by political, economic, and military factors. Additionally, the tendency to romanticize or demonize ancient Roman practices, such as slavery or gladiatorial games, without considering the historical context and the evolution of these practices over time, can lead to inaccurate representations of Roman civilization. Furthermore, the failure to distinguish between mythological and historical accounts, such as the legends surrounding Rome's foundation by Romulus and Remus, and the actual historical records of the city's development, can confuse the understanding of Roman history and culture. These errors stem from a lack of nuanced understanding of the ancient sources, the complexity of Roman society, and the need for a multidisciplinary approach to studying Roman civilization, incorporating history, archaeology, literature, and art.

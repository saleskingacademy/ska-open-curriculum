# Graph Neural Networks

> The course assumes prior knowledge of graph theory, neural networks, and optimization algorithms.

## Foundations

Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) are a class of deep learning models designed to process graph-structured data, which consists of nodes (also known as vertices) connected by edges. A graph is defined as a mathematical structure comprising a set of nodes (V) and a set of edges (E) between them, represented as G = (V, E). In this context, nodes can represent entities such as atoms in a molecule, users in a social network, or objects in a scene, while edges represent relationships between these entities, such as chemical bonds, friendships, or proximity.

Key concepts include adjacency matrices, which are mathematical representations of a graph's structure, where the entry at row i and column j represents the presence or absence of an edge between nodes i and j. Another crucial concept is the node degree, which refers to the number of edges incident on a node. Graph neural networks learn node representations (embeddings) by aggregating information from neighboring nodes, using techniques such as message passing, where each node sends and receives information to and from its neighbors, iteratively updating its representation until convergence or a specified number of iterations.

Understanding graph theory and neural network fundamentals is essential for working with GNNs, including familiarity with multilayer perceptrons (MLPs), convolutional neural networks (CNNs), and recurrent neural networks (RNNs), as GNNs often build upon or combine elements from these architectures. Additionally, knowledge of optimization algorithms, such as stochastic gradient descent (SGD), and backpropagation is necessary for training GNN models.

## Mechanisms

Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) operate by propagating information through a graph structure, leveraging the relationships between nodes to learn node and graph representations. The process involves the following steps: 
1. **Node Representation Initialization**: Each node in the graph is associated with a vector representation, often initialized randomly or based on node attributes.
2. **Message Passing**: Nodes exchange information with their neighbors through a message passing mechanism, where each node aggregates messages from its neighbors using a permutation-invariant function, such as sum or mean.
3. **Node Update**: Each node updates its representation by combining the aggregated message with its current representation, using a learnable function, such as a neural network.
4. **Activation**: The updated node representation is passed through an activation function to introduce non-linearity.
5. **Graph Representation**: The node representations are aggregated to obtain a graph representation, using a permutation-invariant function, such as sum or mean.
6. **Output**: The graph representation is used for downstream tasks, such as node classification, link prediction, or graph classification.
The causal chain is as follows: node attributes and graph structure → node representation initialization → message passing → node update → activation → graph representation → output. This process allows GNNs to capture complex relationships between nodes and learn effective representations for graph-structured data.

## Methods And Frameworks

Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) employ various methods and frameworks to learn node and graph representations. 
The Graph Convolutional Network (GCN) is a widely used method, utilizing convolutional layers to aggregate node features, suitable for node classification and graph classification tasks. 
The Graph Attention Network (GAT) uses self-attention mechanisms to weigh neighboring nodes, effective in tasks requiring complex node relationships. 
The Graph Autoencoder (GAE) and Variational Graph Autoencoder (VGAE) are used for unsupervised learning, such as node clustering and graph generation. 
The Message Passing Neural Network (MPNN) framework is a generalization of GNNs, providing a unified view of various GNN models. 
Each method has its failure mode, such as GCN's oversmoothing, where node features become too similar, and GAT's sensitivity to hyperparameters. 
Understanding the strengths and weaknesses of these methods is crucial for selecting the appropriate framework for a specific task, considering factors such as graph structure, node features, and computational resources.

## Applications

Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) have numerous applications in computer science, particularly in domains involving complex relationships and structures. In social network analysis, GNNs are used for user classification, recommendation systems, and community detection. For instance, they can predict user demographics or preferences based on their social connections and behavior. In natural language processing, GNNs are applied to text classification, sentiment analysis, and machine translation, where they can capture long-range dependencies and syntactic structures in sentences. Additionally, GNNs are used in computer vision for image classification, object detection, and segmentation, especially when dealing with graph-structured data such as 3D models or molecular structures. In recommendation systems, GNNs can learn user and item embeddings by modeling their interactions as a graph, leading to more accurate personalized recommendations. Furthermore, GNNs are applied in traffic forecasting, where they can model the complex relationships between roads and intersections, and in molecular chemistry, where they can predict molecular properties and generate new molecules. The key principle behind these applications is the ability of GNNs to learn effective representations of nodes and edges in a graph, which can then be used for downstream tasks such as classification, clustering, or regression.

## Common Errors

In the implementation and application of Graph Neural Networks (GNNs), several common errors are observed. One of the primary mistakes is the incorrect assumption that all nodes in a graph have the same importance or influence, leading to the use of uniform aggregation methods. However, in many real-world graphs, nodes have varying degrees of importance, and using weighted aggregation or attention mechanisms can significantly improve the model's performance. Another error is the failure to account for over-smoothing, which occurs when the model's aggregation process leads to loss of node-specific information, resulting in nodes having similar representations. This can be mitigated by using techniques such as residual connections or dilated aggregations. Additionally, practitioners often overlook the issue of graph heterophily, where nodes with different labels are more likely to be connected, leading to poor performance of homophily-based GNN models. Using models that can handle heterophily, such as those incorporating edge information or node attributes, can help alleviate this issue. Furthermore, the choice of aggregation function, such as mean, sum, or max, can significantly impact the model's performance, and selecting the appropriate function based on the specific problem and graph structure is crucial. Lastly, neglecting to consider the computational complexity and memory requirements of GNNs can lead to scalability issues, emphasizing the need for efficient implementation and optimization techniques.

Another mistake is the misuse of aggregation functions, such as sum, mean, or max, without considering the specific characteristics of the graph data. For instance, using a sum aggregator on a graph with a large number of nodes can result in feature dominance, where the features of nodes with higher degrees overshadow those of nodes with lower degrees. Additionally, many practitioners neglect to consider the over-smoothing issue in GNNs, where repeated aggregation and transformation of node features can lead to loss of information and node differentiation. This can be mitigated by using techniques such as residual connections, skip connections, or attention mechanisms to preserve the unique characteristics of each node. Furthermore, the choice of activation functions and the initialization of model weights are also critical, as improper choices can result in vanishing or exploding gradients during training. Lastly, the evaluation metrics used to assess GNN performance are often inadequate, with many practitioners relying solely on node classification accuracy, rather than considering more comprehensive metrics that account for graph structure and relationships, such as graph reconstruction accuracy or link prediction performance.

## Advanced

The graduate-level extensions of Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) involve exploring new architectures, improving scalability, and addressing open questions. One key area of research is the development of more expressive GNN models, such as Graph Attention Networks (GATs) and Graph Transformers, which can capture complex relationships between nodes. Another direction is the integration of GNNs with other machine learning paradigms, like reinforcement learning and adversarial training, to enhance their robustness and adaptability. The field is also moving towards addressing challenges like over-smoothing, where node representations become indistinguishable, and the lack of interpretability, by developing techniques like graph pruning and attention visualization. Furthermore, researchers are exploring the application of GNNs to dynamic graphs, where edges and nodes change over time, and to graphs with heterogeneous node and edge types. Open questions include the development of theoretical frameworks to understand the representational power of GNNs and the design of efficient algorithms for training GNNs on large-scale graphs. The field is rapidly evolving, with new architectures and techniques being proposed to address these challenges and open questions, and to expand the applications of GNNs to new domains.

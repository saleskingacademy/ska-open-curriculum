## Symmetric

## Foundations

In Applied Cryptography, core definitions and first principles are crucial for a practitioner to understand. **Cryptography** refers to the practice and study of techniques for secure communication, ensuring confidentiality, integrity, and authenticity of data. A **cryptosystem** is a system that implements cryptographic techniques to provide secure data transmission. **Plaintext** refers to the original, unencrypted data, while **ciphertext** is the encrypted data. **Encryption** is the process of converting plaintext into ciphertext using an **encryption algorithm** and a **key**. A key is a secret value used to control the encryption and decryption processes. **Decryption** is the reverse process, converting ciphertext back into plaintext. **Symmetric-key cryptography** uses the same key for both encryption and decryption, whereas **asymmetric-key cryptography**, also known as **public-key cryptography**, uses a pair of keys: a **public key** for encryption and a corresponding **private key** for decryption. Understanding these foundational concepts and vocabulary is essential for a practitioner to design, implement, and analyze secure cryptosystems.

## Mechanisms

In applied cryptography, the mechanisms refer to the step-by-step processes that enable secure data transmission and protection. The causal chain involves several key components: plaintext, encryption algorithm, encryption key, ciphertext, decryption algorithm, and decryption key. The process begins with plaintext, the original data to be secured. This plaintext is then fed into an encryption algorithm, which uses an encryption key to transform the data into ciphertext, the encrypted form of the plaintext. The encryption algorithm, such as Advanced Encryption Standard (AES) or RSA, dictates the specific steps and mathematical transformations applied to the plaintext. The encryption key, a secret value, determines the exact output of the encryption algorithm, ensuring that only those with access to the corresponding decryption key can retrieve the original plaintext. The ciphertext is then transmitted or stored, and upon receipt, is fed into a decryption algorithm, which uses the decryption key to reverse the encryption process, yielding the original plaintext. The decryption algorithm must be designed to work in tandem with the encryption algorithm, using the decryption key to undo the transformations applied during encryption. This interplay between encryption and decryption algorithms, mediated by the encryption and decryption keys, forms the core mechanism of applied cryptography, enabling secure communication and data protection.

The encrypted data, or ciphertext, is then transmitted over an insecure channel. Key management, including generation, distribution, and revocation, is crucial to maintaining the security of the cryptographic system, as it ensures that keys are handled securely and that access to encrypted data is controlled.

## Methods And Frameworks

In applied cryptography, several methods and frameworks are employed to ensure secure data transmission and protection. The Advanced Encryption Standard (AES) is a widely used symmetric-key block cipher, suitable for bulk data encryption due to its high speed and low computational overhead. It is commonly used for secure data transfer and storage. However, its failure mode can occur if the key is compromised or weak. 
The RSA algorithm, based on the principles of asymmetric cryptography, is often used for secure key exchange and digital signatures. It is particularly useful when the parties involved do not share a common secret key. The failure mode of RSA can occur if the private key is compromised or if the key size is too small, making it vulnerable to factorization attacks. 
The Diffie-Hellman key exchange is another method used for secure key exchange over an insecure channel. It is useful when the parties involved want to establish a shared secret key without actually exchanging the key. However, its failure mode can occur if the parties involved are not authenticated, making it vulnerable to man-in-the-middle attacks. 
The Elliptic Curve Cryptography (ECC) framework provides a more efficient alternative to traditional public-key cryptography, offering similar security levels with smaller key sizes. It is particularly useful in resource-constrained devices. However, its failure mode can occur if the elliptic curve parameters are not properly chosen, making it vulnerable to certain attacks. 
In general, the choice of method or framework depends on the specific use case, considering factors such as computational overhead, key size, and the level of security required. The Elliptic Curve Cryptography (ECC) framework offers a more efficient alternative to RSA, providing equivalent security with smaller key sizes, thus reducing computational requirements. Additionally, quantum computer attacks pose a significant threat to many cryptographic systems, including RSA and ECC, due to their ability to efficiently factor large numbers and compute discrete logarithms.

The message is first converted to its ASCII values: H=72, E=69, L=76, L=76, O=79. Then, it is divided into blocks of 16 bytes (128 bits). Since our message is shorter, we pad it with zeros to get a full block: 72 69 76 76 79 00 00 00 00 00 00 00 00 00 00. The AES encryption process involves multiple rounds of substitution and permutation, resulting in the encrypted block: 84 48 8C 9F 3B 3E 9C 22 6F 8E 2A 5E 8B 12 31 41.

2. **Digital Signature using RSA**: A sender wants to send a message "TRANSFER 100" to a receiver, and they want to ensure the authenticity of the message. They use RSA with a public key (e=17, n=323) and a private key (d=275, n=323). The message is hashed using SHA-256 to get a digest: 0x6A 0x24 0x2F 0x6E 0x74 0x61 0x6E 0x73 0x66 0x65 0x72 0x20 0x31 0x30 0x30. The sender then encrypts the digest with their private key to get the signature: 0x53 0x9F 0x2A 0x6E 0x1C 0x12 0x34 0x5A 0x7F 0x2B 0x9C 0x6D 0x1A 0x35 0x4E. The receiver can verify the signature by decrypting it with the sender's public key and comparing it to the hash of the received message.

3. **Diffie-Hellman Key Exchange**: Two parties, Alice and Bob, want to establish a shared secret key over an insecure channel. Alice chooses a private number a=6 and a public prime p=23, and a generator g=5. Bob chooses a private number b=15. Alice computes A = g^a mod p = 5^6 mod 23 = 8 and sends it to Bob. Bob computes B = g^b mod p = 5^15 mod 23 = 19 and sends it to Alice. Then, Alice computes the shared key K = B^a mod p = 19^6 mod 23 = 10, and Bob computes the same key K = A^b mod p = 8^15 mod 23 = 10. Now, both Alice and Bob have the shared secret key 10 without actually exchanging it.

## Common Errors

In applied cryptography, practitioners often make mistakes that compromise the security of their systems. One common error is using insufficient key sizes, such as using 1024-bit RSA keys, which can be factored by powerful computers. Another mistake is using outdated encryption algorithms, like MD5 or SHA-1, which have been shown to be vulnerable to collisions and other attacks. Additionally, many practitioners fail to properly implement secure protocols, such as TLS, by not verifying certificates or not using secure cipher suites. 
A significant error is also the misuse of random number generators, where predictable or weak random numbers are used to generate keys or nonces, allowing attackers to predict or guess these values. Furthermore, side-channel attacks, like timing or power analysis attacks, can be successful if implementations do not take into account the physical properties of the system, such as execution time or power consumption. 
Lastly, rolling out custom or proprietary cryptographic protocols or algorithms, without rigorous testing and peer review, can lead to unforeseen vulnerabilities and weaknesses, as these may not have been thoroughly analyzed or tested. These mistakes can be avoided by following established cryptographic standards, using well-tested libraries and frameworks, and staying up-to-date with the latest research and developments in the field of cryptography. Implementing encryption incorrectly, such as not using authenticated encryption modes like GCM or EAX, can also lead to security breaches. Additionally, hardcoding encryption keys or storing them insecurely can provide an attacker with direct access to sensitive data. Reusing nonces or initialization vectors in encryption schemes can also compromise security. Furthermore, neglecting to regularly update and patch cryptographic libraries and frameworks can expose systems to known vulnerabilities. These mistakes can be attributed to a lack of understanding of cryptographic principles, inadequate testing, and insufficient consideration of potential attack vectors. By recognizing and avoiding these common errors, practitioners can significantly improve the security of their cryptographic implementations. Additionally, many practitioners fail to properly implement secure random number generation, leading to predictable keys and nonces. The use of hardcoded or default passwords for cryptographic keys and the storage of sensitive cryptographic material, such as private keys, in insecure locations are also prevalent mistakes. A fundamental error is misunderstanding the differences between various cryptographic primitives, such as confusing authentication with encryption, or using digital signatures incorrectly.

## Advanced

In advanced applied cryptography, several extensions and open questions are being explored. One key area is the development of homomorphic encryption, which enables computations to be performed directly on ciphertext, generating an encrypted result that, when decrypted, matches the result of operations performed on the plaintext. This has significant implications for secure outsourcing of computations and privacy-preserving data analysis. Another area of research is zero-knowledge proofs, which allow one party to prove that a statement is true without revealing any information beyond the validity of the statement itself. Advances in lattice-based cryptography and code-based cryptography are also being investigated as potential replacements for traditional public-key cryptosystems, which may be vulnerable to attacks by quantum computers. The field is moving towards post-quantum cryptography, focusing on cryptographic primitives and protocols that are resistant to quantum computer attacks, such as hash-based signatures and multivariate cryptography. Furthermore, the integration of cryptographic techniques with other areas of computer science, such as machine learning and artificial intelligence, is an active area of research, with applications in secure multi-party computation, differential privacy, and federated learning. Open questions include the development of more efficient and scalable cryptographic protocols, the improvement of side-channel attack resistance, and the exploration of new cryptographic primitives and models, such as fully homomorphic encryption and indistinguishability obfuscation. Recent advancements in zk-SNARKs (zero-knowledge succinct non-interactive arguments of knowledge) have made these proofs more efficient and practical. The field is also moving towards the development of post-quantum cryptography, which involves designing cryptographic primitives that are resistant to attacks by quantum computers. Lattice-based cryptography, code-based cryptography, and hash-based signatures are some of the approaches being explored.

# Spiking Neural Networks

> The course teaches core concepts and mechanisms of spiking neural networks, assuming some basic knowledge of artificial neural networks.

## Foundations

Spiking neural networks (SNNs) are a type of artificial neural network (ANN) that mimic the behavior of biological neural networks, where information is transmitted through discrete events, or spikes. A **neuron**, the fundamental unit of SNNs, is a mathematical model that receives, processes, and transmits information. **Membrane potential** refers to the voltage across the neuron's membrane, which is the difference in electrical charge between the interior and exterior of the cell. When the membrane potential reaches a **threshold**, the neuron emits a **spike**, a brief, all-or-nothing electrical impulse. This process is known as **firing**. The time between spikes is called the **inter-spike interval**. SNNs consist of multiple **layers**, with each layer comprising multiple neurons that communicate through **synapses**, which are connections between neurons that enable the exchange of information. The **weight** of a synapse determines the strength of the connection between two neurons. **Plasticity** refers to the ability of SNNs to adapt and change in response to experience, enabling **learning** and **memory** formation. Understanding these core concepts is essential for designing and implementing effective SNNs.

## Mechanisms

Spiking Neural Networks (SNNs) operate by mimicking the behavior of biological neurons, which communicate through discrete electrical impulses, or spikes. The mechanism involves several key steps: 
1. **Membrane Potential**: Each artificial neuron maintains a membrane potential, a value that represents the likelihood of the neuron firing. 
2. **Synaptic Input**: When a presynaptic neuron fires, it sends a signal to the postsynaptic neuron, which increases or decreases the postsynaptic neuron's membrane potential, depending on the synapse type (excitatory or inhibitory). 
3. **Integration**: The postsynaptic neuron integrates the incoming signals over time, effectively summing the synaptic inputs. 
4. **Thresholding**: If the integrated membrane potential exceeds a certain threshold, the neuron fires, generating a spike. 
5. **Reset**: After firing, the neuron's membrane potential is reset to a lower value, often below the threshold, to prevent immediate refiring. 
6. **Spike Timing**: The timing of spikes is crucial, as it conveys information about the input patterns. 
7. **Learning**: SNNs learn through spike-timing-dependent plasticity (STDP), where the strength of synapses is adjusted based on the relative timing of presynaptic and postsynaptic spikes. 
This causal chain enables SNNs to process spatiotemporal patterns in data, making them suitable for applications such as real-time signal processing and event-driven perception.

## Methods And Frameworks

Spiking Neural Networks (SNNs) employ various methods and frameworks to simulate neural behavior, including Leaky Integrate-and-Fire (LIF) and Integrate-and-Fire (IF) models, which describe the membrane potential of a neuron over time. The LIF model is used when the neuron's membrane potential is subject to leakage, while the IF model is used for simpler simulations. The Spike-Timing-Dependent Plasticity (STDP) rule is a synaptic plasticity model that updates weights based on the relative timing of pre- and post-synaptic spikes, useful for learning and adaptation. The Hodgkin-Huxley model is a more complex, biophysically-inspired model that simulates the behavior of ion channels and action potentials, used when detailed, realistic simulations are required. Failure modes include overfitting, where the network becomes too specialized to the training data, and underfitting, where the network fails to capture the underlying patterns. The choice of method and framework depends on the specific application, such as image recognition, natural language processing, or robotic control, and the desired level of biological realism.

## Worked Examples

To illustrate the application of Spiking Neural Networks (SNNs) in computer science, consider the following examples.

1. **Spike-Timing-Dependent Plasticity (STDP)**: In an SNN, suppose we have two neurons, A and B, where A synapses onto B. If A spikes 10ms before B, the synaptic weight increases by 0.1. If A spikes 10ms after B, the synaptic weight decreases by 0.05. Initially, the synaptic weight is 0.5. After one iteration where A spikes 15ms before B, and another iteration where A spikes 5ms after B, calculate the new synaptic weight.

First iteration: weight = 0.5 + 0.1 = 0.6 (since A spikes before B)
Second iteration: weight = 0.6 - 0.05 = 0.55 (since A spikes after B)

2. **Leaky Integrate-and-Fire (LIF) Neuron**: An LIF neuron has a membrane potential V, a leak rate λ, and a threshold θ. Suppose λ = 0.1 and θ = 1.0. If the neuron receives a spike train with inter-spike intervals of 20ms, and the initial membrane potential is 0.2, calculate the membrane potential after 50ms.

Let's break it down: 
- At 0ms, V = 0.2
- At 20ms, V = 0.2 + (1 - 0.2) * (1 - exp(-20*0.1)) = 0.2 + 0.8 * 0.8647 = 0.692
- Since 0.692 < θ, the neuron doesn't spike, and the potential leaks.
- At 40ms, V = 0.692 * exp(-20*0.1) = 0.592
- At 50ms (10ms after the second spike), V = 0.592 + (1 - 0.592) * (1 - exp(-10*0.1)) = 0.592 + 0.408 * 0.465 = 0.706

3. **SNN with Input Encoding**: Suppose we want to encode an input value of 0.8 into a spike train using a time-to-first-spike encoding, where the first spike occurs at time t = 100 * (1 - input). Calculate the time of the first spike for the input 0.8.

Time of first spike = 100 * (1 - 0.8) = 20ms. This means the neuron will spike at 20ms to represent the input value 0.8.

1. **Spike-Timing-Dependent Plasticity (STDP)**: Given a neuron with an initial synaptic weight of 0.5, and STDP parameters of A+ = 0.1 and A- = -0.1, calculate the new synaptic weight after a pre-synaptic spike at time 0 and a post-synaptic spike at time 10ms, assuming a time constant of 20ms. The weight update is calculated as Δw = A+ * exp(-Δt/τ) for Δt > 0, where Δt is the time difference between the spikes. Here, Δt = 10ms, so Δw = 0.1 * exp(-10/20) = 0.067. The new synaptic weight is then w = 0.5 + 0.067 = 0.567.

2. **Leaky Integrate-and-Fire (LIF) Neuron**: A LIF neuron has a membrane potential V, a leak rate τ, and an input current I. Given V(0) = -70mV, τ = 10ms, I = 1.5nA, and a threshold potential of -55mV, calculate the time it takes for the neuron to fire. The membrane potential is modeled by the differential equation dV/dt = (-V + RI)/τ, where R is the membrane resistance. Assuming R = 1MΩ, we can solve this equation numerically to find the time of firing.

3. **SNN Pattern Recognition**: Suppose we have an SNN trained to recognize two patterns: a vertical line and a horizontal line. Each pattern is represented by a set of 10 spikes, and the SNN has two output neurons. Given a new input pattern of 10 spikes, calculate the output of each neuron using the spike counts and synaptic weights. For example, if the synaptic weights for the vertical line are [0.2, 0.1, ..., 0.2] and the spike counts for the input pattern are [1, 1, ..., 1], the output of the first neuron is calculated as the dot product of the weights and spike counts, followed by an activation function such as a sigmoid or threshold function.

1. **Spike-Timing-Dependent Plasticity (STDP)**: In an SNN, suppose we have two neurons, A and B, with a synaptic weight of 0.5. If neuron A fires 10ms before neuron B, and the STDP rule states that the synaptic weight increases by 0.1 for each 10ms of positive spike timing difference, calculate the new synaptic weight. 
Given: initial synaptic weight = 0.5, Δt = 10ms, Δw = 0.1 for each 10ms. 
New synaptic weight = initial synaptic weight + Δw = 0.5 + 0.1 = 0.6.

2. **Leaky Integrate-and-Fire (LIF) Neuron**: A LIF neuron has a membrane potential of -70mV, a leak rate of 0.1, and a threshold potential of -55mV. If the neuron receives a 10mV input, calculate the new membrane potential after 1 time step (dt = 1ms). 
Given: Vm = -70mV, leak rate = 0.1, threshold = -55mV, input = 10mV, dt = 1ms. 
New Vm = Vm + (input - leak rate * Vm) * dt = -70mV + (10mV - 0.1 * -70mV) * 1ms = -70mV + 10mV + 7mV = -53mV.

3. **SNN Pattern Recognition**: An SNN is trained to recognize two patterns: a 10-spike sequence and a 5-spike sequence. Suppose the SNN has two output neurons, each corresponding to one of the patterns. If the SNN receives a 7-spike input sequence, and the similarity metric between the input and the 10-spike sequence is 0.6, and the similarity metric between the input and the 5-spike sequence is 0.4, calculate the output of each neuron. 
Given: similarity metric for 10-spike sequence = 0.6, similarity metric for 5-spike sequence = 0.4. 
Output of neuron 1 (10-spike sequence) = 0.6, Output of neuron 2 (5-spike sequence) = 0.4. The SNN will fire the first output neuron with higher probability.

## Applications

Spiking Neural Networks (SNNs) have various applications in computer science, particularly in areas that require real-time processing, low power consumption, and adaptive learning. In robotics, SNNs are used for control and navigation, enabling robots to respond to changing environments and learn from experience. For instance, SNNs can be used to implement adaptive control systems that adjust to varying sensorimotor feedback. 
In image and speech recognition, SNNs can be used to model the spatiotemporal patterns in data, allowing for more accurate and efficient processing. This is particularly useful in applications where data is streamed in real-time, such as in autonomous vehicles or smart home devices. 
SNNs are also applied in neuromorphic computing, where they are used to develop brain-inspired computing systems that mimic the structure and function of biological neurons. This has led to the development of specialized hardware, such as memristor-based chips and neuromorphic processors, which can efficiently simulate SNNs and enable low-power, real-time processing. 
Furthermore, SNNs have been used in brain-computer interfaces (BCIs) to decode neural activity and control prosthetic devices or communicate with paralyzed individuals. The adaptive and real-time processing capabilities of SNNs make them well-suited for these applications, where rapid and accurate processing of neural signals is critical. 
Overall, the unique properties of SNNs make them an attractive choice for applications that require adaptive, real-time, and low-power processing, and their use is expected to continue growing in various domains.

## Common Errors

In Spiking Neural Networks (SNNs), practitioners often make mistakes that hinder the performance and efficiency of their models. One common error is incorrect parameter initialization, particularly for the membrane potential, threshold potential, and synaptic weights. Initializing these parameters with values that are too high or too low can lead to unstable network behavior, such as neuronal saturation or silence. Another mistake is the misuse of spike timing-dependent plasticity (STDP) rules, which can result in unintended changes to synaptic weights and disrupt the learning process. Additionally, many practitioners fail to properly account for the effects of neuronal refractoriness, which can cause neurons to become unresponsive to incoming spikes and affect the overall network dynamics. Furthermore, the choice of encoding scheme, such as rate coding or temporal coding, can significantly impact the network's performance, and incorrect choices can lead to suboptimal results. Lastly, neglecting to consider the effects of synaptic delay and axonal conduction delay can lead to inaccurate simulations and poor performance in tasks that require precise timing, such as audio or video processing. These errors can be mitigated by carefully considering the underlying neuroscientific principles and testing different configurations to find the optimal setup for a given task.

# Permaculture

> The course assumes foundational knowledge and applies permaculture principles to real situations and methods.

## Principles

12 principles (Holmgren): observe and interact, catch and store energy, obtain a yield, apply self-regulation, use renewable resources, produce no waste, design from patterns to details, integrate rather than segregate, use small/slow solutions, use diversity, use edges, creatively use change. DESIGN: Zones (0-5, concentric from home), sectors (wind, sun, water, fire), keyline water harvesting, guilds, food forests. SOIL: Sheet mulching (lasagna gardening), composting, vermiculture, biochar, cover crops, chop-and-drop. WATER: Swales on contour, rain gardens, hugelkultur, keyline design (P.A. Yeomans). PLANTS: Perennial polycultures, nitrogen fixers, dynamic accumulators, companion planting. ANIMALS: Integrated animal systems (chicken tractors, rotational grazing). Aquaponics, aquaculture integration.

## Foundations

Permaculture, a term coined by Bill Mollison and David Holmgren, refers to a holistic design system for creating sustainable and regenerative agricultural ecosystems. The core definition of permaculture is based on the principles of working with nature, rather than against it. A practitioner of permaculture, known as a permaculturist, aims to design and manage agricultural systems that are diverse, resilient, and productive. Key vocabulary includes **polyculture**, which refers to the practice of growing multiple crops together, and **monoculture**, which is the practice of growing a single crop. **Agroecology** is the study of the interactions between plants, animals, and their environment, and is a fundamental concept in permaculture. **Biodiversity** refers to the variety of different species and ecosystems present in a given area. Permaculture design principles include **zones**, which refer to the division of a farm or garden into areas based on their frequency of use and intensity of management, and **sectors**, which refer to the external factors that influence a site, such as wind, sun, and water. Understanding these core definitions and principles is essential for a permaculturist to create a sustainable and regenerative agricultural ecosystem.

Permaculture, a design system in agriculture, emphasizes sustainable and regenerative practices, integrating plants, animals, and humans to create thriving ecosystems. **Ecosystem** refers to a biological community of interacting organisms and their physical environment. In permaculture, **sustainability** means meeting current needs without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own needs. **Regenerative agriculture** involves practices that enhance ecosystem services, such as soil conservation, biodiversity, and efficient water use. Key principles include **diversity**, where multiple plant and animal species coexist, and **resilience**, the ability of a system to withstand and recover from disturbances. **Polycultures**, or multiple crops grown together, mimic natural ecosystems and promote biodiversity. **Agroecology**, the study of ecological relationships in agricultural systems, underlies permaculture design. Practitioners use **holistic management**, considering the entire system and its interconnectedness, to create **closed-loop systems**, where waste is minimized and resources are cycled back into the system. Understanding these core concepts is essential for effective permaculture practice.

## Mechanisms

Permaculture operates through a set of interconnected mechanisms that work together to create a sustainable and regenerative agricultural system. The first mechanism is the design of the farm layout, which aims to maximize biodiversity and minimize waste. This is achieved by arranging plants and animals in a way that mimics natural ecosystems, with a focus on polycultures rather than monocultures. The causal chain begins with the selection of plant species that have complementary growth habits and nutrient requirements, such as the "three sisters" combination of maize, beans, and squash. As these plants grow, they provide mutual support and shade, reducing the need for external inputs like fertilizers and pesticides. The next step in the mechanism is the creation of a soil ecosystem that is rich in organic matter and beneficial microorganisms. This is achieved through the use of techniques like mulching, composting, and cover cropping, which help to build soil fertility and structure. As the soil ecosystem develops, it begins to support a diverse array of beneficial insects, pollinators, and other wildlife, which in turn contribute to the overall health and resilience of the farm. The final step in the mechanism is the integration of animals into the system, which provides a source of fertilizer, pest control, and additional income streams. By combining these different components in a holistic and integrated way, permaculture creates a self-reinforcing cycle of growth and regeneration that minimizes the need for external inputs and maximizes the overall productivity and sustainability of the farm. ## Methods And Frameworks

In permaculture, several methods and frameworks guide the design and management of agricultural systems. The Zone Model is used to organize elements in a system based on their frequency of use and need for maintenance, with zones ranging from intensively managed areas near the homestead (Zone 1) to wild, unmanaged areas (Zone 5). The Sector Analysis is applied to understand and manage external energies such as sunlight, wind, and water flow, ensuring that the design works with these forces rather than against them. The Keyline Design method is utilized for water management, aiming to maximize water retention and minimize erosion through the strategic placement of dams, swales, and irrigation channels. The Holmgren's Permaculture Principles, a set of 12 principles, provide a framework for designing regenerative and self-sufficient systems, emphasizing the importance of observing and interacting with nature, catching and storing energy, and obtaining a yield. Failure modes for these methods include inadequate observation and understanding of the site's specific conditions, insufficient diversity and redundancy in the system, and failure to adapt designs over time as conditions change. Effective application of these methods and frameworks requires a deep understanding of the local ecosystem, careful observation, and a willingness to adapt and evolve the design over time. ## Worked Examples

To illustrate the application of permaculture principles in agriculture, consider the following examples. 
1. **Polyculture Design**: A farmer has 1 hectare of land and wants to design a polyculture system with maize, beans, and squash. The maize requires 4000 kg/ha of nitrogen, beans fix 150 kg/ha of nitrogen, and squash requires 2000 kg/ha of nitrogen. If the farmer wants to minimize external nitrogen inputs, how many hectares of beans should be planted to meet the nitrogen requirements of the maize and squash? 
Let's assume a 1:1:1 ratio of maize, beans, and squash. The total nitrogen required is 4000 kg/ha (maize) + 2000 kg/ha (squash) = 6000 kg/ha. The beans fix 150 kg/ha of nitrogen. To meet the nitrogen requirements, the farmer needs 6000 kg/ha / 150 kg/ha = 40 hectares of beans, but since the total land area is 1 hectare, the farmer can allocate 1/3 of the land to beans, which will provide approximately 50 kg/ha of nitrogen, and supplement the rest with external inputs. 
2. **Water Harvesting**: A farm receives an average annual rainfall of 600 mm. The farmer wants to harvest rainwater for irrigation using a 1000 m² roof catchment. If the roof has a runoff coefficient of 0.8, how much water can be harvested in a year? 
The total rainfall collected is 600 mm * 1000 m² * 0.8 = 480,000 liters. This water can be used for irrigation, reducing the farm's reliance on external water sources. 
3. **Crop Rotation**: A farmer has a 2-hectare plot and wants to design a crop rotation system with four crops: wheat, oats, barley, and legumes. The wheat and oats require 200 kg/ha of phosphorus, barley requires 150 kg/ha of phosphorus, and legumes fix 50 kg/ha of phosphorus. If the farmer wants to maintain soil phosphorus levels, how many years should the legumes be included in the rotation cycle? 
Let's assume a 4-year rotation cycle. The total phosphorus required is 200 kg/ha (wheat) + 200 kg/ha (oats) + 150 kg/ha (barley) = 550 kg/ha. The legumes fix 50 kg/ha of phosphorus. Using the "Three Sisters" method, the farmer allocates 60% of the land to corn, 20% to beans, and 20% to squash. If the yield per hectare for corn is 8,000 kg, for beans is 2,000 kg, and for squash is 15,000 kg, calculate the total yield. The total yield would be: (0.6 * 8,000) + (0.2 * 2,000) + (0.2 * 15,000) = 4,800 + 400 + 3,000 = 8,200 kg.

With a roof catchment area of 100 square meters, and an efficiency of 80%, calculate the total volume of water that can be harvested. The total volume of water harvested would be: 600 mm * 100 m^2 * 0.8 = 0.6 m * 100 m^2 * 0.8 = 48 cubic meters.
3. **Crop Rotation and Soil Fertility**: A farmer has a 4-year crop rotation plan: Year 1 - nitrogen-fixing legumes, Year 2 - maize, Year 3 - soybeans, and Year 4 - fallow. If the legumes add 100 kg/ha of nitrogen to the soil, and the maize and soybeans deplete 50 kg/ha and 20 kg/ha respectively, calculate the net change in soil nitrogen after one rotation cycle. The net change would be: +100 kg/ha (legumes) - 50 kg/ha (maize) - 20 kg/ha (soybeans) + 0 kg/ha (fallow) = +30 kg/ha.

## Applications

Permaculture is applied in various agricultural settings to create sustainable and regenerative food systems. In practice, permaculture principles are used to design and manage farms, gardens, and orchards that mimic natural ecosystems. This involves arranging plants, animals, and infrastructure to maximize biodiversity, reduce waste, and promote efficient use of resources. For example, companion planting is used to enhance growth and reduce pests, while polycultures and agroforestry systems are employed to promote ecological interactions and increase yields. Permaculture design also incorporates techniques such as keyline design, which optimizes water harvesting and distribution, and zone planning, which organizes elements based on their frequency of use and maintenance needs. Additionally, permaculture is used in urban agriculture, where it informs the design of community gardens, rooftop gardens, and other forms of intensive food production. By applying permaculture principles, farmers and gardeners can create resilient and productive food systems that prioritize soil health, conserve water, and promote ecological balance.

## Common Errors

In permaculture, a design system for sustainable and self-sufficient agriculture, several common mistakes can undermine the effectiveness and resilience of a permaculture design. One error is the failure to conduct thorough site analysis, leading to inadequate understanding of the site's microclimate, soil conditions, and hydrology. This can result in misplaced elements, such as planting water-loving species in dry areas or vice versa. Another mistake is the over-reliance on exotic or non-native species, which can outcompete native species, disrupt local ecosystems, and require more maintenance. Additionally, some practitioners neglect to implement effective water harvesting and conservation strategies, leading to water scarcity and reduced system productivity. Incorrect zone placement is also a common error, where elements are placed in zones that do not match their maintenance or access requirements, leading to inefficiency and reduced productivity. Furthermore, failure to consider the principle of stacking, where multiple functions are assigned to each element, can result in a design that is not maximally efficient or resilient. These mistakes can be avoided by carefully applying permaculture principles, such as observing and interacting with the site, catching and storing energy, and applying self-regulation and accepting feedback. Additionally, some practitioners neglect to implement a diverse and layered polyculture, instead relying on monocultures that are more susceptible to pests, diseases, and climate variability. The lack of consideration for succession and the dynamic nature of ecosystems can also lead to designs that are not adaptable to changing conditions. Furthermore, the failure to integrate animals into the design can result in missed opportunities for nutrient cycling, pest control, and soil improvement.

Open questions in the field include the development of scalable and economically viable permaculture systems, as well as the integration of permaculture principles with other sustainable agriculture approaches, such as organic and biodynamic farming. Furthermore, the application of permaculture in urban and peri-urban areas is an emerging area of study, with a focus on designing productive and resilient food systems for cities. The field is moving towards a greater emphasis on social and economic aspects of permaculture, including issues of food justice, equity, and community engagement. Additionally, the use of digital tools and technologies, such as geographic information systems (GIS) and precision agriculture, is being explored to support permaculture design and management. Overall, advanced studies in permaculture aim to deepen our understanding of the complex interactions between agricultural ecosystems, society, and the environment, and to develop innovative solutions for a more sustainable food future.

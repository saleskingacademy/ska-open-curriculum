# Causal Inference Ml

> The course teaches core concepts and principles of causal inference in machine learning, assuming some basic knowledge of statistics and machine learning.

## Foundations

Causal inference in machine learning (ML) relies on understanding the relationships between variables. A **variable** is a characteristic or attribute that can take on different values. In causal inference, we distinguish between **treatment variables** (the variables we manipulate or intervene on) and **outcome variables** (the variables we measure in response to the treatment). The **treatment effect** is the change in the outcome variable resulting from a change in the treatment variable. **Confounding variables** are external factors that affect both the treatment and outcome variables, potentially leading to biased estimates of the treatment effect if not properly controlled. **Covariates** are variables that are related to the outcome variable but not directly affected by the treatment. **Counterfactuals** refer to the potential outcomes that would have occurred if a different treatment had been applied. The **structural causal model** (SCM) is a mathematical framework used to represent the causal relationships between variables, consisting of a set of equations that describe how each variable is generated based on its parents in the causal graph. The **causal graph** is a visual representation of the causal relationships between variables, where nodes represent variables and directed edges represent causal effects. Understanding these core concepts is essential for a practitioner to design and interpret causal inference studies in ML.

In Causal Inference ML, a subfield of machine learning, the core definitions and first principles are rooted in understanding cause-and-effect relationships. **Causal inference** refers to the process of drawing conclusions about the causal relationships between variables. **Causal relationships** imply that changes in one variable (the **cause** or **treatment**) affect the value of another variable (the **effect** or **outcome**). The **treatment effect** is the difference in outcomes between the treatment and control groups. A **confounder** is a variable that affects both the treatment and the outcome, potentially biasing the estimated treatment effect. **Conditional independence** occurs when two variables are independent given a third variable. The **do-operator**, denoted as **do(X=x)**, represents an intervention that sets the value of variable X to x. **Interventions** are actions that intentionally change the value of a variable to study its effect. Understanding these foundational concepts is crucial for a practitioner to design and analyze causal inference studies in machine learning. **Confounding variables** are extraneous variables that affect both the treatment and outcome, potentially misleading causal inferences if not accounted for.

## Mechanisms

Causal inference in machine learning involves a series of steps to establish cause-and-effect relationships between variables. The process begins with data collection, where relevant variables are identified and measured. Next, a causal graph is constructed, representing the relationships between variables as a directed acyclic graph (DAG). Each node in the graph represents a variable, and the edges represent the causal relationships between them. The causal chain is made explicit by identifying the treatment (cause) and outcome (effect) variables. The do-operator is then applied to intervene on the treatment variable, simulating the effect of setting the treatment to a specific value. The resulting causal effect is estimated using statistical methods, such as regression or propensity scoring. The causal inference algorithm then adjusts for confounding variables, which can affect both the treatment and outcome, to ensure that the estimated effect is due to the treatment alone. Finally, the algorithm outputs the estimated causal effect, providing insight into the cause-and-effect relationship between the treatment and outcome variables.

Causal inference in machine learning (ML) involves a series of steps to establish cause-and-effect relationships between variables. The causal chain is made explicit through the following mechanisms: 
1. **Data Collection**: Gathering data on the variables of interest, including the treatment (cause) and outcome (effect) variables. 
2. **Causal Graph Construction**: Creating a directed acyclic graph (DAG) to represent the causal relationships between variables. The DAG encodes the causal structure, with nodes representing variables and edges representing direct causal effects. 
3. **Identification**: Identifying the causal effect of interest, which involves determining whether the effect is identifiable from the observed data. This step ensures that the causal query can be answered using the available data. 
4. **Adjustment for Confounding**: Controlling for confounding variables that affect both the treatment and outcome variables, to prevent biased estimates of the causal effect. Techniques such as stratification, matching, or regression adjustment are used to adjust for confounding. 
5. **Interventional Distribution**: Computing the interventional distribution, which represents the distribution of the outcome variable when the treatment variable is intervened upon. This step involves simulating the effect of setting the treatment variable to a specific value. 
6. **Causal Effect Estimation**: Estimating the causal effect using the interventional distribution, which provides the average treatment effect (ATE) or other causal effect measures. 
These mechanisms work together to establish a causal chain, allowing ML models to infer cause-and-effect relationships from data and make predictions about the outcomes of interventions. This allows for the estimation of causal effects, such as the average treatment effect (ATE) or the conditional average treatment effect (CATE). The causal inference mechanism relies on assumptions, including consistency, ignorability, and overlap, to ensure that the estimated effects are unbiased. By analyzing the causal graph and applying the do-operator, ML algorithms can infer causal relationships and predict the outcomes of interventions, enabling informed decision-making.

## Methods And Frameworks

Causal inference in machine learning employs various methods to establish cause-and-effect relationships. The Potential Outcomes Framework is used to estimate causal effects by comparing outcomes under different interventions. Instrumental Variables (IV) analysis is applied when a causal relationship is confounded, using an instrumental variable to identify the causal effect. The IV estimator is given by the formula: E[Y|do(X)] = E[Y|X] / E[X|Z], where Y is the outcome, X is the treatment, and Z is the instrumental variable. 
The Structural Causal Model (SCM) framework represents causal relationships using directed acyclic graphs (DAGs) and structural equations, allowing for the estimation of causal effects through do-calculus. 
The failure mode of these methods often arises from incorrect assumptions about the underlying causal structure or the presence of unmeasured confounding variables. 
The Additive Noise Model (ANM) assumes that the effect of the cause on the effect is additive, and is used when the causal relationship is linear. 
The Identifiability Formula is used to determine whether a causal effect is identifiable from observational data, and is given by: P(Y|do(X)) = ∑[P(Y|X, U) * P(U)], where U represents the unmeasured confounding variables. 
Each of these methods has its own strengths and limitations, and the choice of method depends on the specific problem and data at hand.

Causal inference in machine learning employs several methods and frameworks to estimate causal effects. The Instrumental Variable (IV) method is used when a causal link is confounded by unmeasured variables, but an instrumental variable is available that affects the treatment and not the outcome directly. The IV method involves a two-stage least squares (2SLS) regression, where the first stage predicts the treatment using the instrumental variable, and the second stage predicts the outcome using the predicted treatment. 
The Structural Causal Model (SCM) framework represents causal relationships using directed acyclic graphs (DAGs) and provides a foundation for causal inference. The do-calculus, introduced by Judea Pearl, is a mathematical framework for causal inference that allows for the identification of causal effects from observational data. 
The Potential Outcomes framework, also known as the Neyman-Rubin causal model, estimates causal effects by comparing the potential outcomes under different treatments. This framework is useful when the treatment is binary and the outcome is continuous. 
Failure modes of these methods include model misspecification, where the assumed causal structure is incorrect, and confounding bias, where unmeasured variables affect both the treatment and the outcome. Regularization techniques, such as L1 and L2 regularization, can help mitigate overfitting in causal models.

## Worked Examples

To illustrate the concepts of causal inference in machine learning, consider the following examples. 
1. **Inferring Treatment Effects**: Suppose we have a dataset of users who either received a new recommendation algorithm (treatment group) or the old one (control group). The outcome variable is the number of purchases made. If we observe that the treatment group has an average of 5 purchases and the control group has an average of 3 purchases, the difference (5 - 3 = 2) represents the average treatment effect (ATE) on the number of purchases. However, to ensure this effect is causal, we must control for confounding variables such as user demographics and previous purchase history.
2. **Controlling for Confounders**: In a study examining the effect of a new educational software on student test scores, we might find that students using the software have higher scores. However, if these students also tend to have more educated parents, we must control for parental education level to isolate the effect of the software. Using a technique like propensity scoring, we can match students with similar parental education levels across both the treatment and control groups, thereby reducing confounding.
3. **Instrumental Variables**: Suppose we want to estimate the causal effect of the number of hours spent studying on exam scores, but there's a confounder (e.g., innate ability) that affects both study time and exam scores. If we find an instrumental variable (e.g., being assigned a particularly motivating teacher) that affects study time but not directly exam scores (except through study time), we can use this to identify the causal effect. For instance, if having a motivating teacher increases study time by 2 hours on average and this increase is associated with a 10-point increase in exam scores, we can infer that each additional hour of study time causally increases exam scores by 5 points.

1. **Inferring Treatment Effect**: Suppose we want to estimate the effect of a new drug on blood pressure. We have a dataset of 100 patients, with 50 treated and 50 untreated. The average blood pressure reduction in the treated group is 10 mmHg, and in the untreated group is 2 mmHg. However, the treated group has an average age of 55, while the untreated group has an average age of 45. Using causal inference techniques, such as propensity scoring, we can adjust for the age difference and estimate the treatment effect.

2. **Identifying Causal Relationships**: Consider a dataset of website user interactions, where we want to determine if the number of ads displayed causes users to leave the site. We collect data on the number of ads displayed, user engagement (e.g., time spent on site), and whether the user left the site. Using causal inference algorithms, such as causal additive models, we can analyze the data and determine if there is a causal relationship between the number of ads and user departure.

3. **Controlling for Confounding Variables**: Suppose we want to investigate the relationship between hours studied and exam scores. However, we also know that students from higher-income families tend to have more resources and thus may study more. Using causal inference techniques, such as instrumental variables, we can control for the confounding variable of family income and estimate the causal effect of hours studied on exam scores. For instance, if we find that an additional hour of study increases exam scores by 5 points, while controlling for family income, we can conclude that studying has a positive causal effect on exam performance.

We want to estimate the average treatment effect (ATE) of the new method. Let's say we have 100 students in the treatment group with an average score of 80 and 100 students in the control group with an average score of 75. However, the treatment group has an average prior score of 78, while the control group has an average prior score of 72. Using the backdoor adjustment formula, we can estimate the ATE by adjusting for the prior score, a confounding variable.

2. **Identifying Confounding Variables**: In a study on the effect of a new medication on blood pressure, we collect data on patients' age, gender, medication intake, and blood pressure. To apply causal inference, we need to identify confounding variables. Age and gender could be confounders because they might affect both the likelihood of being prescribed the medication and blood pressure. By using techniques like regression analysis or causal graphs, we can determine if these variables indeed confound the relationship between medication intake and blood pressure.

An instrumental variable (IV) could be the availability of a gym membership, which affects exercise but not directly weight loss. If we have data on gym membership, exercise hours, and weight loss for a group of individuals, we can use the IV method to estimate the causal effect of exercise on weight loss, under certain assumptions about the IV's validity.

In healthcare, causal inference is used to identify the causal effects of treatments on patient outcomes, such as determining the effectiveness of a new medication. In economics, causal inference is applied to analyze the impact of policy interventions, such as the effect of a tax reform on economic growth. In recommender systems, causal inference helps to identify the causal effects of recommendations on user behavior, enabling the development of more effective personalized recommendation algorithms. ## Common Errors

In Causal Inference ML, practitioners often make mistakes that can lead to incorrect conclusions. One common error is ignoring confounding variables, which can create spurious correlations between the treatment and outcome. For instance, if a model is designed to estimate the effect of a new medication on patient outcomes without accounting for the patient's pre-existing conditions, the results may be biased. Another mistake is reverse causality, where the outcome is mistakenly assumed to be the cause. This can occur when the model is not properly temporal, failing to account for the direction of causality. Additionally, omitting relevant variables or including irrelevant ones can lead to biased estimates. Overreliance on statistical significance rather than effect size is also a common error, as it can lead to the identification of meaningless relationships. Furthermore, failing to consider the causal graph and the resulting identifiability of the causal effects can lead to incorrect model specifications. These errors can be mitigated by carefully considering the underlying causal mechanisms, using techniques such as instrumental variables, and performing thorough sensitivity analyses. These errors can be mitigated by carefully considering the study design, data collection, and causal assumptions underlying the model. This can occur when using regression analysis without considering the causal direction. Furthermore, failing to consider the causal graph or structural causal model can result in incorrect identification of causal relationships.

## Advanced

In causal inference machine learning, advanced topics include the integration of causal discovery with deep learning, leveraging techniques such as causal variational autoencoders and causal generative adversarial networks to learn causal representations from high-dimensional data. Another key area is the development of transfer learning and meta-learning methods for causal inference, enabling models to adapt to new environments and populations. Open questions in the field include the development of robust causal inference methods for high-dimensional and non-stationary data, as well as the integration of causal inference with other machine learning paradigms, such as reinforcement learning and imitation learning. Researchers are also exploring the application of causal inference to real-world problems, such as fairness and transparency in AI decision-making, and the development of causal inference frameworks for time-series and longitudinal data. Furthermore, there is a growing interest in the development of causal inference methods that can handle non-parametric and non-linear relationships, as well as the use of causal inference for explaining and interpreting machine learning models. The field is moving towards the development of more robust, scalable, and interpretable causal inference methods, with applications in areas such as healthcare, economics, and social sciences.

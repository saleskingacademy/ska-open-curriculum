# Hospitality Law

> The course applies legal principles to real situations in the hospitality industry, requiring some foundational knowledge of law and business.

## Innkeeper

Common law duty to receive guests. Right to exclude (trespass, disorderly conduct). Innkeeper's lien. LIABILITY: Guest property (limited liability statutes, safe deposit), personal injury (slip and fall, negligence), food safety (strict liability). CONTRACTS: Reservation agreements, meeting space contracts, block agreements, attrition clauses, cancellation policies, force majeure. EMPLOYMENT: FLSA (tip credit, overtime), EEOC (discrimination, harassment), ADA (accommodation), OSHA (workplace safety). Immigration (I-9 verification). LICENSING: Liquor license (dram shop liability, server training), food service permits, business permits, occupancy permits. INTELLECTUAL PROPERTY: Trademark (brand protection), franchise agreements. DATA: PCI DSS (payment card security), privacy (guest data), GDPR for international guests.

## Methods And Frameworks

In Hospitality Law, several methods and frameworks are employed to analyze and resolve legal issues. The Risk Management Framework is used to identify, assess, and mitigate potential risks in hospitality operations, such as slip-and-fall accidents or foodborne illnesses. The Duty of Care model is applied to determine the standard of care owed to guests, and when to use it is crucial in cases of negligence or liability. The Reasonable Person Test formula is utilized to assess whether a hospitality provider has acted reasonably in a given situation, and its failure mode occurs when the test is applied too subjectively. The vicarious liability doctrine is used to determine when an employer is liable for the actions of an employee, and its failure mode arises when the employer-employee relationship is not clearly established. The principle of strict liability is applied in cases where a hospitality provider is liable for harm caused, regardless of fault, and its failure mode occurs when the harm is not directly related to the provider's actions. Understanding these methods and frameworks is essential in Hospitality Law to ensure that hospitality providers are aware of their legal obligations and can take steps to minimize potential liabilities. ## Worked Examples

A hotel guest checks into a room with a rate of $150 per night and stays for 3 nights, but checks out a day early due to a family emergency. If the hotel has a 24-hour cancellation policy and the guest did not provide adequate notice, calculate the amount the guest owes. 
First, calculate the total cost of the stay: $150 * 3 = $450. Since the guest stayed for 2 nights, they owe for those nights: $150 * 2 = $300. The hotel may charge for the third night due to the cancellation policy, so the total owed is $300 + $150 = $450.
In another scenario, a restaurant patron orders a meal that costs $25, but the patron sends it back to the kitchen due to a mistake with the order. If the restaurant has a policy of not charging for mistakes, calculate the amount the patron owes. 
The patron owes $0 for the meal since it was a mistake, but they may still owe for any drinks or other items ordered. 
A tour operator offers a package deal for $800 per person, including transportation, accommodations, and meals. If 20 people sign up for the tour, but 2 people cancel at the last minute due to illness, calculate the amount the tour operator owes in refunds. 
First, calculate the total revenue: $800 * 20 = $16,000. Since 2 people cancelled, the tour operator owes 2 * $800 = $1,600 in refunds. The tour operator may deduct any non-refundable expenses, such as transportation or accommodation costs, from the refund amount.

In hospitality law, understanding the application of legal principles to real-world scenarios is crucial. Here are three worked examples:

1. **Liability for Guest Injuries**: A hotel guest slips on a wet floor in the lobby and suffers a broken ankle, resulting in $10,000 in medical expenses. If the hotel had failed to display warning signs or clean up the spill in a timely manner, they may be liable for the guest's injuries. Using the principle of negligence, the hotel's liability can be calculated as follows: Damages ($10,000) x Percentage of Fault (e.g., 80% if the hotel is found to be primarily responsible) = $8,000.

2. **Contractual Disputes**: A catering company agrees to provide services for a wedding reception at a hotel for $5,000. However, the hotel cancels the event due to unforeseen circumstances, such as a fire. If the contract does not have a force majeure clause, the catering company may be entitled to claim damages for breach of contract. The amount of damages would depend on the catering company's losses, which could include preparation costs and lost profit, potentially up to the full $5,000. However, the catering company fails to deliver the agreed-upon services, resulting in a $2,000 loss for the hotel. Using the principle of breach of contract, the hotel can claim damages from the catering company. The calculation would be: Contract Value ($5,000) - Amount Paid ($3,000) = $2,000 in damages.

3. **Employment Law**: A restaurant employee is terminated after refusing to work on a Sunday, citing religious reasons. If the employee can prove that the employer did not make reasonable accommodations for their religious beliefs, the employee may have a claim for discrimination under employment laws. Assuming the employee's average weekly earnings were $800, and they were unable to find new employment for 4 weeks, they may be entitled to $3,200 in back pay, plus potential damages for discrimination.

3. **Employment Law**: A hotel employee is terminated after refusing to work on a scheduled day off, citing the Fair Labor Standards Act (FLSA). If the employee is entitled to overtime pay and was not provided with adequate notice, the hotel may be liable for back pay and penalties. Using the principle of employment law, the calculation would be: Back Pay (e.g., $1,000) + Penalties (e.g., $500) = $1,500 in total liability.

## Common Errors

In the context of hospitality law, practitioners often make mistakes that can lead to legal and financial repercussions. One common error is the failure to properly document and communicate hotel policies and procedures to guests, which can result in disputes over issues such as room rates, cancellations, and refunds. Another mistake is the inadequate training of staff on laws related to accessibility, disability, and discrimination, leading to potential claims of non-compliance with regulations such as the Americans with Disabilities Act (ADA). Additionally, hospitality establishments often incorrectly assume that they are not liable for injuries or damages sustained by guests on their premises, when in fact, they have a duty of care to ensure the safety and well-being of their patrons. Furthermore, the misuse of contractual terms and conditions, such as unfair or deceptive practices, can also lead to legal issues and damage to the establishment's reputation. These errors can be attributed to a lack of understanding of the relevant laws and regulations, inadequate staff training, and insufficient risk management practices.

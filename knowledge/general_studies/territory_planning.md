# Territory Planning

> The course teaches core principles and fundamental concepts of territory planning, assuming no prior study but requiring understanding of key terminology and vocabulary.

## Overview

Sales discipline: territory planning. Practical field technique for professional selling.

## Foundations

Territory planning refers to the process of dividing a geographic area into distinct regions, known as territories, to facilitate efficient allocation of resources, management, and control. A territory is a defined geographic area, bounded by physical or administrative boundaries, such as streets, roads, or jurisdictional limits. Geographic information systems (GIS) are often utilized in territory planning to analyze and visualize spatial data, including demographic, economic, and environmental factors. 
Core definitions include: 
- Spatial analysis: the process of examining and interpreting geographic data to identify patterns, relationships, and trends. 
- Administrative boundaries: officially designated limits, such as city or county borders, that define the extent of a territory. 
- Resource allocation: the assignment and distribution of resources, including personnel, equipment, and funding, to support territorial management and operations. 
First principles of territory planning involve understanding the interplay between geographic, demographic, and economic factors, and applying this knowledge to create functional, manageable territories that support organizational objectives. Key vocabulary includes: 
- Cartography: the science and practice of creating maps to represent geographic data and relationships. 
- Geocoding: the process of assigning geographic coordinates to specific locations or addresses, enabling spatial analysis and mapping. 
- Thematic mapping: a type of cartography that uses maps to display specific themes or topics, such as population density or economic activity. 
Practitioners must be familiar with these concepts and terminology to develop and implement effective territory planning strategies.

## Mechanisms

Territory planning involves a series of steps that work together to achieve effective allocation and management of geographic areas. The process begins with data collection, where relevant information about the territory is gathered, including demographic data, market trends, and geographical features. This data is then analyzed to identify patterns, opportunities, and challenges, which informs the development of planning objectives. These objectives are typically aligned with organizational goals, such as increasing sales or improving customer service. The next step involves dividing the territory into manageable segments, often using geographic information systems (GIS) to create visual representations of the area. Each segment is then evaluated based on its potential value, taking into account factors such as population density, competition, and accessibility. The causal chain is as follows: data collection informs analysis, analysis informs objective development, objectives guide segmentation, and segmentation enables the evaluation and prioritization of areas. This, in turn, leads to the allocation of resources, such as assigning sales representatives to specific territories or targeting marketing efforts towards high-potential areas. Ultimately, the effective implementation of territory planning mechanisms enables organizations to optimize their territorial coverage, leading to improved efficiency, increased revenue, and enhanced customer satisfaction.

## Methods And Frameworks

The Central Place Theory is utilized to understand the hierarchical structure of urban settlements, with larger cities offering more services and goods. It is useful for regional planning, but its failure mode lies in its oversimplification of complex urban systems. 
The Location Quotient (LQ) formula, LQ = (Local Employment in Industry / Total Local Employment) / (National Employment in Industry / Total National Employment), is employed to analyze the concentration of industries in a specific area. It is helpful for identifying cluster formations, but its failure mode occurs when the data is not standardized or when the industry classification is too broad. 
The Spatial Autocorrelation (SA) method, using formulas such as Moran's I, is applied to measure the similarity of neighboring values in a spatial dataset. It is useful for identifying patterns and clusters, but its failure mode arises when the data is not spatially dependent or when the chosen distance band is incorrect. 
The Urban Growth Boundary (UGB) model is used to manage urban expansion by designating a boundary beyond which development is restricted. It is effective for controlling sprawl, but its failure mode occurs when the boundary is not regularly updated or when it is not accompanied by complementary policies. 
The Mixed-Use Development (MUD) framework is utilized to promote compact, walkable, and diverse urban environments. It is beneficial for reducing transportation costs and increasing social interaction, but its failure mode lies in its potential for gentrification and increased housing costs if not properly managed.

If the company wants to divide the area into equal-sized territories with approximately 100 clients per representative, the territory size would be 50 square miles per representative. This calculation is based on the principle of dividing the total area by the number of representatives, ensuring each has a manageable and equal-sized territory.

2. **Resource Allocation**: A local government needs to allocate 20 community centers across a territory of 200 square miles with a population of 400,000 residents. Using the principle of allocating resources based on population density, the government can divide the territory into sections with approximately 20,000 residents per community center. This ensures that resources are distributed fairly and accessible to the population.

3. **Zoning Regulations**: A city is planning to develop a new residential area of 100 acres with zoning regulations requiring 20% of the land for parks and recreational facilities. To apply this regulation, the city would allocate 20 acres for parks, ensuring that the development meets the zoning requirements. This example demonstrates the principle of applying zoning regulations to territory planning, balancing development with community needs.

## Common Errors

The mistakes practitioners actually make in territory planning often stem from inadequate analysis of spatial data, leading to inefficient allocation of resources. One common error is the failure to account for geographic barriers, such as rivers or highways, which can significantly impact travel times and access to markets. Another mistake is ignoring the modifiable areal unit problem (MAUP), where the aggregation of data into larger or smaller units can lead to different conclusions. Practitioners also often overlook the importance of scale, where plans developed at one scale may not be applicable at another. Furthermore, the assumption of uniform distribution of resources or population within a territory is a frequent mistake, as it neglects the complexities of real-world spatial patterns. Additionally, the lack of consideration for dynamic changes, such as population growth or economic shifts, can render territory plans obsolete. These errors can result in suboptimal resource allocation, decreased efficiency, and reduced effectiveness of territory planning initiatives.

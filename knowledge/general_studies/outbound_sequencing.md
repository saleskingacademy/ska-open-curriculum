# Outbound Sequencing

> The course assumes some basic knowledge of marketing and sales concepts, but still focuses on core principles and key terminology of outbound sequencing.

## Overview

Marketing discipline: outbound sequencing. Demand creation and conversion for revenue teams.

## Foundations

Outbound sequencing refers to the process of ordering and organizing a series of outbound interactions, such as phone calls, emails, or messages, to achieve a specific goal, typically in a sales, marketing, or customer success context. A **sequence** is a predetermined series of steps or actions designed to accomplish a particular objective. **Outbound** denotes the initiation of communication from an organization to a customer or prospect, as opposed to **inbound**, where the customer initiates contact.

Key terms include **touch**, which refers to a single interaction or communication, such as a phone call or email, and **cadence**, which describes the rhythm or pattern of touches within a sequence. A **practitioner** is an individual responsible for designing, executing, and optimizing outbound sequences, often using specialized software or tools. **Sequencing rules** are the logical conditions that determine the flow of a sequence, such as triggers, delays, and branching logic. Understanding these core definitions and principles is essential for effective outbound sequencing.

## Mechanisms

Outbound sequencing relies on a series of coordinated steps to ensure that data packets are transmitted efficiently and in the correct order. The process begins with the segmentation of data into smaller packets, each assigned a sequence number. This sequence number is crucial as it allows the receiving end to reassemble the packets in the original order. The packets are then passed through a buffer, where they are stored temporarily before being transmitted. The buffer plays a key role in managing the flow of packets, preventing congestion, and ensuring that packets are not lost during transmission. Once the packets are transmitted, the receiving end acknowledges their receipt through acknowledgement packets (ACKs). The sender uses these ACKs to determine which packets have been successfully received and which need to be retransmitted. This feedback loop is essential for maintaining the integrity of the data transmission. The causal chain is as follows: data segmentation → packet sequencing → buffering → transmission → acknowledgement → retransmission (if necessary). This step-by-step process ensures that data is transmitted reliably and in the correct order, which is fundamental to outbound sequencing. The principle of using sequence numbers and ACKs allows for the detection of packet loss, duplication, or corruption, enabling corrective actions to be taken to maintain data integrity.

## Methods And Frameworks

Outbound sequencing employs several methods and frameworks to optimize the order of tasks, activities, or processes. The Program Evaluation and Review Technique (PERT) is used for complex, time-sensitive projects, where it helps identify critical tasks and allocate resources accordingly. However, its failure mode lies in its sensitivity to estimation errors, which can lead to inaccurate project timelines. 
The Critical Path Method (CPM) is applied to projects with interdependent tasks, allowing for the determination of the longest stretch of dependent activities and the minimum time required to complete a project. Its failure mode is the assumption of constant activity durations, which can be unrealistic in dynamic environments. 
The Theory of Constraints (TOC) is utilized to identify and manage bottlenecks in processes, focusing on maximizing throughput by optimizing the constraint. Its failure mode is the potential for local optima, where improving a single constraint does not necessarily lead to a global optimum. 
The First-In-First-Out (FIFO) method is used for simple, repetitive processes, where tasks are executed in the order they are received. Its failure mode lies in its inability to adapt to changing priorities or task dependencies. 
The Last-In-First-Out (LIFO) method is applied to processes where the most recent task has the highest priority, such as in stack-based algorithms. Its failure mode is the potential for starvation, where older tasks are perpetually delayed in favor of newer ones. 
The Shortest Job First (SJF) method is used for processes with varying task durations, where the task with the shortest duration is executed first. Its failure mode is the potential for process starvation, where longer tasks are consistently delayed. 
The Priority Scheduling method is applied to processes with tasks of varying priority, where tasks are executed based on their priority level. Its failure mode lies in the potential for priority inversion, where a higher-priority task is delayed due to a lower-priority task holding a shared resource. 
Each method has its strengths and weaknesses, and the choice of method depends on the specific requirements and constraints of the process or project.

## Worked Examples

Outbound sequencing involves determining the optimal order of tasks to minimize delays and maximize efficiency. Here are three concrete worked problems:

1. A manufacturing plant produces three products: A, B, and C. The production times are 5, 10, and 15 minutes respectively. The plant operates for 8 hours a day, and the products are produced in batches. If the daily demand for products A, B, and C are 100, 50, and 20 units respectively, and the setup time between batches is 30 minutes, what is the optimal sequence to minimize the total production time?

To solve this, we calculate the total production time for each product: 100A + 50B + 20C = 100(5) + 50(10) + 20(15) = 500 + 500 + 300 = 1300 minutes. Considering the setup time, the optimal sequence is to produce the products with the longest production time first, so the sequence is C-B-A.

2. A call center has three types of calls: sales, customer service, and technical support. The average handling times are 10, 15, and 20 minutes respectively. If the call center receives 200 sales calls, 150 customer service calls, and 100 technical support calls per day, and the agents are available for 7 hours a day, what is the optimal sequence to minimize the total handling time?

To solve this, we calculate the total handling time for each type of call: 200(10) + 150(15) + 100(20) = 2000 + 2250 + 2000 = 6250 minutes. Considering the average handling times, the optimal sequence is to handle the calls with the shortest handling time first, so the sequence is sales-customer service-technical support.

3. A logistics company has three trucks with different capacities: 5, 10, and 15 tons. The company needs to transport 50, 30, and 20 tons of cargo respectively. If the loading and unloading times are 30 minutes each, and the travel time between locations is 1 hour, what is the optimal sequence to minimize the total transportation time?

To solve this, we calculate the total transportation time for each truck: 50/5 + 30/10 + 20/15 = 10 + 3 + 1.33 = 14.33 trips. Considering the loading and unloading times, and the travel time, the optimal sequence is to transport the cargo with the largest capacity first, so the sequence is 15-10-5 tons.

## Common Errors

In outbound sequencing, practitioners often make mistakes that can lead to decreased efficiency, reduced customer engagement, and ultimately, lower conversion rates. One common error is incorrect lead prioritization, where practitioners prioritize leads based on arbitrary criteria rather than data-driven insights. This can result in high-value leads being neglected, while low-value leads receive unnecessary attention. Another mistake is inadequate personalization, where practitioners fail to tailor their messaging and content to the specific needs and preferences of each lead. This can lead to generic, unengaging communications that fail to resonate with potential customers. Additionally, practitioners may make the error of insufficient follow-up, where they fail to maintain consistent contact with leads, allowing them to go cold and resulting in missed opportunities. Furthermore, incorrect timing of follow-ups can also be detrimental, as contacting leads too frequently can be perceived as spammy, while contacting them too infrequently can lead to forgotten interest. Practitioners must also avoid the mistake of not tracking and analyzing key performance indicators (KPIs), such as email open rates, response rates, and conversion rates, as this data is essential for refining and optimizing outbound sequencing strategies. By understanding these common errors, practitioners can take steps to avoid them and develop more effective outbound sequencing approaches.

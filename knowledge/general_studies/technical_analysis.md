# Technical Analysis

> The course teaches core concepts and principles of technical analysis, assuming no prior study but requiring understanding of key terminology and vocabulary.

## Foundations

Technical analysis is a method of evaluating securities by analyzing statistical patterns and trends in their price movements and other market-related data. A practitioner of technical analysis is known as a technical analyst or chartist. The core definitions include: 
**Trend**, a pattern of price movement in a particular direction over time, which can be **uptrend** (increasing prices), **downtrend** (decreasing prices), or **sideways trend** (stable prices). 
**Support** refers to a price level at which buying interest is strong enough to prevent the price from falling further, while **resistance** is a price level at which selling interest is strong enough to prevent the price from rising further. 
**Chart patterns** are formations that appear on price charts, such as **head and shoulders**, **triangles**, and **wedges**, which can indicate potential trend reversals or continuations. 
**Indicators** are mathematical calculations based on price and volume data, such as **moving averages** and **relative strength index (RSI)**, used to identify trends, predict price movements, and gauge market sentiment. 
**Candlestick charts** are a type of price chart that displays the high, low, open, and close prices for a security over a specific period, providing a visual representation of price action. 
Understanding these core definitions and principles is essential for a technical analyst to interpret market data and make informed investment decisions.

## Mechanisms

Technical analysis operates through a series of steps that analyze past market data, primarily price and volume, to predict future market behavior. The process begins with data collection, where historical price and volume information is gathered from financial markets. This data is then used to identify patterns and trends, which are the core of technical analysis. Charting is a key mechanism, where data is visualized to help identify these patterns, such as trends, reversals, and continuations. Indicators and oscillators, such as moving averages and the Relative Strength Index (RSI), are applied to this data to further analyze market conditions and predict potential future movements. The causal chain is as follows: past price and volume data influence the formation of patterns and trends, which in turn affect the calculations of indicators and oscillators. These indicators then provide signals that guide investment decisions, under the assumption that historical market patterns will repeat themselves. The principle behind this mechanism is that markets are driven by supply and demand, and by analyzing the manifestations of these forces (price and volume), technicians can gain insights into future market movements.

Technical analysis operates on the premise that market prices reflect all available information, and by analyzing historical price movements and patterns, traders can identify trends and make informed investment decisions. The step-by-step mechanism involves: (1) Data collection: gathering historical price and trading volume data for a security. (2) Chart construction: plotting this data on charts to visualize price movements and patterns. (3) Pattern recognition: identifying specific patterns such as trends, reversals, and continuations, which are believed to indicate future price movements. (4) Indicator application: using technical indicators like moving averages, relative strength index (RSI), and Bollinger Bands to confirm patterns and generate buy/sell signals. (5) Signal generation: when a pattern or indicator reaches a specific condition, a buy or sell signal is generated. (6) Trade execution: the trader executes the trade based on the signal, with the goal of profiting from the anticipated price movement. The causal chain is as follows: historical price data influences chart patterns, which in turn influence indicator values, leading to signal generation, and ultimately, trade execution. This mechanism relies on the principles of market efficiency and the belief that prices move in trends and patterns that can be identified and exploited.

## Methods And Frameworks

Technical analysis in economics finance utilizes various methods and frameworks to analyze and predict market trends. The Relative Strength Index (RSI) is used to identify overbought and oversold conditions, with readings above 70 indicating overbought and below 30 indicating oversold. The Moving Average Convergence Divergence (MACD) model is employed to identify trends and potential buy or sell signals, with a crossover above the signal line indicating a buy signal and below indicating a sell signal. The Bollinger Bands framework is used to measure volatility, with prices moving outside the bands indicating a potential trend reversal. The Fibonacci Retracement model is used to identify potential support and resistance levels, with 23.6%, 38.2%, and 61.8% being common retracement levels. Each method has its failure mode, such as the RSI's failure to account for sudden changes in market sentiment, the MACD's lagging indicator nature, and the Bollinger Bands' sensitivity to parameter settings. Understanding these methods and their limitations is crucial for effective technical analysis.

## Worked Examples

To illustrate the application of technical analysis in economics finance, consider the following examples. 
1. **Trend Identification**: Suppose we have the daily closing prices of a stock: $50, $52, $55, $58, $60. To identify the trend, we calculate the differences between consecutive prices: +$2, +$3, +$3, +$2. Since all differences are positive, we conclude that the stock is in an uptrend. 
2. **Moving Average Crossover**: Given a stock's prices over 5 days: $40, $42, $45, $48, $50, we calculate the 3-day moving average: ($40 + $42 + $45)/3 = $42.33, ($42 + $45 + $48)/3 = $45, ($45 + $48 + $50)/3 = $47.67. If the current price ($50) is greater than the moving average ($47.67), we generate a buy signal. 
3. **Relative Strength Index (RSI)**: With the following daily gains and losses: +$5, -$3, +$2, -$1, +$4, we calculate the average gain and loss over 4 days: average gain = (+$5 + $2 + $4)/3 = $3.67, average loss = (-$3 - $1)/2 = -$2. The RSI = 100 - (100 / (1 + ($3.67 / $2))) = 64.85. Since the RSI is below 70, we conclude that the stock is not overbought.

## Common Errors

Practitioners of technical analysis often make mistakes that can lead to incorrect interpretations and trading decisions. One common error is the failure to consider the context in which a chart pattern or indicator is formed. For instance, a head-and-shoulders pattern may be considered a reversal signal, but its reliability depends on the overall trend and market conditions. Ignoring the broader market context can lead to false signals and incorrect trading decisions. Another mistake is over-reliance on a single indicator or chart pattern, neglecting the importance of confirmation from multiple sources. This can result in false positives or false negatives, leading to incorrect trading decisions. Additionally, practitioners may fall victim to curve-fitting, where an indicator or strategy is optimized to fit historical data but fails to perform in real-time markets. It is essential to test strategies on out-of-sample data to ensure their robustness and avoid over-optimization. Furthermore, technical analysts may fail to account for changes in market conditions, such as shifts in volatility or liquidity, which can affect the performance of indicators and chart patterns. By being aware of these common errors, practitioners can refine their approach to technical analysis and make more informed trading decisions.

# Circular Economy

> The course teaches core principles and mechanisms of the circular economy, assuming some basic knowledge of sustainability concepts.

## Principles

Eliminate waste/pollution, circulate products/materials, regenerate nature (Ellen MacArthur Foundation). R-strategies: refuse, reduce, reuse, repair, refurbish, remanufacture, repurpose, recycle. DESIGN: Design for disassembly, modularity, material selection (cradle-to-cradle). Product-as-a-service models. INDUSTRIAL SYMBIOSIS: Waste from one process = feedstock for another. Eco-industrial parks (Kalundborg, Denmark). BUSINESS MODELS: Sharing economy, leasing, take-back programs, remanufacturing. METRICS: Material circularity indicator, resource productivity. POLICY: Extended producer responsibility (EPR), right to repair, plastic taxes. TECHNOLOGY: Digital product passports, blockchain for traceability, AI waste sorting.

## Foundations

The circular economy is a sustainability-focused economic model that emphasizes the reuse and recycling of resources, minimizing waste and the continuous consumption of new raw materials. **Closed-loop production** refers to the process of designing products and systems where materials are constantly cycled back into production, reducing the need for virgin materials. A **regenerative system** is one that restores and regenerates natural resources, rather than depleting them. **Biodegradable materials** are those that can break down naturally in the environment, reducing pollution and waste. **Technical nutrients** are materials that can be recycled and reused, such as metals and plastics. In contrast, **biological nutrients** are organic materials that can decompose and return to nature, such as food waste and agricultural by-products. The **circular economy framework** is guided by the principles of **design for circularity**, which involves designing products and systems that are restorative and regenerative by design. **Systems thinking** is a critical approach in the circular economy, considering the interconnectedness of economic, social, and environmental systems. **Life cycle assessment** is a method used to evaluate the environmental impacts of products and systems throughout their entire life cycle, from raw material extraction to end-of-life disposal or recycling. Understanding these core definitions and principles is essential for practitioners working in the circular economy.

## Mechanisms

The circular economy operates through several key mechanisms that enable the reduction of waste and the promotion of sustainable consumption and production patterns. Firstly, the design of products and services is crucial, as it determines the potential for recycling, reuse, and biodegradability. Products are designed to be restorative and regenerative by design, aiming to keep resources in use for as long as possible. This design principle is followed by the production phase, where the use of renewable energy and minimal waste generation are prioritized. The next step involves the sharing, leasing, and product-as-a-service models, which allow for the optimization of resource use and the reduction of individual ownership. 
The reuse and recycling mechanisms are also vital, as they enable the recovery of materials and the reduction of waste sent to landfills. This is achieved through closed-loop production systems, where materials are constantly cycled back into production, and through the development of recycling technologies and infrastructure. 
Finally, the circular economy relies on the collaboration and participation of all stakeholders, including businesses, governments, and individuals, to create a culture of sustainability and to drive the transition towards a more circular and regenerative economy. The causal chain is as follows: design influences production, production affects consumption, consumption patterns impact waste generation, and waste management practices determine the potential for recycling and reuse, ultimately influencing the overall sustainability of the economy. This design phase considers the entire lifecycle of a product, from production to end-of-life, and aims to minimize waste and the use of non-renewable resources. The next mechanism is the sharing and collaboration economy, where products are shared among users, reducing the need for individual ownership and the consequent demand for new, resource-intensive products. Another mechanism is the implementation of closed-loop production systems, where materials are constantly cycled back into production, reducing the need for virgin materials and the generation of waste. The recycling and upcycling of materials also play a critical role, as they enable the transformation of waste into new products, reducing the need for primary production and the associated environmental impacts. Finally, the circular economy relies on the development of new business models that prioritize service and functionality over ownership, such as product-as-a-service, enabling companies to design and produce products that are restorative and regenerative by design. These mechanisms interact and reinforce each other, creating a system that conserves resources, reduces waste, and promotes sustainable consumption and production patterns.

## Methods And Frameworks

In the context of a circular economy, several methods and frameworks are employed to design and implement sustainable systems. The Circular Economy Framework, also known as the Ellen MacArthur Foundation Framework, is a widely used approach that involves designing out waste and pollution, keeping products and materials in use, and regenerating natural systems. The Cradle-to-Cradle (C2C) design method is another key framework, which focuses on creating products that are safe, circular, and endlessly cycled. The Material Circularity Indicator (MCI) formula is used to measure the circularity of materials in a product or system, calculated as the ratio of circular material inputs to total material inputs. The Life Cycle Assessment (LCA) method is applied to evaluate the environmental impacts of products and systems throughout their entire life cycle, from raw material extraction to end-of-life. Failure modes of these methods include the inability to account for complex system interactions, lack of data availability, and inconsistent application of circular economy principles. The Strengths-Weaknesses-Opportunities-Threats (SWOT) analysis is often used to identify the feasibility of implementing circular economy strategies, while the Stakeholder Engagement Framework helps to ensure that all relevant stakeholders are involved in the decision-making process. Each of these methods and frameworks has its own strengths and limitations, and the choice of which to use depends on the specific context and goals of the circular economy initiative. The Circular Economy Framework, for instance, emphasizes the reduction of waste and the promotion of recycling and reuse. The 3R (Reduce, Reuse, Recycle) principle is a fundamental approach, applicable when designing production systems to minimize environmental impact. The Cradle-to-Cradle (C2C) design model is used to create products that are restorative and regenerative by design, suitable for applications where material circularity is crucial. The Ellen MacArthur Foundation's Circular Economy Model provides a comprehensive approach to transitioning towards a circular economy, applicable when assessing and transforming entire systems. The Material Circularity Indicator (MCI) formula is utilized to measure the circularity of materials in production systems, useful when evaluating the effectiveness of circular economy strategies. Failure modes of these methods include the inability to account for complex system interactions, lack of stakeholder engagement, and insufficient consideration of social and economic factors. The Linear Economy Assumption is a common failure mode, where circular economy principles are applied to linear systems without adequate adaptation.

## Worked Examples

To illustrate the application of circular economy principles in sustainability, consider the following examples. 
1. Product Design: A company producing 100,000 units of a product made from recyclable materials wants to design for recyclability. By increasing the recyclable content from 50% to 80%, they can reduce waste sent to landfills. Assuming a cost savings of $0.50 per unit for increased recyclability, the total savings would be $30,000 (100,000 units * 0.30 or 30% increase in recyclability * $0.50 per unit savings from recycling, noting 80%-50% = 30% increase).
2. Sharing Economy: A city with 500,000 residents implements a bike-sharing program, reducing the number of privately owned bikes from 200,000 to 150,000. With each bike representing a reduction of 1.2 tons of CO2 emissions per year, the total emissions reduction would be 60,000 tons (50,000 bikes * 1.2 tons per bike).
3. Closed-Loop Production: A manufacturer of 50,000 tons of aluminum products per year switches to a closed-loop system, where 80% of the aluminum is recycled and reused. With the energy required to produce new aluminum being 12 times that of recycling, the energy savings would be equivalent to 384,000 megawatt-hours (50,000 tons * 0.8 * 12 * 0.8, noting the 0.8 represents 80% of aluminum recycled and the second 0.8 represents the fraction of total production that is subject to the 12:1 energy ratio). ## Applications

In the sustainability environment, the circular economy is applied through various practices that aim to reduce waste, promote resource efficiency, and regenerate natural systems. One key application is the sharing economy, where products and services are shared among individuals, reducing the need for new resource extraction and waste generation. For example, car-sharing and bike-sharing programs reduce the number of vehicles on the road, decreasing emissions and promoting sustainable transportation. Another application is product-as-a-service, where companies provide products as a service, rather than selling them outright, encouraging the design of longer-lasting, recyclable products. Additionally, circular business models such as closed-loop production, where materials are continuously cycled back into production, are being adopted by companies to reduce waste and improve resource efficiency. The circular economy is also applied in urban planning, where cities are designed to be more resource-efficient, with green spaces, renewable energy systems, and waste management systems that promote recycling and composting. Furthermore, the circular economy is being applied in the agriculture sector, where regenerative agriculture practices, such as permaculture and agroforestry, aim to promote soil health, biodiversity, and efficient water use, reducing the environmental impacts of farming. These applications demonstrate the potential of the circular economy to transform various sectors and promote sustainable development. For example, companies like H&M and Patagonia have implemented garment collection programs, encouraging customers to return used clothing which is then recycled into new products. Another application is the sharing economy, where products are shared or leased rather than owned, reducing the demand for new resources and waste generation. Car-sharing services like Zipcar and bike-sharing systems like Citi Bike are examples of this approach. Additionally, circular economy principles are applied in urban planning, where cities are designed to be more resource-efficient, with green spaces, renewable energy systems, and waste-to-energy facilities. The city of Copenhagen, for instance, aims to be carbon neutral by 2025, using circular economy strategies such as district heating and biomass energy production. Furthermore, circular economy practices are also applied in agriculture, through regenerative farming methods that promote soil health, biodiversity, and efficient water use, reducing the environmental impact of farming practices.

## Common Errors

In the implementation of circular economy principles, practitioners often make mistakes that undermine the effectiveness of their sustainability efforts. One common error is the focus on recycling as the primary means of achieving circularity, while neglecting the importance of reducing waste generation and promoting the reuse of products. This approach is flawed because recycling, although essential, is often energy-intensive and may not address the root causes of waste production. Another mistake is the failure to consider the entire lifecycle of products, from design to end-of-life, leading to the creation of products that are not designed for recyclability, reuse, or biodegradability. Additionally, some practitioners overlook the social dimension of circular economy, neglecting to involve stakeholders and local communities in the decision-making process, which can lead to inequitable distribution of benefits and unintended consequences. Furthermore, the overemphasis on technological solutions, such as biodegradable materials or advanced recycling technologies, can distract from the need for fundamental changes in consumption patterns and production systems. By recognizing these common errors, practitioners can avoid pitfalls and develop more effective strategies for transitioning to a circular economy.

## Advanced

The circular economy concept is evolving to address complex sustainability challenges. At the graduate level, students delve into advanced topics such as circular business model innovation, product design for recyclability and reuse, and the integration of circular economy principles with other sustainability frameworks. Open questions in the field include the development of standardized metrics for measuring circularity, the role of digital technologies in enabling circular economy transitions, and the potential for circular economy approaches to address social justice and equity concerns. Emerging areas of research include the application of circular economy principles to emerging technologies like biotechnology and nanotechnology, and the exploration of circular economy solutions for complex systems like cities and food systems. Additionally, there is a growing interest in the intersection of circular economy with other sustainability concepts, such as the doughnut economics and regenerative development, which aim to reconcile human well-being with environmental limits. As the field continues to evolve, it is likely that new approaches and tools will be developed to support the transition to a more circular and regenerative economy.

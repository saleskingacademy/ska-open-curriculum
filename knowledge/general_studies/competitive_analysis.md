# Competitive Analysis

> The course teaches core concepts and principles of competitive analysis, assuming no prior study but requiring understanding of business terminology.

## Foundations

Competitive analysis is a systematic process used in business to evaluate and understand the competitive landscape of an industry or market. It involves analyzing the strengths, weaknesses, opportunities, and threats (SWOT analysis) of a company and its competitors. A competitor is a firm that offers similar products or services to the same target market. The target market refers to the specific group of consumers that a company aims to serve with its products or services. Market share is the proportion of total sales or revenue in a market that a company captures. A market leader is a company that has the largest market share in a particular industry or market. Porter's Five Forces is a framework used to analyze the competitive structure of an industry, consisting of the threat of new entrants, bargaining power of suppliers, bargaining power of buyers, threat of substitute products, and rivalry among existing competitors. A practitioner of competitive analysis must understand these core concepts to effectively analyze and interpret the competitive landscape.

A **competitive landscape** is the overall environment in which a business operates, including the number and strength of competitors, the availability of resources, and the level of market demand. **Competitive advantage** refers to the unique benefits or characteristics that a business has over its competitors, such as lower costs, innovative products, or strong brand recognition.

## Mechanisms

Competitive analysis in business involves a systematic process to gather, analyze, and interpret data about competitors. The step-by-step mechanism starts with identifying key competitors through market research and industry reports. Next, data collection occurs through various means such as public records, social media, customer reviews, and competitor websites. This data is then analyzed to understand competitors' strengths, weaknesses, strategies, and market positions. The analysis involves assessing competitors' product offerings, pricing strategies, marketing tactics, and operational efficiencies. By comparing this information with the company's own data, a causal chain of implications for business strategy emerges. This chain includes identifying market gaps, assessing competitive threats and opportunities, and informing strategic decisions on product development, pricing, marketing, and investment. The outcome of this mechanism is a competitive intelligence report that guides business strategy formulation and continuous monitoring of the competitive landscape to maintain a competitive edge.

Competitive analysis in business involves a systematic process to gather, analyze, and interpret data about competitors. The step-by-step mechanism works as follows: 
1. **Identification of Competitors**: Identify direct and indirect competitors in the market, considering factors such as market share, product offerings, and target audience.
2. **Data Collection**: Gather relevant data about competitors through secondary research (publicly available information) and primary research (surveys, interviews, etc.).
3. **Analysis of Competitor Strategies**: Analyze competitors' business strategies, including their mission, vision, strengths, weaknesses, opportunities, and threats (SWOT analysis).
4. **Assessment of Competitor Capabilities**: Evaluate competitors' capabilities, such as financial resources, human resources, technology, and operational efficiency.
5. **Market Positioning Analysis**: Analyze how competitors position themselves in the market, including their branding, pricing, and distribution strategies.
6. **Comparison and Contrast**: Compare and contrast the competitor's strategies, capabilities, and market positioning with those of the company conducting the analysis.
7. **Identification of Gaps and Opportunities**: Identify gaps in the market and opportunities to differentiate the company from its competitors.
8. **Development of Counter-Strategies**: Develop counter-strategies to address competitors' strengths and weaknesses, and to capitalize on identified opportunities.
The causal chain is explicit: understanding competitors' strategies and capabilities informs the development of effective counter-strategies, which in turn drive business decisions to gain a competitive advantage.

## Methods And Frameworks

Competitive analysis in business utilizes various methods and frameworks to assess market competition. The Porter's Five Forces model is used to analyze industry competition, identifying bargaining power of suppliers and buyers, threat of new entrants and substitutes, and rivalry among existing competitors. The SWOT analysis framework is applied to evaluate a company's strengths, weaknesses, opportunities, and threats, providing a comprehensive view of its competitive position. The Boston Consulting Group (BCG) growth-share matrix is used to assess product portfolios, categorizing them into stars, cash cows, question marks, and dogs based on market growth and relative market share. The McKinsey 7S framework is employed to examine a company's strategy, structure, systems, skills, style, staff, and shared values, ensuring alignment and effective competitive strategy implementation. Each method has its failure mode, such as overreliance on historical data in Porter's Five Forces or neglecting external factors in SWOT analysis. Understanding the appropriate application and limitations of these methods is crucial for effective competitive analysis.

## Worked Examples

To illustrate the application of competitive analysis in business, consider the following examples.

1. **Market Share Analysis**: Suppose a company, XYZ Inc., operates in the smartphone market. The total market size is 10 million units, with XYZ Inc. holding 20% market share, selling 2 million units at $500 each. Competitor ABC Inc. holds 30% market share, selling 3 million units at $450 each. To gain a competitive edge, XYZ Inc. must analyze ABC Inc.'s pricing strategy and consider reducing its price or improving product features to increase market share.

2. **Cost Structure Comparison**: A retail company, DEF Retail, has a cost of goods sold (COGS) of 60% and operating expenses of 20% of revenue. Its main competitor, GHI Retail, has a COGS of 55% and operating expenses of 15% of revenue. By analyzing the cost structure, DEF Retail can identify areas to improve efficiency and reduce costs to remain competitive.

3. **Competitor Profiling**: Consider a company, JKL Ltd., that offers cloud storage services. Its main competitor, MNO Corp., has a similar service offering but with additional features such as enhanced security and customer support. JKL Ltd. can create a competitor profile to analyze MNO Corp.'s strengths, weaknesses, and strategies, and then develop a plan to enhance its own service features and improve customer satisfaction to gain a competitive advantage.

1. **Market Share Analysis**: Suppose Company A and Company B are competitors in the smartphone market. Company A has a market share of 30% and Company B has a market share of 25%. If the total market size is $100 million, what is the revenue of each company? 
Company A's revenue = 30% of $100 million = $30 million. 
Company B's revenue = 25% of $100 million = $25 million.

2. **Cost Comparison**: A company is considering two suppliers, Supplier X and Supplier Y, for a critical component. Supplier X quotes a price of $10 per unit with a lead time of 4 weeks, while Supplier Y quotes a price of $9.50 per unit with a lead time of 6 weeks. If the company needs 10,000 units per month, which supplier is more competitive? 
The total cost for Supplier X = $10 * 10,000 = $100,000 per month. 
The total cost for Supplier Y = $9.50 * 10,000 = $95,000 per month. 
However, the longer lead time of Supplier Y may result in additional inventory holding costs, which could offset the cost savings.

3. **SWOT Analysis**: A company is analyzing its competitive position using SWOT analysis. It identifies the following factors: 
Strengths: brand reputation, wide distribution network 
Weaknesses: high production costs, limited product range 
Opportunities: growing demand for eco-friendly products, expanding into new markets 
Threats: increasing competition from low-cost producers, potential regulatory changes 
By analyzing these factors, the company can develop strategies to leverage its strengths and opportunities, while addressing its weaknesses and mitigating the threats.

The total market size is 10 million units, with XYZ Inc. selling 1.5 million units, Apple selling 3 million units, and Samsung selling 2.5 million units. To calculate the market share, divide the number of units sold by each company by the total market size. XYZ Inc.'s market share is 1.5/10 = 15%, Apple's is 3/10 = 30%, and Samsung's is 2.5/10 = 25%. This analysis helps XYZ Inc. understand its competitive position and identify areas for improvement.

2. **Competitor Profiling**: A company, ABC Ltd., wants to analyze its competitor, DEF Corp. DEF Corp.'s revenue is $100 million, with a net profit margin of 15%. ABC Ltd.'s revenue is $80 million, with a net profit margin of 10%. By comparing these metrics, ABC Ltd. can identify DEF Corp.'s strengths, such as higher revenue and profitability, and develop strategies to bridge the gap.

3. **SWOT Analysis**: Consider a company, GHI Enterprises, operating in the coffee shop market. Its strengths include a strong brand reputation and high-quality products. Weaknesses include high operating costs and limited geographical presence. Opportunities arise from increasing demand for specialty coffee and potential partnerships with food delivery services. Threats include intense competition from established chains and changing consumer preferences. By conducting a SWOT analysis, GHI Enterprises can develop a competitive strategy that leverages its strengths, addresses its weaknesses, capitalizes on opportunities, and mitigates threats.

## Common Errors

In competitive analysis, practitioners often make mistakes that can lead to flawed conclusions and ineffective strategies. One common error is the failure to define the relevant market scope, resulting in an analysis that is too narrow or too broad. This can lead to a misidentification of key competitors and a lack of understanding of the competitive landscape. Another error is the reliance on secondary research alone, without primary research to validate findings. This can result in outdated or inaccurate information, leading to poor decision-making. Additionally, some practitioners focus too much on competitors' strengths, rather than their weaknesses and vulnerabilities, which can provide opportunities for differentiation and competitive advantage. Furthermore, the failure to consider non-traditional competitors, such as new entrants or substitutes, can lead to a lack of preparedness for disruptive changes in the market. These errors can be avoided by taking a rigorous and comprehensive approach to competitive analysis, incorporating multiple research methods and considering a wide range of factors that can impact the competitive environment.

## Advanced

At the graduate level, competitive analysis extends beyond foundational frameworks to incorporate advanced concepts and methodologies. One key extension is the integration of game theory, which enables the analysis of competitive interactions as strategic games. This involves applying principles such as Nash equilibrium to predict competitor behavior and optimize strategic decisions. Another area of advancement is the use of machine learning and artificial intelligence to analyze large datasets and identify patterns in competitor behavior, allowing for more accurate predictions and informed decision-making. Open questions in the field include the development of more nuanced and dynamic models of competitor behavior, as well as the incorporation of non-market factors, such as regulatory and societal trends, into competitive analysis. The field is also moving towards a more holistic understanding of competition, recognizing that firms compete not only in product markets but also in factor markets, such as talent and capital. Furthermore, the increasing importance of digital platforms and ecosystems is creating new opportunities for competitive analysis, as firms must navigate complex networks of partners, suppliers, and competitors to achieve strategic advantage. Ultimately, the future of competitive analysis will require the integration of advanced analytical techniques, a deep understanding of strategic interactions, and a nuanced appreciation of the complex and dynamic nature of competition in modern business environments.

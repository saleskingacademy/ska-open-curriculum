# Mechanistic Interpretability

> The course teaches core concepts and principles of mechanistic interpretability, assuming some basic knowledge of the field.

## Foundations

Mechanistic interpretability refers to the process of understanding the internal workings of a complex system, such as a machine learning model, by analyzing its constituent components and their interactions. A **system** is a set of interconnected elements that work together to achieve a common goal. **Interpretability** is the degree to which a system's behavior can be understood by humans. **Mechanistic** refers to the focus on the underlying mechanisms and processes that drive the system's behavior. A **model** is a mathematical representation of a system, used to make predictions or decisions. **Complexity** refers to the number of components, interactions, and nonlinear relationships within a system. **Transparency** is the property of a system being open to inspection and understanding. **Explainability** is the ability to provide clear and concise explanations of a system's behavior. Understanding these core concepts is essential for practitioners to develop effective mechanistic interpretability methods, which involve **decomposition** (breaking down a system into its components), **analysis** (examining the behavior of each component), and **recomposition** (reintegrating the components to understand the system's overall behavior).

In the context of computer science, Mechanistic Interpretability refers to the practice of understanding and explaining the internal mechanisms and decision-making processes of complex systems, particularly artificial neural networks (ANNs) and other machine learning (ML) models. A **model** is a mathematical representation of a system, learned from data, that predicts outputs for given inputs. **Interpretability** is the degree to which a model's predictions can be understood and explained by its internal workings. **Mechanistic** refers to the focus on the underlying mechanisms, or the step-by-step processes, by which the model generates its predictions. Key concepts include **transparency**, which refers to the model's ability to provide clear and understandable explanations, and **explainability**, which refers to the ability to provide insights into the model's decision-making process. A **practitioner** must understand these concepts, as well as **feature attribution**, which is the process of assigning importance to specific input features, and **saliency maps**, which are visual representations of the most relevant input features contributing to a model's prediction. **Model interpretability techniques** include methods such as layer-wise relevance propagation, deepLIFT, and SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations), which help to assign contributions to input features. Understanding these **first principles** and **core definitions** is essential for a practitioner to effectively apply mechanistic interpretability to complex systems.

## Mechanisms

In the context of mechanistic interpretability, a mechanism refers to the underlying process by which a complex system, such as a neural network, generates its outputs. To understand how it actually works, we can break down the mechanism into a step-by-step causal chain. This chain typically begins with the input data, which is then processed through a series of transformations, including feature extraction, representation learning, and decision-making. Each transformation step is mediated by specific components or modules within the system, such as convolutional layers, recurrent layers, or attention mechanisms. The causal chain is made explicit by identifying the key factors that influence the system's behavior, including model architecture, hyperparameters, and training data. By analyzing the mechanism, researchers can identify potential sources of bias, error, or unexpected behavior, and develop targeted interventions to improve the system's performance, fairness, or transparency. For example, in a neural network, the mechanism might involve the following steps: (1) input data is fed into the network, (2) the data is processed through convolutional layers to extract features, (3) the features are passed through fully connected layers to generate a representation, and (4) the representation is used to make a prediction or classification. By understanding the mechanism, researchers can modify the architecture, hyperparameters, or training data to improve the network's performance or interpretability. To understand how it actually works, we need to dissect the system into its constituent components and analyze the causal chain of events that leads to the final output. This involves identifying the key variables, their interactions, and the flow of information between them.

The process can be broken down into several steps: 
1. **Input Processing**: The system receives input data, which is then processed by the initial layers of the network. 
2. **Feature Extraction**: The processed input data is used to extract relevant features, which are then passed on to subsequent layers for further processing. 
3. **Pattern Recognition**: The extracted features are used to recognize patterns in the data, which are then used to make predictions or classify the input. 
4. **Output Generation**: The recognized patterns are used to generate the final output, which can be a prediction, classification, or some other form of output.

By analyzing the causal chain of events, we can identify the key factors that contribute to the system's behavior and understand how the different components interact to produce the final output. This can involve techniques such as saliency maps, feature importance, and layer-wise relevance propagation, which can help to highlight the most important variables and their interactions.

Ultimately, the goal of mechanistic interpretability is to provide a detailed understanding of the underlying mechanisms that drive the system's behavior, allowing us to build more transparent, explainable, and trustworthy models.

## Worked Examples

To illustrate the concept of mechanistic interpretability in computer science, consider the following examples. 
1. Analyzing a neural network's decision-making process: Suppose we have a neural network trained to classify images of dogs and cats, with an accuracy of 90%. To understand how the model makes predictions, we can use techniques like saliency maps or feature importance to identify the input features that contribute most to the predictions. For instance, if the model predicts an image is a dog with 80% confidence, the saliency map may show that the model is focusing on the shape of the ears and the texture of the fur, with the ears contributing 40% and the fur contributing 30% to the prediction.
2. Interpreting a decision tree's rules: Consider a decision tree trained to predict whether a customer will buy a product based on their age, income, and location. The tree may have a rule like "if age > 30 and income > 50000, then predict buy". To understand this rule, we can analyze the distribution of ages and incomes in the training data and see how the tree is using these features to make predictions. For example, if 60% of customers who bought the product were between 30 and 50 years old, and had an income above 50000, the tree is likely using this demographic information to inform its predictions.
3. Understanding a recommender system's suggestions: Suppose we have a recommender system that suggests products to users based on their past purchases and ratings. To understand why the system is suggesting a particular product, we can analyze the user's past behavior and the product's features. For instance, if the system suggests a user buy a book by an author they have never read before, but the book is in the same genre as books they have liked in the past, the system is likely using a combination of content-based filtering and collaborative filtering to make the suggestion, with the genre contributing 50% and the user's past ratings contributing 30% to the suggestion. By applying a saliency map technique, we can assign a relevance score to each input pixel. For instance, if the model predicts the digit "7" with a confidence of 0.8, the saliency map may reveal that pixels in the top-left and bottom-right corners of the image have the highest relevance scores (0.6 and 0.4, respectively), indicating that these regions are most influential in the model's decision-making process.
2. **Feature Importance**: Suppose we have a random forest model trained on a dataset of user interactions with a website, with the goal of predicting user engagement (e.g., time spent on the site). By analyzing the feature importance scores, we find that the "average time spent on the homepage" feature has an importance score of 0.3, while the "number of pages visited" feature has an importance score of 0.2. This suggests that the model relies more heavily on the average time spent on the homepage when making predictions.
3. **Layer-wise Relevance Propagation**: Consider a convolutional neural network (CNN) trained on the CIFAR-10 dataset, achieving an accuracy of 90%. To understand how the model's convolutional layers contribute to its predictions, we apply layer-wise relevance propagation (LRP). By analyzing the LRP scores, we observe that the first convolutional layer has a high relevance score (0.7) for the "car" class, indicating that this layer is crucial in detecting car-like features in the input images. In contrast, the second convolutional layer has a lower relevance score (0.1) for the "bird" class, suggesting that this layer is less important for bird-like feature detection.

## Applications

Mechanistic interpretability has numerous applications in computer science, particularly in the development of transparent and explainable artificial intelligence (AI) and machine learning (ML) systems. In practice, mechanistic interpretability is used to analyze and understand the decision-making processes of complex models, such as neural networks and deep learning algorithms. For instance, in computer vision, mechanistic interpretability can be applied to identify the specific features and patterns that a model uses to classify images. This can help developers to identify biases in the model and improve its performance. In natural language processing (NLP), mechanistic interpretability can be used to analyze the attention mechanisms of a model, providing insights into how the model weighs different input elements when generating text or making predictions. Additionally, mechanistic interpretability is used in model-based reinforcement learning to understand how an agent makes decisions and navigates complex environments. By providing a detailed understanding of the underlying mechanisms of a model, mechanistic interpretability enables developers to refine and improve their systems, leading to more accurate, reliable, and trustworthy AI and ML applications. ## Common Errors

In mechanistic interpretability, practitioners often make mistakes that can lead to misleading or incomplete explanations of complex systems. One common error is over-reliance on feature importance methods, such as SHAP or LIME, without considering the underlying mechanistic assumptions. These methods can provide insights into which input features are driving model predictions, but they do not necessarily reveal the underlying causal relationships or system dynamics. Another mistake is conflating interpretability with transparency, where practitioners focus on providing detailed descriptions of model architecture or training data without actually explaining how the model works. Additionally, some practitioners may oversimplify complex systems by focusing on a single component or mechanism, rather than considering the interactions and interdependencies between components. This can lead to incomplete or inaccurate explanations of system behavior. Furthermore, failing to consider the context and scope of the system being interpreted can also lead to errors, as mechanistic interpretability requires a deep understanding of the system's boundaries, constraints, and operating conditions. By recognizing these common errors, practitioners can develop more effective and accurate approaches to mechanistic interpretability.

In mechanistic interpretability, a key aspect of explainable AI, practitioners often make mistakes that can lead to misleading or incomplete explanations of complex systems. One common error is conflating model interpretability with model simplicity, assuming that simpler models are inherently more interpretable. However, simplicity does not guarantee interpretability, as even simple models can have complex, nonlinear interactions that are difficult to understand. Another mistake is relying solely on feature importance scores, such as SHAP or LIME values, without considering the context and relationships between features. This can lead to oversimplification of the model's decision-making process and neglect of important interactions. Furthermore, practitioners may incorrectly assume that interpretability methods are model-agnostic, applying techniques designed for linear models to complex neural networks without proper adaptation. This can result in explanations that do not accurately reflect the model's behavior. Additionally, the use of proxy metrics, such as accuracy or F1 score, as a substitute for true interpretability can be misleading, as a model can be highly accurate yet still lack transparency into its decision-making process. These errors highlight the need for a nuanced understanding of mechanistic interpretability and careful consideration of the strengths and limitations of various explanation methods.

# Product Roadmapping

> The course teaches applied practice of product roadmapping, assuming foundational knowledge of the field.

## Overview

Professional discipline for the forge specialist: product roadmapping. Applied practice used to generate revenue.

## Foundations

Product roadmapping is a strategic planning process that outlines the vision, goals, and initiatives for a product or product line over a specific period. A **product roadmap** is a high-level, visual representation of a product's evolution, describing how it will achieve its vision and meet customer needs. **Vision** refers to a concise statement that defines the desired future state of a product, while **goals** are specific, measurable objectives that a product must achieve to realize its vision. **Initiatives** are discrete projects or activities designed to achieve these goals. 
A **product** is an offering that meets a specific customer need or want, and can be a physical good, service, or digital experience. **Customer needs** are the problems, desires, or requirements that a product aims to satisfy. **Stakeholders** are individuals or groups with an interest in the product's success, including customers, users, business leaders, and team members. 
Effective product roadmapping relies on a deep understanding of the **market**, which encompasses the external environment, including competitors, trends, and regulatory factors that impact the product's success. **Competitors** are other products or services that satisfy similar customer needs, while **trends** refer to patterns or directions of change in the market. 
A **product lifecycle** describes the stages a product goes through, from introduction to maturity and eventual decline. Understanding the product lifecycle is crucial for developing a roadmap that addresses the product's unique challenges and opportunities at each stage. 
By grasping these core definitions and principles, practitioners can develop a robust product roadmap that guides the development of successful products. A **product roadmap** is a visual representation of a product's evolution, detailing the key milestones, deliverables, and timelines. It serves as a guiding document for stakeholders, including product managers, engineers, and executives. The **product vision** is the overarching goal that the roadmap aims to achieve, defining the product's purpose, target audience, and desired outcomes. **First principles** in product roadmapping involve understanding the customer's needs, market trends, and technological advancements. A **product strategy** is the approach taken to achieve the product vision, encompassing decisions on features, resources, and priorities. Key vocabulary includes **epics**, which are large bodies of work that span multiple iterations or releases, and **user stories**, which are specific, actionable descriptions of the desired functionality from the user's perspective. **Initiatives** are high-level efforts to achieve specific business objectives, often comprising multiple epics and user stories. **Milestones** mark significant events or accomplishments in the product's development, such as major releases or feature completions.

## Mechanisms

Product roadmapping operates through a series of interconnected steps that translate business strategy into actionable product plans. It begins with the definition of the company's overall vision and mission, which informs the product vision. This product vision is then decomposed into specific, measurable, achievable, relevant, and time-bound (SMART) objectives. The next step involves identifying the key product features and initiatives required to achieve these objectives, taking into account customer needs, market trends, and technological advancements. These features and initiatives are prioritized based on their potential impact, feasibility, and alignment with the product vision. The prioritized list is then used to create a roadmap, which is a visual representation of the product's evolution over time, including milestones, timelines, and resource allocations. The roadmap serves as a guide for the product development process, ensuring that all stakeholders are aligned and working towards common goals. Regular review and updating of the roadmap allow for adaptations to changing market conditions, customer feedback, and new technological opportunities, maintaining the product's relevance and competitiveness. This iterative process ensures that the product roadmap remains a dynamic and effective tool for driving product strategy and development.

## Methods And Frameworks

Product roadmapping employs various methods, models, and formulas to guide the development process. The Kano Model is used to categorize features into basic, performance, and excitement attributes, helping prioritize development based on customer satisfaction. Use the Kano Model when understanding customer needs and preferences is crucial. Its failure mode occurs when it oversimplifies complex customer needs. 
The MoSCoW Method prioritizes features into must-haves, should-haves, could-haves, and won't-haves, facilitating effective resource allocation. Apply the MoSCoW Method when prioritizing features with limited resources. It fails when priorities are not regularly reassessed. 
The RICE scoring model (Reach, Impact, Confidence, Effort) evaluates feature viability by calculating a score based on these factors. Use RICE when evaluating multiple features with varying resource requirements. Its failure mode arises when the scoring system is not tailored to the specific product or market. 
The PRD (Product Requirements Document) framework outlines product vision, goals, and requirements, ensuring stakeholders are aligned. Utilize PRD when developing complex products with multiple stakeholders. It fails when not regularly updated or when it becomes overly detailed, causing analysis paralysis. 
The Agile methodology, including Scrum and Kanban, emphasizes iterative development, flexibility, and continuous improvement. Apply Agile when developing products with rapidly changing requirements or high uncertainty. Its failure mode occurs when teams do not adhere to Agile principles or when the process becomes too rigid. 
Understanding these methods, models, and formulas enables product managers to select the most appropriate approach for their specific product roadmapping needs, avoiding common pitfalls and ensuring effective product development. The Goal-Question-Metric (GQM) framework is used to define and measure product objectives, where goals are set, questions are formulated to achieve those goals, and metrics are established to answer those questions. The RICE formula (Reach, Impact, Confidence, Effort) is used to evaluate and prioritize features based on their potential impact and resource requirements. Failure modes for these methods include overemphasis on metrics, neglecting customer feedback, and inadequate prioritization, leading to misaligned product development. Understanding the context and limitations of each method is crucial to avoid these pitfalls and ensure effective product roadmapping.

## Worked Examples

To illustrate the application of product roadmapping, consider the following examples.

1. **New Feature Development**: A company plans to develop a new payment gateway for its e-commerce platform. The estimated development time is 12 weeks, with 4 weeks for research, 4 weeks for development, and 4 weeks for testing. The development team consists of 2 full-stack developers, 1 QA engineer, and 1 product manager, with a total weekly cost of $15,000. The expected revenue increase from the new feature is $200,000 per month.

2. **Platform Migration**: A software company needs to migrate its application from an outdated platform to a newer one. The estimated migration time is 20 weeks, with 8 weeks for planning, 8 weeks for execution, and 4 weeks for testing. The migration team consists of 3 developers, 1 architect, and 1 project manager, with a total weekly cost of $20,000. The expected cost savings from the migration is $150,000 per year.

3. **Product Enhancement**: A company plans to enhance its existing customer relationship management (CRM) system by adding new features. The estimated development time is 16 weeks, with 6 weeks for requirements gathering, 6 weeks for development, and 4 weeks for testing. The development team consists of 2 developers, 1 QA engineer, and 1 product owner, with a total weekly cost of $12,000. The expected revenue increase from the enhancement is $100,000 per quarter.

In each example, the product roadmap would outline the key milestones, timelines, and resource allocation, ensuring that the development team is aligned with the business objectives and that the project is delivered on time and within budget.

## Applications

Product roadmapping is utilized in various industries, including software development, technology, and manufacturing. In software development, product roadmaps are used to outline the development and release of new features, updates, and versions of a product. For instance, a roadmap for a mobile app might include milestones such as the release of a new user interface, integration with a third-party service, or enhancement of existing features. In the technology sector, companies like Amazon and Google use product roadmaps to plan and prioritize the development of new products and services, such as artificial intelligence, cloud computing, and internet of things (IoT) devices. In manufacturing, product roadmaps are used to plan the development and release of new products, including design, prototyping, testing, and production. A key principle in product roadmapping is to ensure alignment with business objectives and customer needs, which involves continuous feedback and iteration to refine the roadmap and adapt to changing market conditions and customer requirements.

The key principle behind these applications is to create a shared understanding among stakeholders of the product's vision, goals, and timelines, facilitating effective communication and collaboration across functions and teams. By outlining key milestones, deliverables, and resource allocations, product roadmaps enable organizations to make informed decisions, manage complexity, and drive innovation in their respective domains.

## Common Errors

A common error is failing to align the product roadmap with business objectives, resulting in a roadmap that prioritizes features over customer and market needs. Another mistake is not regularly reviewing and updating the roadmap, leading to a disconnect between the product's evolution and changing market conditions. Practitioners also often fall into the trap of creating a roadmap that is overly focused on technology, rather than customer problems and needs. Additionally, not involving key stakeholders, such as sales, marketing, and customer support, in the roadmapping process can lead to a lack of buy-in and understanding of the product's vision and direction. Furthermore, prioritizing features based on intuition rather than data-driven decision making can result in a roadmap that does not accurately reflect customer needs or market trends. Lastly, not considering the dependencies and trade-offs between different product features and initiatives can lead to unrealistic timelines and resource allocation, ultimately causing roadmapping efforts to fail.

## Advanced

Product roadmapping is evolving to incorporate emerging trends and technologies, such as artificial intelligence (AI), Internet of Things (IoT), and agile methodologies. One key extension is the integration of data-driven approaches, using techniques like data analytics and machine learning to inform roadmapping decisions. This involves analyzing customer feedback, market trends, and product performance data to identify patterns and predict future outcomes. Another area of advancement is the adoption of lean and agile principles, which emphasize flexibility, iteration, and continuous learning. This requires roadmappers to be adaptable and responsive to changing market conditions and customer needs. Open questions in the field include how to effectively balance long-term vision with short-term agility, and how to measure the effectiveness of product roadmaps in driving business outcomes. Additionally, there is a growing recognition of the need for more inclusive and diverse roadmapping processes, which take into account the perspectives and needs of diverse stakeholders, including customers, employees, and societal groups. As the field continues to evolve, it is likely that product roadmapping will become increasingly integrated with other business functions, such as strategy, innovation, and operations, to drive more holistic and sustainable business outcomes. This approach enables product managers to prioritize features and allocate resources more effectively. Another area of development is the application of roadmapping to non-traditional domains, such as services, platforms, and ecosystems. Researchers are also exploring the role of roadmapping in driving innovation, particularly in the context of digital transformation and disruption. Furthermore, the field is moving towards more dynamic and adaptive roadmapping approaches, which can respond to changing market conditions and customer needs. This requires product managers to be more agile, flexible, and responsive, and to prioritize continuous learning and iteration. Ultimately, the future of product roadmapping will depend on its ability to adapt to emerging trends and technologies, while remaining focused on delivering value to customers and stakeholders.

# Fundamental Analysis

> The course teaches core concepts and principles of fundamental analysis, assuming some basic knowledge of economics and finance.

## Foundations

In the context of economics finance, Fundamental Analysis refers to a method of evaluating a company's stock price by analyzing its underlying financial and operational performance. A practitioner of Fundamental Analysis, known as a Fundamental Analyst, examines a company's financial statements, management team, industry trends, and competitive position to estimate its intrinsic value. Intrinsic value is the true value of a company, as opposed to its market price, which is the current price at which the stock is trading. The goal of Fundamental Analysis is to identify mispricings in the market, where the market price deviates from the intrinsic value, and to make investment decisions based on this analysis. Key concepts in Fundamental Analysis include Financial Statements, such as the Balance Sheet, Income Statement, and Cash Flow Statement, which provide a snapshot of a company's financial health. Ratio Analysis, which involves calculating metrics such as Price-to-Earnings (P/E) ratio, Debt-to-Equity ratio, and Return on Equity (ROE), helps to assess a company's profitability, leverage, and efficiency. Industry Analysis involves examining the company's position within its industry, including its market share, competition, and growth prospects. A Fundamental Analyst must also consider Qualitative Factors, such as a company's management team, corporate governance, and industry trends, to form a comprehensive view of the company's prospects. By combining these quantitative and qualitative factors, a Fundamental Analyst can estimate a company's intrinsic value and make informed investment decisions. A practitioner of Fundamental Analysis is concerned with estimating a company's intrinsic value, defined as the true value of the company based on its financial statements, management, industry, and economic trends. The goal of Fundamental Analysis is to identify mispricings in the market, where the market value diverges from the intrinsic value, and to make investment decisions based on this analysis. Other essential concepts include Revenue, defined as the income generated from a company's core business operations, and Earnings, which represent the company's net profit. Additionally, Fundamental Analysis involves assessing a company's management quality, industry trends, and competitive position to estimate its future growth prospects and profitability.

Key terms include: Financial Statements, which comprise the Balance Sheet, Income Statement, and Cash Flow Statement, providing a snapshot of a company's financial health. The Balance Sheet lists a company's assets, liabilities, and equity, while the Income Statement details its revenues and expenses over a specific period. The Cash Flow Statement shows the inflows and outflows of cash and cash equivalents.

Other essential concepts are: Revenue, the income generated from a company's core business operations; Net Income, the profit remaining after accounting for all expenses and taxes; and Earnings Per Share (EPS), the net income attributable to each outstanding share of common stock. A practitioner must also understand financial ratios, such as the Price-to-Earnings (P/E) ratio, which compares a stock's market price to its EPS, and the Debt-to-Equity ratio, which assesses a company's leverage.

Understanding these core definitions and principles is crucial for a Fundamental Analysis practitioner to accurately estimate a company's intrinsic value and make informed investment decisions.

## Mechanisms

Fundamental analysis operates through a step-by-step process that evaluates a company's intrinsic value by examining its financial statements, management team, industry trends, and competitive position. The causal chain begins with the collection of financial data, including income statements, balance sheets, and cash flow statements. Analysts then use this data to calculate key ratios and metrics, such as price-to-earnings (P/E) ratio, return on equity (ROE), and debt-to-equity ratio. These metrics provide insights into the company's profitability, efficiency, and solvency. Next, analysts assess the company's qualitative factors, including its management team, corporate governance, and industry position. They also consider external factors, such as macroeconomic trends, industry outlook, and competitive landscape. By analyzing these factors, analysts estimate the company's future financial performance and determine its intrinsic value. This intrinsic value is then compared to the company's current market price to determine if the stock is undervalued or overvalued, thus informing investment decisions. The fundamental analysis process relies on the principle that the market price of a security will eventually converge to its intrinsic value, allowing investors to capitalize on mispricings.

## Methods And Frameworks

Fundamental analysis in economics finance employs various methods and frameworks to evaluate a company's intrinsic value. The Discounted Cash Flow (DCF) model is a widely used method, which estimates a company's present value by discounting its future cash flows using the weighted average cost of capital (WAC). The DCF model is suitable for companies with stable cash flows, but its failure mode lies in the difficulty of accurately forecasting future cash flows. The Gordon Growth Model is a variant of the DCF model, used for companies with consistent growth rates, and is calculated using the formula: Intrinsic Value = (Dividend per share * (1 + growth rate)) / (discount rate - growth rate). The Relative Valuation method, which includes Price-to-Earnings (P/E) and Price-to-Book (P/B) ratios, is used to compare a company's valuation multiples with those of its peers. However, its failure mode lies in the assumption that the comparison companies are similar in terms of growth prospects and risk profiles. The Enterprise Value-to-EBITDA (EV/EBITDA) ratio is another relative valuation method, which is useful for comparing companies with different capital structures. The failure mode of this method lies in the potential for manipulation of EBITDA figures. The Benjamin Graham formula, also known as the intrinsic value formula, estimates a company's intrinsic value using the formula: Intrinsic Value = (Earnings per share * (8.5 + 2 * growth rate)) * 4.4 / (bond yield + growth rate), and is suitable for companies with stable earnings and growth prospects. However, its failure mode lies in the oversimplification of the valuation process.

## Worked Examples

To illustrate the application of fundamental analysis in economics finance, consider the following examples.

1. **Earnings Per Share (EPS) Calculation**: Suppose we are analyzing the stock of XYZ Inc., which reported a net income of $1,000,000 and has 500,000 outstanding shares. To calculate the EPS, we divide the net income by the number of outstanding shares: $1,000,000 ÷ 500,000 = $2.00 EPS. This indicates that for every share owned, the company earned $2.00 in profit.

2. **Price-to-Earnings (P/E) Ratio Analysis**: Let's say XYZ Inc.'s current stock price is $40. Using the EPS calculated earlier ($2), we can find the P/E ratio: $40 ÷ $2 = 20. This indicates that investors are willing to pay $20 for every dollar of earnings. Comparing this to the industry average P/E ratio can help determine if the stock is overvalued or undervalued.

3. **Dividend Yield Calculation**: Assume XYZ Inc. pays an annual dividend of $1 per share, and its current stock price is $40. The dividend yield is calculated as: ($1 ÷ $40) * 100 = 2.5%. This means that for every dollar invested in the stock, the investor can expect a 2.5% return in the form of dividends. Comparing this yield to other investment opportunities can help investors make informed decisions.

## Common Errors

In fundamental analysis, practitioners often make mistakes that can lead to incorrect valuations and investment decisions. One common error is relying too heavily on historical data, failing to account for changes in market conditions, industry trends, and company-specific factors. Another mistake is not considering the limitations of financial ratios, such as price-to-earnings (P/E) or debt-to-equity, which can be influenced by various factors like accounting practices, industry norms, and market sentiment. Additionally, analysts may overlook the importance of qualitative factors, such as management quality, corporate governance, and competitive advantage, which can significantly impact a company's long-term performance. Furthermore, failing to update assumptions and models in response to new information can lead to outdated and inaccurate valuations. It is also essential to avoid anchoring bias, where analysts rely too heavily on initial estimates or targets, rather than re-evaluating them based on new data. By being aware of these common errors, practitioners can refine their analytical approach and produce more accurate and reliable valuations.

Practitioners of fundamental analysis often make mistakes that can lead to incorrect investment decisions. One common error is failing to consider the overall market and economic conditions when analyzing a company's financials. This can lead to overemphasis on a company's individual performance without considering how broader market trends may impact its stock price. Another mistake is relying too heavily on historical data, assuming that past performance is indicative of future results, without adequately accounting for changes in the company's operations, industry, or competitive landscape. Additionally, some analysts may incorrectly apply valuation models, such as the discounted cash flow (DCF) model, by using unrealistic growth rates or discount rates, which can result in inaccurate estimates of a company's intrinsic value. Furthermore, neglecting to adjust for risk factors, such as country or industry-specific risks, can also lead to flawed analysis. These errors highlight the importance of considering multiple factors and using a comprehensive approach when conducting fundamental analysis to ensure a more accurate assessment of a company's potential for future growth and profitability.

## Advanced

Fundamental analysis in economics finance has several advanced extensions that are relevant at the graduate level. One key area is the integration of financial statement analysis with quantitative models, such as discounted cash flow (DCF) models and residual income models. These models require estimates of future cash flows, discount rates, and terminal values, which are typically derived from a firm's financial statements and industry trends. Another area of advancement is the incorporation of macroeconomic and market-wide factors into fundamental analysis, such as the impact of monetary policy, fiscal policy, and geopolitical events on stock prices and firm valuations. Additionally, the use of machine learning and natural language processing techniques to analyze large datasets, including financial statements, news articles, and social media posts, is becoming increasingly important in fundamental analysis. Open questions in the field include the optimal weighting of different fundamental factors, such as earnings, dividends, and book value, in predicting stock returns, as well as the role of behavioral biases and market inefficiencies in shaping stock prices. The field is moving towards a more integrated approach, combining fundamental analysis with other methods, such as technical analysis and quantitative trading, to form a more comprehensive view of the markets.

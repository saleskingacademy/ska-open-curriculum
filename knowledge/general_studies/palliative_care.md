# Palliative Care

> The course teaches core concepts, principles, and frameworks in Palliative Care, assuming some basic knowledge of medical terminology and concepts.

Palliative care is a medical approach that focuses on alleviating the symptoms, pain, and stress of a serious illness, rather than curing the disease itself. A **serious illness** is a medical condition that significantly impacts an individual's quality of life, such as cancer, heart disease, or neurodegenerative disorders. **Symptom management** is a crucial aspect of palliative care, involving the use of pharmacological and non-pharmacological interventions to reduce the burden of symptoms such as pain, nausea, and shortness of breath. **Pain**, in this context, refers to any unpleasant sensory or emotional experience, including physical, emotional, or spiritual distress. **Quality of life** encompasses the physical, emotional, social, and spiritual well-being of an individual, and is a primary concern in palliative care. A **palliative care team** typically consists of healthcare professionals from various disciplines, including medicine, nursing, social work, and spiritual care, who work together to provide comprehensive care to patients and their families. **Comprehensive care** involves addressing the physical, emotional, social, and spiritual needs of patients, and may include services such as **hospice care**, which provides support and care to individuals with a terminal illness and their loved ones. **Terminal illness** refers to a medical condition that is incurable and will likely result in the patient's death. Understanding these core definitions and principles is essential for practitioners to provide effective palliative care. **Quality of life** refers to a patient's overall well-being, encompassing physical, emotional, social, and spiritual aspects. Palliative care is provided by an **interdisciplinary team**, including physicians, nurses, social workers, and other healthcare professionals who work together to address the patient's complex needs. **Patient-centered care** is a fundamental principle of palliative care, emphasizing the patient's unique values, preferences, and goals. **Holistic care** is another key principle, considering the patient's physical, emotional, social, and spiritual needs to provide comprehensive support.

## Mechanisms

Palliative care works through a multidisciplinary approach, integrating medical, nursing, and allied health professionals to address the physical, emotional, social, and spiritual needs of patients with life-limiting illnesses. The causal chain begins with a comprehensive assessment of the patient's condition, identifying symptoms, pain, and distress. This assessment informs the development of an individualized care plan, which may include pharmacological interventions, such as analgesics and adjuvant therapies, to manage pain and other symptoms. Non-pharmacological interventions, like counseling, cognitive-behavioral therapy, and complementary therapies, are also employed to address emotional and spiritual distress. The care plan is regularly reviewed and revised to ensure that it remains aligned with the patient's changing needs and priorities. Effective communication and collaboration among the healthcare team, patient, and family are crucial, facilitating shared decision-making and ensuring that care is patient-centered and culturally sensitive. By addressing the complex needs of patients with life-limiting illnesses, palliative care can improve quality of life, reduce symptom burden, and enhance patient and family satisfaction with care. The care plan is regularly reviewed and updated to reflect changes in the patient's condition, ensuring that their needs are continually met. Through this process, palliative care aims to improve the patient's quality of life, reducing suffering and enhancing their ability to cope with their illness. The mechanism involves a step-by-step process: 
1. Comprehensive assessment of the patient's symptoms, functional status, and psychological and social well-being.
2. Development of an individualized care plan, prioritizing the patient's goals and preferences.
3. Implementation of evidence-based interventions, such as pharmacological and non-pharmacological therapies, to manage pain, symptoms, and stress.
4. Ongoing evaluation and adjustment of the care plan to ensure optimal symptom control and quality of life.
5. Coordination of care across settings, including hospitals, hospices, and community-based services, to provide seamless support. The causal chain is as follows: effective palliative care leads to improved symptom management, which in turn enhances patient comfort, reduces distress, and promotes a better quality of life. This, in turn, can lead to increased patient and family satisfaction, reduced healthcare utilization, and improved bereavement outcomes for families.

## Methods And Frameworks

In palliative care, several methods and frameworks guide the delivery of comprehensive and patient-centered care. The Liverpool Care Pathway (LCP) is a care pathway that provides a framework for the care of dying patients, focusing on symptom control, comfort, and dignity. It is used when a patient is diagnosed as being in the last days or hours of life. However, its failure mode includes the risk of overly rigid application, leading to inadequate individualization of care. 
The Gold Standards Framework (GSF) is a framework for providing palliative care in primary care settings, focusing on early identification, assessment, and planning. It is used to improve the quality of care for patients with advanced illnesses. Its failure mode includes the risk of inadequate training and support for healthcare professionals. 
The Supportive Care Framework is a model that integrates palliative care into the overall care plan, focusing on physical, emotional, social, and spiritual support. It is used to address the complex needs of patients with advanced illnesses. Its failure mode includes the risk of inadequate communication and coordination among healthcare professionals. 
The Edmonton Symptom Assessment System (ESAS) is a tool used to assess the severity of symptoms in patients with advanced illnesses. It is used to monitor and manage symptoms, and its failure mode includes the risk of inadequate validation and reliability in certain patient populations. 
The Palliative Performance Scale (PPS) is a tool used to assess the functional status of patients with advanced illnesses. It is used to guide care planning and decision-making, and its failure mode includes the risk of inadequate sensitivity to changes in patient condition. 
These methods and frameworks are not mutually exclusive, and healthcare professionals often use a combination of approaches to provide comprehensive palliative care. It is used in the last days of life, typically when death is imminent. However, its use has been criticized due to concerns about inadequate training and potential for misuse, leading to its withdrawal in some countries. It is used to provide comprehensive care to patients with serious illnesses. It is used to monitor symptoms and adjust treatment plans accordingly. Each of these methods and frameworks has its own strengths and limitations, and healthcare providers must be aware of their potential failure modes, such as inadequate training, lack of communication, and insufficient resources.

## Worked Examples

1. **Opioid Dose Calculation**: A patient with advanced cancer is experiencing moderate to severe pain, requiring an opioid dose increase. The current dose is 20mg of oral morphine every 4 hours. To increase the dose by 25%, calculate the new dose. 
   - Current dose: 20mg
   - Increase percentage: 25%
   - Calculation: 20mg * 0.25 = 5mg increase
   - New dose: 20mg + 5mg = 25mg every 4 hours

2. **Symptom Management**: A patient has dyspnea (shortness of breath) with a respiratory rate of 24 breaths per minute. Using a numerical rating scale (0-10) for symptom severity, the patient rates their dyspnea as 7. After administering 2L of oxygen via nasal cannula, the patient's respiratory rate decreases to 20 breaths per minute, and they rate their dyspnea as 4. 
   - Initial symptom severity: 7
   - Intervention: 2L oxygen via nasal cannula
   - Outcome: Respiratory rate decreased to 20 breaths per minute, dyspnea rating decreased to 4

3. **Medication Adherence**: A patient is prescribed a regimen of medications including 10mg of oral haloperidol twice daily for nausea and vomiting, and 5mg of oral lorazepam at bedtime for anxiety. The patient reports taking only 50% of the prescribed haloperidol doses and 75% of the lorazepam doses over the past week. Calculate the average daily dose of each medication actually taken.
   - Haloperidol: 10mg * 2 doses * 0.5 = 10mg/day
   - Lorazepam: 5mg * 1 dose * 0.75 = 3.75mg/day

1. **Pain Management**: A 65-year-old patient with advanced cancer is experiencing moderate to severe pain, rated 7/10 on the numerical pain rating scale. The patient is currently taking 50mg of oral morphine every 4 hours. To improve pain control, the palliative care team decides to increase the dose by 25%. Calculate the new dose of morphine. 
New dose = 50mg * 1.25 = 62.5mg, which can be rounded to 65mg for convenience, thus the patient will take 65mg of oral morphine every 4 hours.

2. **Symptom Assessment**: A patient with end-stage heart failure reports shortness of breath (dyspnea) at rest, rated 8/10 on the dyspnea scale. The patient's oxygen saturation is 88% on room air. To improve symptoms, the palliative care team considers supplemental oxygen. If the patient's oxygen saturation increases to 92% with 2 liters per minute (L/min) of oxygen, calculate the percentage increase in oxygen saturation.
Percentage increase = ((92 - 88) / 88) * 100 = (4 / 88) * 100 = 4.55%, thus the oxygen saturation increases by approximately 4.55% with 2 L/min of supplemental oxygen.

3. **Medication Conversion**: A patient with chronic pain is being converted from oral oxycodone to transdermal fentanyl for better pain control and convenience. The patient is currently taking 40mg of oral oxycodone every 8 hours. Using the conversion ratio where 40mg of oral oxycodone is equivalent to 25mcg/hour of transdermal fentanyl, calculate the required dose of transdermal fentanyl.
First, calculate the total daily dose of oral oxycodone: 40mg * 3 = 120mg/day. Then, using the conversion ratio, the equivalent dose of transdermal fentanyl is 120mg/day * (25mcg/hour / 40mg) = 75mcg/hour, thus the patient will require a 75mcg/hour transdermal fentanyl patch.

The recommended dose adjustment for furosemide in patients with renal impairment is to reduce the dose by 50% if eGFR is <30ml/min. Calculate the new dose. 
New dose = 40mg * 0.5 = 20mg orally daily.

3. A patient with amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) is experiencing respiratory distress, with an oxygen saturation of 88% on room air. The palliative care team decides to initiate oxygen therapy to improve the patient's symptoms. If the patient requires a liter flow of 2L/min to achieve an oxygen saturation of 92%, and the oxygen tank contains 680L of oxygen, how many hours will the oxygen tank last? 
Hours = Total oxygen / (Liter flow * 60) = 680L / (2L/min * 60) = 680 / 120 = 5.67 hours.

## Applications

Palliative care is applied in various medical settings to alleviate symptoms, pain, and stress associated with serious illnesses. In practice, palliative care teams, comprising physicians, nurses, social workers, and other specialists, work together to provide comprehensive care. This care is often integrated into the treatment plan for patients with cancer, heart failure, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), and other life-limiting conditions. The primary goal is to improve the patient's quality of life by managing symptoms such as pain, nausea, and shortness of breath. Palliative care also involves addressing the patient's emotional, social, and spiritual needs, providing support to both the patient and their family. Additionally, palliative care teams help patients and their families make informed decisions about their care, including advance care planning and end-of-life care. Effective communication and collaboration between the palliative care team and other healthcare providers are essential to ensure seamless care coordination and optimal patient outcomes. By applying the principles of palliative care, healthcare providers can deliver patient-centered care that prioritizes comfort, dignity, and well-being. ## Common Errors

In palliative care, common mistakes include inadequate pain assessment and management, where practitioners fail to regularly reassess pain levels or do not use validated pain assessment tools, leading to undertreatment or overtreatment of pain. Another error is the underutilization of non-pharmacological interventions, such as cognitive-behavioral therapy or mindfulness-based stress reduction, which can complement pharmacological treatments and improve patient outcomes. Additionally, practitioners may not adequately address the emotional, social, and spiritual needs of patients and their families, neglecting the holistic approach that palliative care embodies. Insufficient communication and collaboration among the interdisciplinary team, including nurses, physicians, social workers, and chaplains, can also lead to fragmented care and poor patient outcomes. Furthermore, not respecting patient autonomy and preferences, such as not discussing advance care planning or do-not-resuscitate orders, can result in care that does not align with the patient's values and goals. These errors can be attributed to lack of training, inadequate resources, or insufficient time, highlighting the need for ongoing education and support for practitioners in palliative care. Another error is the underutilization of palliative care services, often due to late referrals or lack of awareness about the benefits of early palliative care integration. Insufficient communication with patients and families about prognosis, treatment options, and goals of care can also lead to misunderstandings and unrealistic expectations. Furthermore, practitioners may inappropriately withhold or withdraw treatments, such as antibiotics or artificial hydration, without thorough discussion with patients and families about the potential benefits and burdens. Additionally, the failure to address spiritual and existential distress can neglect a crucial aspect of patient care, as these concerns can significantly impact patients' quality of life and overall well-being. These errors can be attributed to various factors, including lack of training, insufficient time, and inadequate interdisciplinary collaboration. Another error is the underuse or misuse of opioids, such as not titrating doses appropriately or not addressing opioid-related side effects. Additionally, practitioners may neglect to address non-pain symptoms, such as dyspnea, nausea, or constipation, which can significantly impact patient quality of life. Furthermore, failure to involve interdisciplinary teams, including social workers, chaplains, and other specialists, can result in unmet patient and family needs.

## Advanced

The graduate-level extensions of palliative care involve the integration of specialized knowledge from various disciplines, including psychology, sociology, and ethics, to address complex patient needs. One key area of advancement is the development of personalized palliative care plans, which take into account individual patient preferences, values, and cultural backgrounds. Another area of focus is the use of technology, such as telehealth and mobile apps, to improve access to palliative care services, particularly for rural or underserved populations. Open questions in the field include the optimal timing and delivery of palliative care, with ongoing debates about the benefits of early versus late integration of palliative care into disease management. Additionally, there is a growing recognition of the need for palliative care to address the unique needs of diverse patient populations, such as those with dementia, intellectual disabilities, or serious mental illness. The field is moving towards a greater emphasis on patient-centered and family-centered care, with a focus on promoting resilience, coping, and overall well-being, rather than just managing symptoms and pain. Furthermore, there is an increasing recognition of the importance of palliative care in non-cancer conditions, such as heart failure, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), and neurodegenerative diseases, highlighting the need for further research and education in these areas. The field is moving towards a more comprehensive and holistic approach, incorporating elements of spiritual care, existential support, and caregiver support, with an emphasis on improving patient and family outcomes, and reducing healthcare disparities. Furthermore, research is ongoing to develop and validate new assessment tools and outcome measures, such as the Palliative Care Quality Network (PCQN) metrics, to evaluate the quality and effectiveness of palliative care services.

The field of palliative care is continually evolving, with ongoing research and advancements in symptom management, psychosocial support, and care delivery models. Graduate-level studies in palliative care delve into complex issues such as the intersection of palliative care with other medical specialties, including oncology, cardiology, and neurology. One key area of focus is the development of personalized palliative care plans, tailored to individual patient needs and preferences. This involves integrating patient-reported outcomes, such as symptom burden and quality of life, into clinical decision-making. Another critical area of investigation is the role of palliative care in non-cancer conditions, including chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), heart failure, and dementia. Open questions in the field include how to optimize palliative care delivery in resource-constrained settings, and how to address health disparities in access to palliative care services. The field is moving towards greater emphasis on early integration of palliative care into disease management, rather than solely as an end-of-life intervention. Additionally, there is a growing recognition of the importance of palliative care in pediatric populations, and the need for specialized training and resources to support this vulnerable group. As the field continues to evolve, it is likely that advances in technology, including telehealth and mobile health applications, will play an increasingly important role in expanding access to palliative care services.

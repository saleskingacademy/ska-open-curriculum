# Penetration Testing

> The course applies principles of cybersecurity to real situations, using standard tools and methodologies for penetration testing.

## Methodology

Reconnaissance (passive OSINT + active scanning) → Scanning (Nmap port/service detection, vulnerability scanners — Nessus, OpenVAS) → Exploitation (Metasploit, custom exploits) → Post-exploitation (lateral movement, privilege escalation, data exfiltration, persistence) → Reporting (findings, evidence, remediation). ## Foundations

In computer science, penetration testing, also known as pen testing or ethical hacking, refers to the practice of simulating cyber attacks on a computer system, network, or web application to assess its security vulnerabilities. A **vulnerability** is a weakness or flaw in a system's design, implementation, or configuration that can be exploited by an attacker to gain unauthorized access or disrupt service. The primary goal of penetration testing is to identify and prioritize vulnerabilities, allowing organizations to remediate them before they can be exploited by malicious actors. 
Key terms include **threat**, which refers to a potential occurrence that could compromise the security of a system, and **risk**, which is the likelihood and potential impact of a threat. A **penetration tester**, or **ethical hacker**, is a security professional who conducts penetration tests using various tools and techniques, such as **network scanning**, **vulnerability scanning**, and **exploitation** of identified vulnerabilities. The **attack vector** refers to the path or means by which an attacker gains access to a system, while **exploit** refers to the actual code or technique used to take advantage of a vulnerability. Understanding these core definitions and principles is essential for a practitioner to effectively conduct penetration testing and improve the security posture of an organization.

## Mechanisms

Penetration testing involves a series of steps that mimic real-world attacks to test the security of computer systems, networks, or applications. The process begins with planning and reconnaissance, where the tester gathers information about the target system, including network topology, operating systems, and potential vulnerabilities. This information is used to create a tailored attack plan. The next step is to scan and enumerate the target system, using tools such as Nmap or Nessus to identify open ports, services, and potential entry points. The tester then attempts to exploit identified vulnerabilities, using techniques such as SQL injection or cross-site scripting, to gain access to the system. Once access is gained, the tester attempts to escalate privileges, allowing for deeper access and control. The tester then maintains access, potentially installing backdoors or other malicious software to simulate a persistent threat. Finally, the tester covers their tracks, removing any evidence of the test to prevent detection. Throughout the process, the tester documents all steps, including successes and failures, to provide a comprehensive report of the system's vulnerabilities and recommendations for remediation. This causal chain of reconnaissance, exploitation, escalation, maintenance, and cover-up mimics the actions of a real attacker, allowing for a realistic assessment of the system's security posture.

## Methods And Frameworks

Penetration testing employs various methods and frameworks to simulate cyber attacks and identify vulnerabilities. The Open Source Security Testing Methodology Manual (OSSTMM) provides a comprehensive framework for penetration testing, emphasizing the importance of reconnaissance, targeting, and exploitation. The Penetration Testing Execution Standard (PTES) outlines a structured approach to testing, including pre-engagement, intelligence gathering, and post-exploitation. 
The Dolev-Yao model is used to analyze the security of communication protocols, focusing on the actions of an intruder. The Mitre ATT&CK framework provides a knowledge base of adversary tactics, techniques, and procedures, helping testers to simulate real-world attacks. 
The time-based method involves testing within a limited timeframe, while the scope-based method focuses on specific systems or applications. 
Failure modes include incomplete testing, inadequate reporting, and insufficient exploitation of identified vulnerabilities, highlighting the need for thorough planning, execution, and documentation.

## Worked Examples

To illustrate the concepts of penetration testing, consider the following examples. 
1. **Network Scanning**: A penetration tester is tasked with identifying open ports on a target system with IP address 192.168.1.100. Using Nmap, a popular network scanning tool, the tester runs the command `nmap -sS 192.168.1.100`. The results show that ports 22 (SSH) and 80 (HTTP) are open. The tester can then use this information to plan further attacks, such as attempting to brute-force the SSH password or exploiting a vulnerability in the web server.
2. **Vulnerability Exploitation**: A penetration tester discovers a vulnerability in a web application using the OWASP Zed Attack Proxy (ZAP) tool. The vulnerability is a SQL injection flaw, which allows an attacker to inject malicious SQL code into the application's database. The tester uses the Metasploit framework to exploit the vulnerability, gaining access to the database and extracting sensitive data.
3. **Password Cracking**: A penetration tester is tasked with cracking a password-protected ZIP file. Using the John the Ripper tool, the tester attempts to crack the password using a dictionary attack. After 30 minutes, the tool cracks the password, which is found to be a weak password ("password123"). The tester can then use this password to access the contents of the ZIP file, potentially gaining access to sensitive information. In each of these examples, the penetration tester uses specialized tools and techniques to simulate an attack on a target system, identifying vulnerabilities and weaknesses that can be exploited by an attacker.

## Applications

Penetration testing has numerous applications in computer science, primarily in the realm of cybersecurity. It is used to identify vulnerabilities in computer systems, networks, and applications, allowing organizations to strengthen their defenses against potential attacks. In practice, penetration testing is employed in various domains, including web application security, network security, and cloud security. For instance, in web application security, penetration testers use techniques such as SQL injection and cross-site scripting to simulate attacks on web applications, helping developers to identify and fix vulnerabilities. In network security, penetration testers use network scanning and enumeration techniques to discover open ports, identify operating systems, and detect potential entry points for attackers. Additionally, penetration testing is used in compliance testing, such as PCI DSS and HIPAA, to ensure that organizations meet regulatory requirements for security. Overall, the goal of penetration testing is to provide a realistic assessment of an organization's security posture, enabling them to prioritize and address vulnerabilities before they can be exploited by malicious actors.

## Common Errors

Penetration testers often make mistakes that can compromise the integrity of the test, lead to false positives or negatives, or even cause unintended damage to the target system. One common error is failing to properly scope the test, resulting in unauthorized access to sensitive areas or systems. Another mistake is not adequately configuring testing tools, leading to inaccurate or incomplete results. Additionally, testers may neglect to validate their findings, which can lead to false positives or incorrect conclusions. Insufficient documentation and reporting are also prevalent, making it difficult to reproduce or verify the results. Furthermore, testers may not properly handle sensitive information, such as credentials or personal data, which can result in security breaches or non-compliance with regulations. Lastly, testers often overlook the importance of continuous learning and professional development, failing to stay up-to-date with the latest threats, vulnerabilities, and testing methodologies, which can render their skills and knowledge obsolete. These errors can be mitigated by following industry standards, best practices, and guidelines, such as those provided by OWASP, PTES, and OSCP.

Penetration testing practitioners often make mistakes that can compromise the effectiveness and validity of their tests. One common error is failing to obtain proper authorization and scope definition from the client, leading to potential legal and reputational risks. Another mistake is not adequately testing for vulnerabilities in non-technical areas, such as social engineering and physical security. Many practitioners also rely too heavily on automated tools, neglecting to perform thorough manual testing and analysis. Additionally, some testers may not properly document their findings and methodologies, making it difficult to reproduce and verify their results. Furthermore, failing to consider the potential impact of the test on the target system and its users can lead to unintended downtime or disruption. Practitioners may also overlook the importance of maintaining confidentiality and integrity of sensitive data encountered during the test. These errors can be attributed to inadequate training, lack of experience, or insufficient understanding of the testing methodology and its limitations.

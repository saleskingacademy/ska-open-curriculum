# Cryo Electron Microscopy

> The course teaches core concepts and principles of cryo electron microscopy, assuming some basic knowledge of scientific terminology.

## Foundations

Cryo electron microscopy (cryo-EM) is a technique that combines cryogenic preservation of specimens with electron microscopy to produce high-resolution images of biological molecules. The core definition of cryo-EM involves the use of a transmission electron microscope (TEM) to image specimens preserved in a vitrified, or glassy, state. Vitrification is the process of rapidly cooling a specimen to a temperature at which the water molecules within the specimen become immobilized, forming a glass-like solid. This process prevents the formation of ice crystals, which can damage the specimen's structure. The first principle of cryo-EM is that the specimen must be preserved in a near-native state, meaning that the molecular structure and arrangement of the specimen are maintained as closely as possible to their natural state. Key vocabulary includes: electron microscopy, which refers to the use of a beam of electrons to produce an image of a specimen; transmission electron microscopy, which involves the transmission of electrons through a thin specimen; and cryogenic preservation, which refers to the use of low temperatures to preserve a specimen. A practitioner of cryo-EM must also be familiar with terms such as: contrast, which refers to the difference in electron density between different regions of the specimen; resolution, which refers to the minimum distance between two points that can be distinguished as separate; and pixel, which is the smallest unit of digital information in an image. Additionally, understanding of Fourier transform, which is a mathematical operation used to convert spatial information into frequency information, is essential for image processing and analysis in cryo-EM.

## Mechanisms

Cryo electron microscopy (cryo-EM) operates by leveraging the principles of electron microscopy and cryogenic preservation to visualize the structure of biological specimens at near-atomic resolution. The process begins with specimen preparation, where biological samples, such as proteins, viruses, or cells, are rapidly frozen in a cryogen, typically liquid ethane or nitrogen, to preserve their native structure. This rapid freezing, known as vitrification, prevents the formation of ice crystals that would otherwise damage the specimen. The frozen specimen is then inserted into the cryo-EM instrument, where it is maintained at cryogenic temperatures to prevent degradation. An electron beam is generated and focused onto the specimen, and the scattered electrons are detected by a camera, producing a two-dimensional image. The electron beam's interaction with the specimen is based on the principle that electrons scatter differently depending on the density of the material they encounter, allowing for the differentiation of various components within the biological sample. By collecting multiple images of the specimen from different angles, a three-dimensional reconstruction can be computed using algorithms that align and merge the two-dimensional projections, resulting in a high-resolution structure of the biological specimen. This structure can then be interpreted to understand the arrangement of atoms, molecules, and larger complexes within the specimen, providing insights into the mechanisms and functions of biological systems.

## Methods And Frameworks

Cryo electron microscopy (cryo-EM) employs several methods and frameworks to determine the three-dimensional structure of biological molecules. Single Particle Analysis (SPA) is used to study individual protein complexes, involving the collection of 2D images of randomly oriented particles, which are then combined to form a 3D reconstruction. When to use: SPA is ideal for studying proteins with multiple conformations or those that are difficult to crystallize. Failure mode: Heterogeneity in the sample can lead to incorrect or incomplete reconstructions. 
Helical reconstruction is used for filamentous or helical structures, such as microtubules or actin filaments. When to use: This method is suitable for studying the structure of filaments or helices with a repeating pattern. Failure mode: Incorrect determination of the helical parameters can result in an incorrect reconstruction. 
The Random Conical Tilt (RCT) method is used to determine the 3D structure of a specimen by collecting images at different tilt angles. When to use: RCT is useful for studying the structure of large or complex biological molecules. Failure mode: Insufficient tilt angles or incorrect determination of the tilt angles can lead to an incomplete or incorrect reconstruction. 
The Contrast Transfer Function (CTF) correction is essential in cryo-EM to account for the phase contrast introduced by the microscope. When to use: CTF correction should be applied to all cryo-EM images to ensure accurate interpretation of the data. Failure mode: Incorrect CTF correction can lead to artifacts or incorrect interpretation of the data. 
The Fourier Shell Correlation (FSC) is used to evaluate the resolution of a 3D reconstruction. When to use: FSC should be used to assess the resolution of all cryo-EM reconstructions. Failure mode: Incorrect determination of the FSC threshold can lead to an overestimation or underestimation of the resolution.

## Applications

Cryo Electron Microscopy (Cryo-EM) has revolutionized the field of life sciences by providing high-resolution structures of biological molecules and complexes. In practice, Cryo-EM is used to determine the three-dimensional structure of proteins, viruses, and cellular organelles. This technique is particularly useful for studying large and flexible molecules that are difficult to crystallize, such as membrane proteins and protein complexes. Cryo-EM has been instrumental in understanding the structure and function of various biological systems, including the ribosome, proteasome, and viral capsids. Additionally, Cryo-EM has been used to study the structure of amyloid fibrils and other disease-related proteins, providing valuable insights into the mechanisms of neurodegenerative diseases such as Alzheimer's and Parkinson's. The high-resolution structures obtained from Cryo-EM have also facilitated the development of novel therapeutic strategies, including structure-based drug design and vaccine development. Furthermore, Cryo-EM has enabled the study of cellular structures and dynamics at the nanoscale, allowing researchers to visualize and understand the organization and function of cellular components in their native state. Overall, Cryo-EM has become an essential tool in the life sciences, enabling researchers to gain a deeper understanding of the molecular mechanisms underlying various biological processes and diseases.

## Common Errors

In Cryo Electron Microscopy (Cryo-EM), several common errors can occur, affecting the quality and reliability of the results. One mistake is incorrect sample preparation, such as inadequate vitrification, leading to ice crystal formation, which can cause structural damage to the specimen. Another error is improper grid handling, resulting in contamination, mechanical stress, or exposure to air, which can alter the specimen's structure. Additionally, incorrect microscope settings, such as insufficient or excessive electron dose, can lead to radiation damage or poor image quality. Furthermore, errors in data processing, like incorrect particle picking or inadequate 2D classification, can introduce bias and affect the accuracy of the 3D reconstruction. Practitioners must also be aware of the limitations of Cryo-EM, such as the difficulty in resolving small or flexible structures, and avoid over-interpreting the results. These errors can be mitigated by following established protocols, using proper equipment and software, and critically evaluating the results. Understanding the underlying principles of Cryo-EM and being aware of these common errors can help practitioners optimize their experiments and obtain high-quality results.

## Advanced

Open questions in the field include the development of methods for routine determination of structures at near-atomic resolution, improving the efficiency of data collection and processing, and addressing the challenges of analyzing heterogeneous and dynamic systems. Additionally, the integration of cryo-EM with other omics approaches, such as proteomics and genomics, is expected to provide a more comprehensive understanding of biological systems and their dynamics. The use of cryo-EM in combination with other biophysical techniques, such as mass spectrometry and molecular dynamics simulations, is also an active area of research, enabling the study of biological systems in unprecedented detail.

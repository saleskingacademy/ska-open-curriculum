# Cryo Electron Microscopy

> The course teaches core concepts and principles of cryo electron microscopy, assuming some basic knowledge of scientific terminology.

## Foundations

Cryo electron microscopy (cryo-EM) is a technique that combines cryogenic preservation of specimens with electron microscopy to produce high-resolution images of biological molecules. The core definition of cryo-EM involves the use of a transmission electron microscope (TEM) to image specimens preserved in a vitrified, or glassy, state. Vitrification is the process of rapidly cooling a specimen to a temperature at which the water molecules within the specimen become immobilized, forming a glass-like solid. This process prevents the formation of ice crystals, which can damage the specimen's structure. The first principle of cryo-EM is that the specimen must be preserved in a near-native state, meaning that the molecular structure and arrangement of the specimen are maintained as closely as possible to their natural state. Key vocabulary includes: electron microscopy, which refers to the use of a beam of electrons to produce an image of a specimen; transmission electron microscopy, which involves the transmission of electrons through a thin specimen; and cryogenic preservation, which refers to the use of low temperatures to preserve a specimen. A practitioner of cryo-EM must also be familiar with terms such as: contrast, which refers to the difference in electron density between different regions of the specimen; resolution, which refers to the minimum distance between two points that can be distinguished as separate; and pixel, which is the smallest unit of digital information in an image. Additionally, understanding of Fourier transform, which is a mathematical operation used to convert spatial information into frequency information, is essential for image processing and analysis in cryo-EM.

Cryo Electron Microscopy (Cryo-EM) is a life sciences technique that utilizes electrons to image specimens at cryogenic temperatures, typically near liquid nitrogen temperatures (-196°C). The core principle of Cryo-EM is based on the concept of **electron diffraction**, where a beam of electrons interacts with the specimen, producing a diffraction pattern that contains information about the specimen's structure. **Specimens** in Cryo-EM refer to biological samples, such as proteins, viruses, or cells, that are preserved in a **vitreous** (glassy) state by rapid freezing, a process known as **vitrification**. This preservation method prevents the formation of **ice crystals**, which can damage the specimen's structure. The **electron microscope** used in Cryo-EM is a highly specialized instrument that consists of an **electron gun**, which produces the electron beam, and a series of **electromagnetic lenses**, which focus and direct the beam. The resulting **electron micrograph** is a two-dimensional representation of the specimen's structure, which can be used to reconstruct a **three-dimensional** model of the specimen using **single-particle analysis** or **tomography** techniques. Key vocabulary in Cryo-EM includes **resolution**, which refers to the minimum distance between two points that can be distinguished in the image, and **contrast**, which refers to the difference in electron density between different regions of the specimen. Understanding these core definitions and principles is essential for practitioners of Cryo-EM to interpret and analyze the resulting images and models.

## Mechanisms

Cryo electron microscopy (cryo-EM) operates by leveraging the principles of electron optics and cryogenic sample preparation to visualize the structure of biological specimens at near-atomic resolution. The process begins with the preparation of the sample, where biological molecules such as proteins, viruses, or cells are rapidly frozen in a vitrified state, preserving their native structure. This is achieved through plunge freezing, where the sample is submerged in a cryogen, typically liquid ethane or liquid nitrogen, to prevent the formation of ice crystals that could distort the specimen's structure. The frozen sample is then mounted onto an electron microscope grid and inserted into the cryo-EM instrument. Inside the microscope, a high-energy electron beam is generated and focused onto the sample using electromagnetic lenses. The electrons interact with the sample, producing a scattering pattern that is detected by a camera, typically a direct electron detector. The detected electrons form an image, which is then processed using sophisticated computational algorithms to correct for distortions and aberrations introduced by the microscope and the detector. Through a process known as single-particle analysis, multiple images of identical particles are aligned and averaged to enhance the signal-to-noise ratio, ultimately yielding a high-resolution three-dimensional reconstruction of the specimen's structure. This reconstruction is based on the principle of Fourier transform, where the two-dimensional images are transformed into a three-dimensional volume, allowing for the detailed visualization of the specimen's molecular architecture. The frozen specimen is then inserted into the electron microscope, where it is illuminated with a beam of electrons. The electrons interact with the specimen, scattering off its constituent atoms, and this scattered electron signal is detected by a camera, typically a direct electron detector. The detected signal is then used to reconstruct a two-dimensional image of the specimen. The causal chain is as follows: rapid freezing preserves the specimen's structure, electron illumination scatters electrons off the specimen, detected electrons form an image, and multiple images from different angles are combined to form a 3D reconstruction, ultimately revealing the specimen's structure at high resolution.

## Methods And Frameworks

Cryo electron microscopy (cryo-EM) employs several methods and frameworks to determine the three-dimensional structure of biological molecules. Single Particle Analysis (SPA) is used to determine the structure of individual particles, such as proteins or viruses, by averaging multiple two-dimensional images. SPA is suitable for samples with high particle heterogeneity and is commonly used for structural studies of large biomolecules. However, it can be limited by the requirement for a large number of particles and the potential for particle aggregation. 
Another approach is Electron Cryo-Tomography (ECT), which involves the reconstruction of three-dimensional images from a series of two-dimensional images taken at different tilt angles. ECT is ideal for studying the structure of whole cells, organelles, or large molecular complexes in their native state. Nevertheless, ECT can be affected by the limited tilt range and the resulting missing wedge of information, leading to reduced resolution in the reconstructed tomogram. 
The Random Conical Tilt (RCT) method is used to estimate the resolution of cryo-EM reconstructions by analyzing the distribution of particle orientations. The RCT method is essential for validating the resolution of cryo-EM structures but can be sensitive to the quality of the input data and the choice of parameters. 
In terms of models, the Contrast Transfer Function (CTF) model is used to correct for the phase contrast transfer function of the electron microscope, which affects the contrast and resolution of the images. The CTF model is crucial for accurate image processing and reconstruction but requires accurate estimation of the defocus and astigmatism of the microscope. 
The Fourier Shell Correlation (FSC) formula is used to estimate the resolution of cryo-EM reconstructions by comparing the correlation between two independent half-maps. The FSC formula is widely used for resolution estimation but can be influenced by the choice of threshold and the presence of noise in the data. Single Particle Analysis (SPA) is used to determine the structure of individual particles, such as proteins or viruses, by averaging multiple two-dimensional images.SPA is suitable for samples with high molecular weight and symmetry, but fails when particles have low contrast or high conformational heterogeneity. Helical Reconstruction is used for samples with helical symmetry, such as microtubules or actin filaments, by applying helical reconstruction algorithms to two-dimensional images. This method is suitable for samples with well-defined helical symmetry, but fails when the helical symmetry is disrupted or when the sample has high disorder. Tomography is used to determine the three-dimensional structure of whole cells or organelles by reconstructing a three-dimensional image from a series of two-dimensional images taken at different angles. Tomography is suitable for samples with complex morphology, but fails when the sample is too thick or has high density, leading to poor image quality. The Contrast Transfer Function (CTF) is a mathematical model used to correct for the effects of aberrations in the electron microscope, allowing for more accurate determination of the sample's structure. The CTF is suitable for samples with high contrast, but fails when the sample has low contrast or when the CTF is not accurately determined.

Assuming a pixel size of 1.4 Å, the required number of electrons per pixel can be estimated based on the desired resolution and the characteristics of the microscope.

The required dose of electrons depends on the desired resolution, the characteristics of the microscope, and the properties of the sample, such as its thickness and composition.

## Common Errors

In Cryo Electron Microscopy (Cryo-EM), several common errors can occur, leading to inaccurate or misleading results. One of the primary mistakes is incorrect sample preparation, including inadequate vitrification, which can cause ice crystal formation, leading to specimen degradation and loss of high-resolution detail. Another error is improper grid handling, such as touching the grid surface, introducing contaminants, or exposing the sample to air, resulting in ice sublimation and sample degradation. Additionally, incorrect microscope settings, including improper defocus values, can lead to aberrant contrast transfer function (CTF) and incorrect interpretation of the data. Insufficient or incorrect particle picking and 2D classification can also introduce bias and reduce the accuracy of 3D reconstructions. Furthermore, failure to account for the contrast transfer function (CTF) and anisotropic magnification can lead to incorrect interpretation of the data. These errors can be mitigated by following established protocols, using proper sample handling and preparation techniques, and carefully optimizing microscope settings and data processing parameters. Understanding the principles of Cryo-EM and being aware of these common errors can help practitioners to avoid them and produce high-quality, reliable results. ## Advanced

Cryo electron microscopy (cryo-EM) has undergone significant advancements in recent years, enabling the determination of high-resolution structures of biological molecules and complexes. Graduate-level research in cryo-EM focuses on overcoming current limitations, such as ab initio modeling, automated data processing, and integration with other structural biology techniques. One of the open questions in the field is the development of methods to determine the structures of small proteins and peptides, which are challenging due to their low molecular weight and high mobility. Researchers are also exploring the use of cryo-EM to study dynamic processes, such as protein-ligand interactions and conformational changes, by combining it with techniques like molecular dynamics simulations and single-particle analysis. Furthermore, the integration of cryo-EM with other omics approaches, such as proteomics and genomics, is expected to provide a more comprehensive understanding of biological systems. The field is moving towards the development of more sophisticated image processing algorithms, improved detector technologies, and the application of machine learning and artificial intelligence to enhance data analysis and interpretation. Additionally, the use of cryo-EM in the study of cellular tomography and in situ structures is becoming increasingly important, allowing researchers to visualize biological molecules in their native environment. Overall, the advancements in cryo-EM are expected to continue to revolutionize the field of structural biology and our understanding of biological systems. Graduate-level research in cryo-EM focuses on addressing the current limitations and challenges in the field. One of the key areas of extension is the development of new image processing algorithms and software packages, such as RELION and cisTEM, which enable more efficient and accurate processing of large cryo-EM datasets. Another area of advancement is the integration of cryo-EM with other structural biology techniques, such as X-ray crystallography and nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopy, to provide a more comprehensive understanding of biological systems. Open questions in the field include the development of methods for routine determination of atomic-level structures using cryo-EM, as well as the application of cryo-EM to study dynamic and heterogeneous systems, such as membrane proteins and amyloid fibrils. The field is moving towards the development of new technologies, such as cryo-electron tomography (cryo-ET) and single-particle analysis (SPA), which enable the study of biological systems in situ and at the nanoscale. Additionally, the increasing availability of high-performance computing resources and advanced machine learning algorithms is expected to play a crucial role in advancing the field of cryo-EM and enabling new breakthroughs in our understanding of biological systems.

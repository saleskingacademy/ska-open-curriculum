# Ancient Mesoamerica

> The course assumes some basic knowledge of humanities and introduces core principles and concepts of Ancient Mesoamerica.

## Olmec (1500-400 Bce)

"Mother culture" of Mesoamerica. Colossal stone heads (up to 3.4m, 50 tons — basalt transported 80km). Long Count calendar origins. Ball game. Jaguar worship. San Lorenzo, La Venta. MAYA (2000 BCE-1500 CE): Writing — most complete pre-Columbian script (800+ glyphs, fully decoded only in 1980s-90s). MATHEMATICS: Independent invention of zero (before India/Mesopotamia), vigesimal (base-20) system. ASTRONOMY: Predicted Venus cycles to within 2 hours over 500 years. Solar year calculated to 365.2420 days (actual 365.2422 — more accurate than Gregorian calendar of their era). Dresden Codex. Cities: Tikal, Palenque, Copán, Chichén Itzá. Classic Period collapse (~900 CE — drought, warfare, deforestation). TEOTIHUACAN (100 BCE-550 CE): Pyramid of the Sun (third-largest pyramid on Earth). 200K population — one of world's largest cities. Mysterious — no writing, unknown language. Massive influence throughout Mesoamerica. AZTEC/MEXICA (1325-1521): Tenochtitlan — island city, 200K+, larger than most European capitals. Causeways, aqueducts, chinampas (floating gardens). Sophisticated tribute system. Sun Stone (calendar stone). Fell to Cortés 1521 (with indigenous allies, smallpox). INCA (1438-1533): Largest empire in pre-Columbian Americas. 12M people, 4000km road system through Andes. Quipu (knotted strings for record-keeping — potentially a full writing system). Machu Picchu (1450). Terraced agriculture. No wheel, no iron, no writing — yet built one of history's most sophisticated empires.

## Foundations

In the humanities, Ancient Mesoamerica refers to the region that encompasses modern-day Mexico, Guatemala, Belize, Honduras, and El Salvador, where complex societies and civilizations flourished from approximately 2000 BCE to 1521 CE. A practitioner must understand the core definitions and vocabulary to study this subject. 
Mesoamerica is defined as a cultural area, characterized by a set of shared traits, including a common linguistic heritage, such as the Mayan and Nahuatl languages, and a distinct architectural style, exemplified by the use of pyramids, temples, and ball courts. 
The term civilization, in this context, denotes a complex society with a high degree of social stratification, political organization, and economic specialization, as seen in the Aztec and Mayan empires. 
Key concepts include chronology, which is divided into periods such as the Formative (2000 BCE - 250 CE), Classic (250 - 900 CE), and Postclassic (900 - 1521 CE), each marked by distinct cultural and artistic developments. 
Other essential terms are archaeology, the scientific study of past human cultures through material remains, and epigraphy, the study of inscriptions and written texts, which provide crucial evidence for understanding Ancient Mesoamerican cultures. 
A humanities practitioner must also be familiar with the concept of cultural diffusion, which refers to the spread of ideas, technologies, and practices between different societies, and the role of trade networks, such as the obsidian trade, in facilitating this exchange. 
Understanding these foundational concepts and vocabulary is crucial for analyzing and interpreting the history, culture, and achievements of Ancient Mesoamerica.

## Mechanisms

In the humanities study of Ancient Mesoamerica, several mechanisms facilitate our understanding of this complex and multifaceted civilization. Firstly, the process of archaeological excavation and discovery allows for the unearthing of artifacts, which are then subjected to various forms of analysis, including radiocarbon dating and stylistic interpretation. These analyses provide a chronological framework, enabling researchers to reconstruct the historical narrative of Ancient Mesoamerica. 
The deciphering of Mesoamerican writing systems, such as Maya hieroglyphs, is another crucial mechanism, as it permits the direct interpretation of indigenous texts and inscriptions, offering insights into the culture's mythology, politics, and daily life. 
Furthermore, the application of anthropological and sociological theories, like cultural evolution and structuralism, helps scholars to contextualize and make sense of the data collected from archaeological sites and written records. 
Additionally, the integration of ethnohistorical accounts, which are based on the records of early European colonizers and the oral traditions of indigenous peoples, provides a more nuanced understanding of the historical events and cultural practices of Ancient Mesoamerica. 
Lastly, the ongoing dialogue between scholars from diverse disciplines, including archaeology, anthropology, history, and linguistics, ensures a multidisciplinary approach, fostering a richer and more comprehensive comprehension of Ancient Mesoamerica.

In the humanities study of Ancient Mesoamerica, mechanisms refer to the complex systems and processes that drove the development and interactions of civilizations such as the Olmec, Maya, Aztec, and others. A key mechanism is the dynamic of trade networks, where the exchange of goods like cacao, obsidian, and feathers facilitated cultural and technological diffusion. Step by step, this process worked as follows: 
1. **Resource Availability**: The presence of specific resources in different regions created a basis for trade, with areas rich in one resource seeking others.
2. **Demand Creation**: As trade increased, so did the demand for exotic goods, which in turn fueled further trade and the expansion of trade routes.
3. **Cultural Exchange**: Along with goods, ideas, myths, and technologies were exchanged, influencing the development of writing systems, architectural styles, and religious practices.
4. **Political Integration**: Successful trade networks often led to the formation of more complex societies, with mechanisms for governance, taxation, and warfare emerging to manage and protect trade routes and resources.
5. **Conflict and Competition**: The competition for resources and control over trade routes could lead to conflict, which in turn drove the development of military technologies and strategies.
6. **Adaptation and Evolution**: Through these interactions, Mesoamerican civilizations continuously adapted and evolved, with some collapsing and others rising to prominence.
This causal chain underscores how the mechanisms of trade, cultural exchange, political integration, conflict, and adaptation were interconnected, shaping the history of Ancient Mesoamerica.

## Methods And Frameworks

In the humanities study of Ancient Mesoamerica, several methods and frameworks are employed to analyze and interpret the cultural, historical, and archaeological data. 
1. **Hermeneutic Analysis**: This method involves the interpretation of texts, symbols, and artifacts to understand the cultural and historical context of Ancient Mesoamerica. It is used to analyze the meanings of myths, legends, and iconography. Failure mode: Over-interpretation or lack of contextual understanding.
2. **Structuralism**: This framework examines the underlying structures and patterns in Mesoamerican cultures, such as kinship systems, mythological themes, and architectural designs. It is used to identify recurring patterns and relationships. Failure mode: Overemphasis on structure at the expense of historical and cultural context.
3. **Postcolonial Theory**: This method critiques the impact of colonialism on the interpretation and representation of Ancient Mesoamerican cultures. It is used to analyze the power dynamics and biases in historical accounts and archaeological narratives. Failure mode: Overemphasis on colonialism as the sole factor shaping Mesoamerican cultures.
4. **Archaeological Typology**: This method involves the classification and analysis of artifacts, such as ceramics, sculptures, and architecture, to reconstruct the chronology and cultural development of Ancient Mesoamerica. It is used to date sites, identify cultural exchange networks, and trace the evolution of artistic and technological styles. Failure mode: Overreliance on typology at the expense of other forms of evidence, such as textual or ethnographic data.
5. **Ethnohistory**: This framework combines archaeological, historical, and ethnographic data to reconstruct the cultures and histories of Ancient Mesoamerica. It is used to analyze the interactions between different cultures, the impact of historical events, and the persistence of cultural traditions. Failure mode: Lack of critical evaluation of sources and potential biases in historical accounts. The Annales School approach is used to study long-term historical trends and structures, focusing on the intersection of economic, social, and environmental factors. This method is particularly useful for understanding the rise and fall of civilizations, but its failure mode lies in overlooking individual agency and short-term events. Postcolonial theory is applied to critique the dominant narratives and power structures imposed by European colonizers, highlighting the voices and perspectives of indigenous peoples. However, its failure mode can be an overemphasis on colonialism as the sole explanatory factor, neglecting internal dynamics and complexities. The model of cultural ecology, developed by Julian Steward, examines the adaptive relationships between human societies and their environments, which is useful for understanding the development of Mesoamerican civilizations. Nevertheless, its failure mode can be a deterministic view of environmental factors, downplaying the role of human agency and cultural innovation. The formula of "text-archaeology-context" is used to integrate written records, material culture, and historical context, providing a comprehensive understanding of Ancient Mesoamerican societies. This approach is particularly useful for analyzing the symbolic and ideological aspects of Mesoamerican cultures, but its failure mode lies in the potential for overreliance on a single type of evidence, neglecting the complexities and nuances of the data. Ultimately, a combination of these methods and frameworks, along with a critical awareness of their strengths and limitations, is necessary for a nuanced and multifaceted understanding of Ancient Mesoamerica.

## Common Errors

In the study of Ancient Mesoamerica, several common errors arise from misconceptions about the region's cultures, chronology, and interpretations of archaeological evidence. One mistake is the tendency to homogenize Mesoamerican civilizations, such as the Olmec, Maya, and Aztec, overlooking their distinct cultural, linguistic, and historical differences. This error stems from a lack of understanding of the complex regional dynamics and the unique characteristics of each civilization. Another error is the misattribution of achievements, where the accomplishments of one civilization are incorrectly assigned to another. For example, the origins of Mesoamerican writing systems are often incorrectly attributed to the Maya, when in fact, the earliest evidence of writing comes from the Olmec civilization. Additionally, the assumption that Mesoamerican societies were isolated from one another and from other regions is incorrect, as there is evidence of extensive trade networks and cultural exchange. The failure to consider the historical context and the cultural biases of early European colonizers and modern scholars can also lead to misinterpretations of Mesoamerican cultures and histories. Furthermore, the overreliance on Spanish colonial sources, which often reflect the perspectives of the conquerors rather than the conquered, can result in a distorted understanding of Ancient Mesoamerica. These errors highlight the importance of a nuanced and multidisciplinary approach to the study of Ancient Mesoamerica, incorporating archaeological, ethnographic, and historical evidence to reconstruct a more accurate understanding of the region's complex past.

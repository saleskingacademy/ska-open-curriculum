# Agile Scrum

> The course teaches core principles and vocabulary of Agile Scrum, assuming no prior study but requiring understanding of key concepts and terminology.

## Framework

Empirical process control (transparency, inspection, adaptation). Sprint cadence (1-4 weeks). ROLES: Product Owner (backlog prioritization, stakeholder management, ROI responsibility), Scrum Master (servant leader, impediment removal, process coaching), Development Team (cross-functional, self-organizing, 3-9 members). EVENTS: Sprint Planning (what + how, sprint goal), Daily Scrum (15 min, 3 questions), Sprint Review (demo, stakeholder feedback), Sprint Retrospective (inspect + adapt process). ARTIFACTS: Product Backlog (ordered list, DEEP: Detailed, Estimated, Emergent, Prioritized), Sprint Backlog (plan for sprint), Increment (sum of completed PBIs, meets Definition of Done). TOOLS: Jira, Azure DevOps, Monday, Trello. ADVANCED: Backlog refinement, story splitting (INVEST criteria), release planning, multi-team Scrum.

## Foundations

Agile Scrum is a framework used in business to manage and complete complex projects. **Agile** refers to the ability to respond quickly to change and prioritize flexibility. **Scrum** is a specific implementation of Agile, emphasizing teamwork, accountability, and iterative progress toward well-defined goals. The core definitions include: **Sprint**, a short, time-boxed period (usually 2-4 weeks) when a specific task is worked on and completed. **Product Owner**, responsible for defining and prioritizing the **Product Backlog**, a list of features or tasks to be completed. **Scrum Master**, facilitates the Scrum process, ensuring the team follows the principles and removes impediments. **Development Team**, a cross-functional group of individuals who work together to complete the tasks in the Sprint. Key vocabulary includes: **Backlog Refinement**, the process of reviewing and updating the Product Backlog; **Daily Scrum**, a brief meeting where team members share progress and plans; **Velocity**, a measure of the team's productivity and ability to complete tasks during a Sprint. Understanding these first principles and vocabulary is essential for a practitioner to effectively implement Agile Scrum in a business setting.

## Mechanisms

In Agile Scrum, the development process is divided into iterative cycles called Sprints, typically lasting 2-4 weeks. The causal chain begins with the Product Owner, who prioritizes and refines the Product Backlog, a list of features or user stories to be developed. The Scrum Team, consisting of the Product Owner, Scrum Master, and Development Team, collaborates to plan the Sprint, selecting the top-priority items from the Product Backlog. The Development Team then breaks down these items into actionable tasks, which are tracked on the Sprint Backlog. Daily Scrum meetings facilitate communication and progress tracking, allowing team members to share their accomplishments, plans, and obstacles. The Scrum Master ensures the team adheres to Scrum principles and removes impediments. At the end of the Sprint, the team conducts a Sprint Review, demonstrating the working product increment to stakeholders, and a Sprint Retrospective, reflecting on the process to identify improvements. This iterative cycle enables continuous improvement, flexibility, and delivery of working software in short cycles, allowing businesses to respond quickly to changing market conditions and customer needs.

## Methods And Frameworks

Agile Scrum employs various methods and frameworks to facilitate project management and team collaboration. The Scrum framework itself is a widely used approach, emphasizing iterative and incremental development. Key components include Sprint Planning, Daily Stand-ups, and Retrospectives. Kanban is another method, focusing on visualizing work, limiting work in progress, and continuous improvement. It is suitable for teams with variable or unpredictable workloads. The Lean software development method aims to minimize waste and maximize value, often used in conjunction with Scrum or Kanban. The Burn-down Chart is a model used to track progress, illustrating the amount of work remaining over time. It is effective for identifying trends and potential roadblocks. However, its failure mode lies in over-reliance on quantitative metrics, potentially overlooking qualitative aspects of project progress. The Kano Model is a formula used to categorize and prioritize features based on customer satisfaction, helping teams focus on high-impact deliverables. Its failure mode occurs when customer needs are misinterpreted or misunderstood, leading to misplaced priorities. Ultimately, the choice of method or framework depends on the team's specific needs, project requirements, and organizational culture.

## Worked Examples

To illustrate the application of Agile Scrum in a business context, consider the following examples. 
1. **Team Velocity Calculation**: A Scrum team completes 30 story points in Sprint 1 and 40 story points in Sprint 2. To calculate the team's velocity, we take the average of the story points completed in the two sprints: (30 + 40) / 2 = 35 story points per sprint. This means the team can commit to delivering approximately 35 story points in the next sprint.
2. **Sprint Planning**: A product owner has a backlog with the following user stories and their estimated story points: Story A (10 points), Story B (8 points), Story C (12 points), and Story D (5 points). The team's velocity is 20 story points per sprint. In Sprint planning, the team commits to delivering Story A and Story B, totaling 18 story points, which is within their velocity, allowing for some buffer in case any story takes longer than expected.
3. **Burn-Down Chart Analysis**: A Scrum team is working on a sprint with a total of 100 story points. After day 3 of a 10-day sprint, they have completed 20 story points. Using a burn-down chart, we plot the remaining work (100 - 20 = 80 story points) against the number of days remaining (7 days). If the team continues at this rate, they will burn down approximately 11.4 story points per day (20 story points / 3 days, projected over 7 days), indicating they might be on track to complete the sprint goals, but close monitoring is necessary to ensure they maintain this pace.

## Applications

In business, Agile Scrum is applied in various domains, primarily in software development, product management, and IT project management. The framework is used to facilitate team collaboration, iterative progress, and continuous improvement. Scrum teams, typically consisting of a Product Owner, Scrum Master, and Development Team, work together to deliver working products in short cycles, known as Sprints. The Product Owner prioritizes the product backlog, while the Scrum Master ensures the team adheres to Scrum principles and removes impediments. The Development Team develops the product increment during each Sprint, with daily Scrum meetings to review progress and plan tasks. This approach enables businesses to respond quickly to changing market conditions, customer needs, and technological advancements, ultimately driving innovation and competitiveness. By applying Agile Scrum, organizations can improve time-to-market, increase customer satisfaction, and enhance team productivity and morale.

## Common Errors

In Agile Scrum, common mistakes include misunderstanding the role of the Product Owner, who is responsible for prioritizing and refining the product backlog, not just a liaison between stakeholders and the development team. Another error is inadequate sprint planning, where teams fail to break down tasks into manageable chunks or neglect to establish clear sprint goals. Additionally, many teams incorrectly assume that Agile Scrum means no documentation or planning, when in fact, it emphasizes iterative and incremental delivery with continuous improvement. Teams also often misunderstand the concept of velocity, using it as a metric for team comparison rather than a tool for estimating future sprint capacity. Furthermore, neglecting to hold regular retrospectives or failing to implement changes from these meetings can hinder a team's ability to improve and adapt. Lastly, not empowering the Scrum Master to facilitate and remove impediments can lead to inefficiencies and undermine the Scrum framework's effectiveness. These errors often stem from a lack of understanding of Agile principles or inadequate training, highlighting the importance of proper education and implementation.

Additionally, teams often misinterpret the concept of "done," where the definition of done is not clearly established, leading to inconsistent quality and incomplete work. Lastly, ignoring the retrospective process, which is essential for continuous improvement, can prevent teams from identifying and addressing areas for improvement, ultimately hindering their ability to adapt and evolve. These mistakes can lead to decreased team efficiency, poor quality products, and failed Agile implementations.

The field is moving towards greater emphasis on business agility, which encompasses not only team-level agility but also organizational agility, with a focus on enabling companies to respond quickly to changing market conditions and customer needs.

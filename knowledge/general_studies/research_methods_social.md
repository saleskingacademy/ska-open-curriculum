# Research Methods Social

> The course teaches core concepts and principles of research methods in social sciences, assuming some basic knowledge of the field.

## Foundations

In the social sciences, research methods are systematic procedures used to collect and analyze data to answer research questions or test hypotheses. A **hypothesis** is a tentative explanation or assumption that can be tested through research. **Variables** are characteristics or attributes that can be measured or observed, and are often the focus of social science research. **Independent variables** are those that are manipulated or changed by the researcher, while **dependent variables** are those that are affected by the independent variable. **Research design** refers to the overall plan or strategy for conducting a study, including the selection of participants, data collection methods, and data analysis techniques. A **paradigm** is a theoretical framework or perspective that guides the research process, such as positivism or interpretivism. **Positivism** assumes that social phenomena can be studied using scientific methods and that objective knowledge can be obtained, while **interpretivism** emphasizes the importance of understanding social phenomena from the perspective of the individuals being studied. **Empirical research** relies on observation and experience, often involving the collection of **quantitative data** (numerical data) or **qualitative data** (non-numerical data). Understanding these core definitions and principles is essential for social science researchers to design and conduct rigorous and valid studies.

In social sciences, research methods are systematic approaches to investigating social phenomena, guided by core definitions, first principles, and a specific vocabulary. **Epistemology**, the study of knowledge and how it is produced, underlies all research, informing the **research paradigm**, which is the theoretical framework that shapes the research process. A **hypothesis** is a tentative explanation for a phenomenon, tested through **empirical research**, which involves collecting and analyzing data through observation or experiment. **Variables** are characteristics or factors that can be measured, manipulated, or controlled, and are categorized as **independent** (cause), **dependent** (effect), or **extraneous** (confounding). **Sampling** refers to the selection of a subset of individuals or cases from a larger **population**, using methods such as **probability sampling** or **purposive sampling**. **Data** can be **quantitative** (numerical) or **qualitative** (non-numerical), and is analyzed using **statistical methods** or **thematic analysis**, respectively. Understanding these foundational concepts is essential for social sciences researchers to design, implement, and interpret research studies.

## Mechanisms

In social sciences research, mechanisms refer to the underlying processes that explain how and why a particular phenomenon occurs. The causal chain involves a series of events or actions that lead to an outcome. It begins with the identification of a research question or problem, which guides the formulation of a hypothesis. The hypothesis is then tested through the collection and analysis of data, using methods such as surveys, interviews, or experiments. The data analysis reveals patterns or correlations, which are then interpreted to understand the underlying mechanisms. These mechanisms can include social, psychological, or economic factors that influence the phenomenon being studied. For example, in a study on the impact of education on poverty, the mechanism might involve increased earning potential, improved job prospects, and enhanced social mobility, which ultimately lead to a reduction in poverty. The explicit causal chain in this example would be: education increases human capital, human capital improves job prospects, better job prospects lead to higher earnings, and higher earnings reduce poverty. By elucidating these mechanisms, researchers can develop a deeper understanding of the complex relationships between variables and develop more effective interventions or policies to address social problems. The causal chain involves a series of steps that link the independent variable to the dependent variable, with each step representing a specific mechanism. The process begins with the identification of a research question or hypothesis, which guides the selection of relevant data collection methods, such as surveys, interviews, or observations. The data collected is then analyzed using statistical or qualitative methods to identify patterns and relationships. The analysis reveals correlations or associations between variables, which are then subjected to causal analysis to determine the underlying mechanisms. This involves examining the temporal order of events, the presence of a plausible causal link, and the absence of alternative explanations. The identified mechanisms are then used to develop a theoretical explanation for the observed phenomenon, which can be tested and refined through further research. The explicit causal chain is crucial in establishing the validity and reliability of the research findings, allowing for the development of evidence-based theories and interventions in social sciences.

## Methods And Frameworks

In social sciences research, various methods and frameworks are employed to collect and analyze data. 
1. **Survey Research**: involves collecting data through self-report measures, such as questionnaires or interviews. Use when: studying attitudes, opinions, or behaviors in a large population. Failure mode: social desirability bias, where respondents provide answers they think are socially acceptable.
2. **Experimental Design**: involves manipulating an independent variable to observe its effect on a dependent variable. Use when: testing causal relationships between variables. Failure mode: lack of control over extraneous variables, leading to confounding effects.
3. **Grounded Theory**: involves developing a theoretical framework through iterative data collection and analysis. Use when: exploring a new phenomenon or developing a substantive theory. Failure mode: overemphasis on emergent themes, neglecting existing literature and theoretical frameworks.
4. **Content Analysis**: involves systematically analyzing text or media data to identify patterns and themes. Use when: studying cultural or social phenomena through textual or visual data. Failure mode: coder bias, where the researcher's own perspectives influence the analysis.
5. **Social Network Analysis**: involves studying the relationships and structures within social networks. Use when: examining social influence, diffusion of information, or community dynamics. Failure mode: neglecting to account for network boundaries and sampling biases.
6. **Case Study**: involves in-depth analysis of a single case or a small number of cases. Use when: exploring complex phenomena, such as organizational or institutional dynamics. Failure mode: lack of generalizability to other contexts, due to unique case characteristics. The Grounded Theory Method, involving the collection and analysis of data to develop a theory, is useful for exploratory research. The Social Network Analysis Method examines relationships and interactions within a social network, useful for understanding social structures and dynamics. Failure modes include incomplete data and misinterpretation of network dynamics. The Statistical Analysis Method, using formulas such as regression and correlation, is useful for identifying patterns and relationships in numerical data. Failure modes include assumption violations and misinterpretation of results.

Each method has its strengths and limitations, and researchers must carefully select the appropriate method based on their research question and design.

## Worked Examples

To illustrate the application of research methods in social sciences, consider the following examples. 
1. **Survey Research**: A researcher aims to determine the percentage of students who use public transportation to commute to university. A sample of 400 students is selected, and 280 respond that they use public transportation. To calculate the percentage, divide the number of students using public transportation (280) by the total sample size (400), then multiply by 100: (280/400)*100 = 70%. 
2. **Content Analysis**: A study examines the portrayal of gender in advertisements. A sample of 100 advertisements is analyzed, and the number of ads featuring male and female models is counted. The results show 60 ads feature male models and 40 feature female models. To calculate the percentage of ads featuring female models, divide the number of ads with female models (40) by the total sample size (100), then multiply by 100: (40/100)*100 = 40%. 
3. **Correlational Study**: A researcher investigates the relationship between hours spent studying and exam scores. Data is collected from 50 students, showing a range of study hours (2-10 hours) and corresponding exam scores (50-90%). To calculate the correlation coefficient, first, calculate the mean of study hours (e.g., 6 hours) and exam scores (e.g., 75%). Then, calculate the deviations from the mean for each data point, multiply corresponding deviations, and sum these products. Finally, divide this sum by the product of the sum of squared deviations for study hours and exam scores. This calculation yields a correlation coefficient, which may range from -1 to 1, indicating the strength and direction of the relationship between study hours and exam scores. A sample of 100 advertisements is analyzed, and the researcher finds that 60 advertisements feature male protagonists, 30 feature female protagonists, and 10 feature both. Experimental Design: A researcher investigates the impact of a new teaching method on student performance. Two groups of 50 students each are formed: an experimental group using the new method and a control group using the traditional method. The average score of the experimental group is 80, and the average score of the control group is 70. To calculate the difference in average scores, subtract the control group average (70) from the experimental group average (80): 80 - 70 = 10. This indicates that the experimental group scored 10 points higher than the control group. Using a correlation coefficient formula, the researcher calculates a coefficient of 0.8, indicating a strong positive correlation between study hours and exam scores. This suggests that as study hours increase, exam scores also tend to increase.

In anthropology, research methods are utilized to study cultural practices, beliefs, and values, often involving ethnographic fieldwork and participant observation to gain in-depth insights into the lives of diverse communities. Additionally, research methods in social sciences are applied in policy evaluation, program development, and social intervention, enabling policymakers and practitioners to make informed decisions and develop effective solutions to social problems. In political science, content analysis is applied to examine political discourse, media representation, and public opinion. These methods are also used in program evaluation, needs assessment, and policy analysis to assess the effectiveness of social programs and policies. By applying research methods, social scientists can provide insights into complex social issues, such as inequality, social justice, and human well-being, ultimately informing evidence-based decision-making and practice. By applying research methods, social scientists can contribute to evidence-based decision-making, policy development, and social change.

## Common Errors

In social sciences research, common errors often stem from methodological flaws, sampling biases, and incorrect applications of statistical analysis. One prevalent mistake is the failure to establish a clear research question or hypothesis, leading to a lack of focus and direction in the study. Another error is the reliance on convenience sampling, which can result in a non-representative sample and compromise the generalizability of findings. Additionally, researchers may incorrectly assume that correlation implies causation, neglecting to consider alternative explanations and potential confounding variables. Furthermore, the misuse of statistical tests, such as applying parametric tests to non-normal data, can lead to inaccurate conclusions. Moreover, social desirability bias, where participants respond in a way they think is socially acceptable, can also threaten the validity of self-report data. These errors can be mitigated by carefully designing the study, selecting appropriate sampling and analysis methods, and considering potential sources of bias and error.

In social sciences research, common errors occur due to methodological flaws, sampling biases, and incorrect data analysis. One mistake is the failure to operationalize variables, leading to vague or unmeasurable constructs. For instance, using terms like "happiness" without defining how it will be measured can result in unreliable data. Another error is selection bias, where the sample is not representative of the population, often due to convenience sampling or self-selection. This can lead to overgeneralization of findings. Additionally, researchers may commit the error of assuming causality from correlation, neglecting to control for confounding variables. Ignoring the social desirability bias, where respondents provide answers they think are socially acceptable rather than their true beliefs, can also skew results. Furthermore, not considering the limitations of research instruments, such as surveys or interviews, can lead to inaccurate data collection. These errors can be mitigated by using rigorous research designs, piloting data collection tools, and critically evaluating the results.

Graduate-level extensions include the application of machine learning algorithms to large-scale social data, such as network analysis and natural language processing. Open questions in the field pertain to the validation of novel methods, the mitigation of biases in social data, and the development of more nuanced understandings of social phenomena. The field is moving towards increased interdisciplinary collaboration, incorporating insights from computer science, statistics, and philosophy to address complex social problems. Key areas of development include the use of Bayesian inference for causal analysis, the application of agent-based modeling to simulate social dynamics, and the integration of qualitative and quantitative methods to provide more comprehensive understandings of social phenomena. Furthermore, the increasing availability of big data and social media data raises important questions about data quality, ethics, and the role of research in informing social policy. As such, advanced research methods in social sciences require a deep understanding of the theoretical, methodological, and ethical considerations underlying social research.

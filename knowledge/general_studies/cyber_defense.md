# Cyber Defense

> The course assumes foundational knowledge and teaches applied practices of cyber defense.

## Foundations

Cyber defense is the discipline of protecting information systems, networks, and data from unauthorized access, disruption, modification, or destruction through proactive and reactive measures. Rooted in principles of confidentiality, integrity, and availability (the CIA triad), cyber defense integrates threat intelligence, vulnerability management, incident response, and resilience engineering to maintain operational security. At its core, cyber defense operates on the first principles of risk management: identifying assets, assessing threats and vulnerabilities, analyzing potential impacts, and implementing controls to mitigate risk to acceptable levels. It spans technical, procedural, and human domains, leveraging cryptography, network security, endpoint protection, and security operations to create layered defenses against adversaries ranging from script kiddies to nation-states.

In the context of military defense, Cyber Defense refers to the set of strategies, techniques, and actions taken to protect and defend military computer systems, networks, and cyber capabilities from cyber threats. A **cyber threat** is defined as any potential or actual adverse event that could compromise the confidentiality, integrity, or availability of military cyber systems. **Confidentiality** refers to the protection of sensitive information from unauthorized access or disclosure. **Integrity** refers to the accuracy, completeness, and consistency of data, while **availability** refers to the ability of authorized users to access and use cyber systems and data when needed.

Key concepts in Cyber Defense include **cybersecurity**, which encompasses the measures taken to prevent, detect, and respond to cyber threats, and **cyber resilience**, which refers to the ability of military cyber systems to withstand, recover from, and adapt to cyber attacks. A **cyber attack** is a deliberate attempt to disrupt, disable, or exploit military cyber systems, and can be launched by **adversaries**, including nation-state actors, terrorist organizations, or other malicious entities. **Cyber defense operations** involve the use of **cyber defense tools**, such as firewalls, intrusion detection systems, and encryption, to detect, respond to, and counter cyber threats. Understanding these core definitions and concepts is essential for effective Cyber Defense in a military context.

In the context of military defense, Cyber Defense refers to the protection of military computer systems, networks, and cyber terrain from unauthorized access, use, disclosure, disruption, modification, or destruction. This is achieved through a combination of people, processes, and technology. **Cyber terrain** is defined as the virtual environment where military operations are conducted, encompassing the internet, networks, and connected devices. **Computer Network Defense (CND)** is a critical component of Cyber Defense, focusing on detecting, responding to, and mitigating cyber threats against military computer networks. A **cyber threat** is a potential occurrence that could compromise the security of military cyber systems, including **malware** (software designed to harm or exploit systems), **phishing** (attempts to deceive individuals into revealing sensitive information), and **distributed denial-of-service (DDoS) attacks** (overwhelming a system with traffic to make it unavailable). **Information Assurance (IA)** is the practice of protecting military information systems from unauthorized access or disruption, ensuring the **confidentiality**, **integrity**, and **availability** of information. **Cybersecurity** is a broader concept that encompasses the protection of not only military systems but also the entire cyber ecosystem, including civilian infrastructure and commercial systems. Understanding these core definitions and principles is essential for a practitioner of Cyber Defense in a military context.

## Threat Modeling & Risk Assessment

STRIDE & DREAD  
STRIDE (Spoofing, Tampering, Repudiation, Information Disclosure, Denial of Service, Elevation of Privilege) is a threat classification model developed by Microsoft to systematically identify threats in a system design phase. Each category corresponds to a type of attack vector: e.g., spoofing targets authentication weaknesses, tampering targets data integrity. Threats identified via STRIDE are then prioritized using the DREAD model, which scores threats on Damage potential, Reproducibility, Exploitability, Affected users, and Discoverability, each rated 1–10. For example, a denial-of-service vulnerability with a DREAD score above 35 (out of 50) demands immediate mitigation. This combined framework guides security architects in threat prioritization and resource allocation.

## Vulnerability Management

CVSS & PATCH CYCLES  
The Common Vulnerability Scoring System (CVSS) v3.1 quantifies vulnerability severity on a 0.0–10.0 scale, incorporating Base, Temporal, and Environmental metrics. The Base score uses metrics such as Attack Vector (e.g., Network=0.85), Attack Complexity, Privileges Required, User Interaction, and Impact metrics (Confidentiality, Integrity, Availability). For example, CVE-2021-44228 (Log4Shell) scored 10.0 due to remote code execution via network vector with no privileges required. Effective vulnerability management mandates triaging CVSS scores: critical (≥9.0) patched within 24–48 hours, high (7.0–8.9) within one week, medium (4.0–6.9) within one month. Patch cycles must be integrated with continuous vulnerability scanning (e.g., Nessus, Qualys) and asset inventories to ensure coverage.

## Network Defense

DEFENSE-IN-DEPTH & ZERO TRUST  
Defense-in-depth employs layered security controls to prevent, detect, and respond to threats at multiple points: perimeter firewalls (e.g., Palo Alto Networks PA-Series), intrusion detection/prevention systems (IDS/IPS) like Snort or Suricata, network segmentation via VLANs and micro-segmentation (e.g., VMware NSX), and endpoint protection platforms (EPP) such as CrowdStrike Falcon. Complementing this is the Zero Trust model, which assumes no implicit trust regardless of network location. Zero Trust Architecture (ZTA), as defined by NIST SP 800-207, enforces continuous verification of user and device identities, least privilege access, and micro-segmentation. Implementation steps include strong multi-factor authentication (FIDO2, TOTP), device posture assessment, and real-time policy enforcement via Software Defined Perimeters (SDP).

## Security Operations & Incident Response

NIST SP 800-61  
Incident response follows the NIST Computer Security Incident Handling Guide (SP 800-61 Rev. 2) lifecycle: Preparation, Detection & Analysis, Containment, Eradication, Recovery, and Post-Incident Activity. Detection employs Security Information and Event Management (SIEM) systems like Splunk or IBM QRadar, which aggregate logs and apply correlation rules to identify anomalies. Containment strategies vary: e.g., isolating infected hosts via NAC (Network Access Control), blocking malicious IPs via firewall rules, or disabling compromised accounts. Eradication involves root cause removal, such as patching exploited vulnerabilities or removing malware. Recovery restores systems to operational status with integrity validation. Post-incident reviews generate lessons learned and update playbooks.

## Cryptography & Data Protection

AES & PKI  
Robust cyber defense relies on cryptographic primitives for confidentiality and integrity. AES (Advanced Encryption Standard) with 256-bit keys is the de facto symmetric cipher for data at rest and in transit, implemented in modes like Galois/Counter Mode (GCM) for authenticated encryption. Public Key Infrastructure (PKI) underpins identity and trust via asymmetric keys and digital certificates (X.509). Certificate Authorities (CAs) issue certificates validated through chains of trust. Key management best practices include hardware security modules (HSMs) for key storage, regular key rotation (e.g., every 90 days), and revocation via Certificate Revocation Lists (CRLs) or Online Certificate Status Protocol (OCSP). Cryptographic agility mandates readiness to migrate algorithms in response to advances in cryptanalysis or quantum threats.

## Threat Intelligence & Hunting

MITRE ATT&CK & IOC ANALYSIS  
The MITRE ATT&CK framework catalogs adversary tactics, techniques, and procedures (TTPs) across 14 tactics (e.g., Initial Access, Persistence, Lateral Movement). Cyber defenders map detected indicators of compromise (IOCs)—hashes, IP addresses, domain names—to ATT&CK techniques to understand attacker behavior and anticipate next steps. Threat hunting uses hypothesis-driven queries in endpoint detection and response (EDR) tools like Carbon Black or Microsoft Defender ATP to proactively seek hidden threats. For instance, hunting for unusual PowerShell usage (T1059.001) or credential dumping (T1003) involves analyzing process execution logs and memory dumps. Integrating threat intelligence feeds (e.g., STIX/TAXII) enhances situational awareness and informs proactive defenses.

## Mechanisms

In military cyber defense, the mechanisms involve a series of steps and processes that work together to protect military networks, systems, and data from cyber threats. The causal chain begins with threat detection, where advanced sensors and monitoring tools identify potential security breaches. This is followed by threat analysis, where the detected threats are evaluated to determine their severity and potential impact. The analysis informs the incident response, which involves containing and mitigating the threat to prevent further damage. 
The next step is eradication, where the root cause of the threat is removed, and the affected systems are restored to a known good state. This is followed by recovery, where normal operations are resumed, and the affected systems are brought back online. 
Throughout this process, continuous monitoring and feedback loops ensure that the defense mechanisms are effective and adaptive, allowing for real-time adjustments to counter evolving threats. The mechanisms also involve collaboration and information sharing between different military units and agencies to enhance situational awareness and coordinate responses to cyber threats. 
The underlying principle is to implement a layered defense approach, combining multiple security controls and mechanisms to provide robust protection against cyber threats. This includes firewalls, intrusion detection and prevention systems, encryption, and secure authentication and authorization protocols. 
By understanding the mechanisms of cyber defense, military organizations can develop effective strategies to protect their critical assets and maintain operational security in the face of increasingly sophisticated cyber threats.

In military cyber defense, the mechanisms involve a series of coordinated steps to detect, respond, and mitigate cyber threats. The process begins with network monitoring, where specialized software and hardware systems continuously scan the military's computer networks for signs of unauthorized access or malicious activity. This monitoring is typically performed using intrusion detection systems (IDS) and intrusion prevention systems (IPS), which analyze network traffic for patterns or anomalies that may indicate a cyber attack. Upon detection of a potential threat, the system triggers an alert, which is then analyzed by cyber defense personnel to determine the nature and severity of the threat. If the threat is confirmed, the cyber defense team initiates a response, which may involve isolating affected systems, blocking malicious traffic, or deploying countermeasures to neutralize the threat. The causal chain is as follows: detection leads to alerting, which leads to analysis, and then to response, with each step informing and influencing the next to effectively counter the cyber threat. Throughout this process, military cyber defense mechanisms rely on a combination of technological, procedural, and human factors to ensure the integrity and security of military networks and systems.

## Methods And Frameworks

In military cyber defense, several methods and frameworks are employed to protect against cyber threats. The NIST Cybersecurity Framework provides a structured approach to managing cyber risk, and is often used in conjunction with the Department of Defense's (DoD) Cybersecurity Risk Management Framework (RMF). The OODA (Observe, Orient, Decide, Act) loop is a decision-making model used to respond to cyber threats in real-time. The Kill Chain model is used to understand and disrupt adversary cyber attacks, by identifying and mitigating each stage of the attack. The CARVER (Criticality, Accessibility, Recuperability, Vulnerability, Effect, and Recognizability) shock method is a risk assessment framework used to identify and prioritize cyber vulnerabilities. Each of these methods has its own failure mode, such as the NIST framework being ineffective if not regularly updated, the OODA loop being vulnerable to information overload, and the Kill Chain model being limited by its reliance on accurate threat intelligence. Understanding these methods and their limitations is crucial for effective military cyber defense.

## Worked Examples

To illustrate the application of cyber defense principles in a military context, consider the following scenarios:

1. **Network Intrusion Detection**: A military network with 1000 devices is being monitored for potential intrusions. The network intrusion detection system (NIDS) flags 50 alerts per day, with a false positive rate of 20%. If the NIDS has a detection rate of 90% for legitimate threats, how many actual intrusions can be expected per day? 
Let's calculate: 50 alerts * (1 - 0.20) = 40 potential intrusions. Since the NIDS detects 90% of legitimate threats, the expected number of actual intrusions per day is 40 / 0.9 = 44.44.

2. **Encryption Key Management**: A military unit uses AES-256 encryption for secure communication. If the encryption key is changed every 30 days, and it takes 2 hours to generate and distribute a new key, how many personnel-hours per year are spent on key management? 
Calculate: 365 days / 30 days per key = 12.17 key changes per year. With 2 hours per key change, the total personnel-hours per year are 12.17 * 2 = 24.34 hours.

3. **Vulnerability Patching**: A military cyber defense team identifies 500 vulnerabilities in their network, with 20% being critical. If the team can patch 10 vulnerabilities per day, how many days will it take to patch all critical vulnerabilities? 
First, calculate the number of critical vulnerabilities: 500 * 0.20 = 100 critical vulnerabilities. Then, divide by the daily patching rate: 100 critical vulnerabilities / 10 patches per day = 10 days to patch all critical vulnerabilities.

## Applications

In military defense, Cyber Defense is applied in various domains to protect against cyber threats and maintain operational security. Network defense systems are employed to monitor and control network traffic, detecting and preventing intrusions. Endpoint security measures, such as firewalls and antivirus software, are used to protect individual devices and systems from malware and unauthorized access. Cryptographic techniques, including encryption and secure communication protocols, are utilized to safeguard sensitive information and maintain confidentiality. 
In tactical operations, Cyber Defense is used to secure command and control systems, ensuring the integrity of mission-critical data and preventing disruption of operational plans. Cyber situational awareness is maintained through continuous monitoring and analysis of network activity, enabling rapid response to emerging threats. 
Cyber Defense is also integrated into broader military operations, such as electronic warfare and information operations, to disrupt and degrade adversary capabilities. This includes conducting cyber reconnaissance, exploiting vulnerabilities, and executing cyber attacks to gain strategic advantage. 
Furthermore, military organizations implement Cyber Defense measures to protect against insider threats, including unauthorized data disclosure and malicious activity by authorized personnel. This involves implementing strict access controls, monitoring user activity, and conducting regular security audits to identify and mitigate potential risks. 
The application of Cyber Defense in military operations requires a deep understanding of cyber threats, vulnerabilities, and mitigation strategies, as well as the ability to integrate cyber defense with other military capabilities to achieve strategic objectives.

## Common Errors

In the realm of cyber defense within military operations, several common errors are consistently observed, stemming from misunderstandings of cyber warfare principles, inadequate training, and the evolving nature of cyber threats. One prevalent mistake is the underestimation of insider threats, where authorized personnel, intentionally or unintentionally, compromise security. This error arises from a focus primarily on external threats, neglecting the potential damage an insider can inflict. Another critical error is the failure to implement a layered defense strategy, relying too heavily on a single security measure, such as firewalls or antivirus software. This oversight leaves networks vulnerable to sophisticated attacks that can bypass or disable these defenses. Furthermore, the lack of continuous monitoring and incident response planning hinders the ability to detect and respond to breaches effectively, allowing attackers to remain undetected within the network for extended periods. Additionally, neglecting to keep software and systems updated with the latest security patches exposes military networks to known vulnerabilities that can be easily exploited by adversaries. These errors underscore the importance of comprehensive cyber defense strategies that include thorough risk assessments, robust security protocols, continuous training, and vigilant monitoring to protect against the dynamic and ever-present threats in the cyber domain.

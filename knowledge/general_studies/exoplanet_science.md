# Exoplanet Science

> The course teaches core principles and definitions of exoplanet science, assuming no prior study but providing foundational knowledge beyond introductory level.

## Discovery

First confirmed exoplanet around Sun-like star: 51 Pegasi b (Mayor & Queloz, 1995 — Nobel Prize 2019). Hot Jupiter, 4.2-day orbit. Now 5,500+ confirmed exoplanets. DETECTION METHODS: Radial velocity (Doppler wobble — stellar spectrum shifts as planet's gravity tugs star), Transit photometry (planet crosses star → brightness dip, Kepler Space Telescope found 2600+ planets), Direct imaging (blocking starlight — challenging, best for large distant planets), Gravitational microlensing, Astrometry. KEPLER MISSION (2009-2018): Stared at 150K stars for 4 years. Revolutionized field. Found Earth-sized planets are COMMON. TYPES: Hot Jupiters (gas giants close to star), Super-Earths (1.2-2× Earth radius), Mini-Neptunes, Water worlds, Rogue planets (no star). HABITABLE ZONE: "Goldilocks zone" — distance where liquid water possible. Depends on star type, atmospheric composition, albedo. TRAPPIST-1: 7 Earth-sized planets, 3 in habitable zone, only 40 light-years away. BIOSIGNATURES: Atmospheric markers that could indicate life — oxygen + methane (thermodynamic disequilibrium), phosphine (Venus controversy), water vapor, ozone. JWST can analyze exoplanet atmospheres via transmission spectroscopy. STATISTICS: Conservative estimates: 300M+ potentially habitable planets in Milky Way alone. Drake Equation and Fermi Paradox — "Where is everybody?"

## Foundations

In the physical sciences, exoplanet science is the study of planets that orbit stars outside of our own solar system. A **planet** is defined as a celestial body that is in orbit around a star, has sufficient mass to assume a hydrostatic equilibrium shape, has cleared the neighborhood around its orbit, and is not a satellite of another planet. **Hydrostatic equilibrium** refers to the state in which the inward pull of gravity is balanced by the outward pressure of the planet's interior. **Exoplanets** are planets that orbit **main-sequence stars**, which are stars that are fusing hydrogen into helium in their cores. The **habitable zone** of a star is the region around the star where temperatures are suitable for liquid water to exist on a planet's surface, which is a key factor in determining a planet's potential for supporting life. **Transit** refers to the phenomenon where a planet passes in front of its host star, blocking a small amount of light and allowing scientists to measure the planet's size and orbit. **Radial velocity** is the speed at which a star moves towards or away from an observer, which can be used to detect the presence of an exoplanet by measuring the star's wobble caused by the planet's gravitational pull. Understanding these core definitions and principles is essential for practitioners in the field of exoplanet science.

Understanding these core definitions and principles is essential for a practitioner in exoplanet science.

## Mechanisms

The detection and characterization of exoplanets rely on several mechanisms that allow scientists to infer the presence of planets around other stars. The transit method involves a planet passing in front of its host star, causing a decrease in the star's brightness. This decrease is directly proportional to the ratio of the planet's area to the star's area. As the planet transits, it blocks a small amount of the star's light, resulting in a mini-eclipse. By measuring the duration and frequency of these mini-eclipses, astronomers can determine the planet's orbital period and size. The radial velocity method, on the other hand, involves measuring the star's subtle wobble caused by the gravitational pull of an orbiting planet. As the planet moves around the star, it causes the star to move slightly back and forth, resulting in a shift of the star's spectral lines. By measuring this shift, scientists can infer the presence of a planet and determine its mass. The combination of these mechanisms provides a comprehensive understanding of exoplanet properties, including size, mass, and orbital characteristics. Additionally, the gravitational lensing method and direct imaging also contribute to the detection and characterization of exoplanets, offering insights into their atmospheres and potential habitability.

## Methods And Frameworks

The Transit Method involves measuring the decrease in brightness of a star as a planet passes in front of it, used for detecting exoplanets with sizes similar to or larger than Jupiter. The Radial Velocity Method measures the star's wobble caused by the gravitational pull of an orbiting planet, effective for detecting massive exoplanets close to their stars. The Gravitational Lensing Method observes the bending of light around a star caused by an exoplanet's gravity, useful for detecting exoplanets at large distances from their stars. 
The Keplerian Model describes the orbital motion of exoplanets, using formulas such as Newton's Law of Universal Gravitation and Kepler's Laws of Planetary Motion. The Transit Duration Formula (T = (R_star + R_planet) / v) calculates the duration of a transit event, where T is the transit duration, R_star and R_planet are the radii of the star and planet, and v is the planet's velocity. 
Failure modes include the False Positive Problem in the Transit Method, where a decrease in brightness is caused by a binary star system or other astrophysical phenomena, and the Degeneracy Problem in the Radial Velocity Method, where multiple orbital solutions are possible for a given set of observations.

## Worked Examples

To illustrate the principles of exoplanet science, consider the following examples. 
1. Calculating the orbital period of an exoplanet: Given a star with mass 1.2 M⊙ and an exoplanet orbiting at a distance of 0.8 AU, we can use Kepler's third law to calculate the orbital period. The law states that P² = (4π²/G \* (a³ / M)), where P is the orbital period, G is the gravitational constant (6.674 * 10⁻¹¹ N m² kg⁻²), a is the semi-major axis, and M is the mass of the star. Plugging in the values, we get P² = (4π² / (6.674 * 10⁻¹¹)) \* ((0.8 * 1.496 * 10¹¹)³ / (1.2 * 1.989 * 10³⁰)), which simplifies to P ≈ 280 days.
2. Determining the radius of an exoplanet: If we know the mass of an exoplanet (e.g., 2.5 M⊕) and its surface gravity (e.g., 20 m/s²), we can calculate its radius using the formula g = G \* M / r², where g is the surface gravity, G is the gravitational constant, M is the mass, and r is the radius. Rearranging the formula to solve for r, we get r = √(G \* M / g) = √((6.674 * 10⁻¹¹) \* (2.5 * 5.972 * 10²⁴) / 20) ≈ 1.3 R⊕.
3. Estimating the temperature of an exoplanet: For an exoplanet orbiting a star with luminosity 2.5 L⊙ at a distance of 1.2 AU, we can estimate its equilibrium temperature using the formula T = (√(L / (16 \* π \* σ \* a²))) \* (1 - α)¹/4, where T is the temperature, L is the luminosity, σ is the Stefan-Boltzmann constant (5.67 * 10⁻⁸ W m⁻² K⁻⁴), a is the distance, and α is the albedo (assumed to be 0.3 for a rocky planet). Plugging in the values, we get T ≈ (√(2.5 * 3.846 * 10²⁶ / (16 \* π \* 5.67 * 10⁻⁸ \* (1.2 * 1.496 * 10¹¹)²))) \* (1 - 0.3)¹/4 ≈ 250 K.

## Applications

Exoplanet science has numerous applications in the physical sciences, particularly in the fields of astrophysics and planetary science. One of the primary applications is the search for life beyond Earth, where the discovery of exoplanets with conditions similar to those of our own planet can provide insights into the possibility of life existing elsewhere in the universe. The study of exoplanet atmospheres, for example, can reveal the presence of biosignatures, such as oxygen, methane, or other gases that could be produced by living organisms. 
The characterization of exoplanet properties, such as mass, radius, and orbital parameters, is also crucial for understanding planetary formation and evolution processes. By analyzing the diversity of exoplanetary systems, scientists can gain insights into the mechanisms that shape the architecture of our own solar system and the potential for life to arise on other planets. 
Furthermore, the development of new observational and analytical techniques for exoplanet detection and characterization has driven innovation in fields such as optics, spectroscopy, and data analysis, with applications extending beyond exoplanet science to other areas of astronomy and physics. 
The study of exoplanet science also informs the development of future space missions, such as the James Webb Space Telescope and the Terrestrial Planet Finder, which are designed to study the atmospheres and surfaces of exoplanets in unprecedented detail, and to search for signs of life beyond our solar system.

## Common Errors

In exoplanet science, practitioners often make mistakes in data analysis and interpretation. One common error is assuming that the transit method can detect exoplanets regardless of their orbital inclination. However, the transit method relies on the exoplanet passing in front of its host star, which only occurs if the orbital plane is aligned with the observer's line of sight. Failure to account for this limitation can lead to incorrect estimates of exoplanet frequencies and properties. Another mistake is neglecting the effects of stellar variability on radial velocity measurements, which can mimic the signatures of exoplanets and lead to false positives. Additionally, practitioners may incorrectly assume that the mass of an exoplanet can be directly determined from its radius, ignoring the complexities of planetary composition and internal structure. These errors can be avoided by carefully considering the underlying physics and limitations of each detection method, as well as by using robust statistical analysis and model selection techniques to interpret the data. By recognizing and addressing these common errors, researchers can improve the accuracy and reliability of exoplanet science results.

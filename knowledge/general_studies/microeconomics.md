# Microeconomics

> The course assumes no prior study but teaches core principles and fundamental concepts of microeconomics.

## Foundations

Microeconomics is the branch of economics that analyzes the behavior of individual economic agents—consumers, firms, and governments—and their interactions within markets. It rests on the core principle of rational choice under scarcity, where agents maximize utility (consumers) or profit (firms) subject to constraints. The fundamental problem is allocation of limited resources to optimize outcomes. The analytical framework employs marginal analysis, equilibrium concepts, and comparative statics to derive demand and supply, price formation, and welfare implications. Central to microeconomics is the assumption of methodological individualism and the use of partial equilibrium analysis before extending to general equilibrium.

Microeconomics is the study of economic units, such as households, firms, and markets, analyzing their behavior and interactions. A **market** is a structure that allows buyers and sellers to exchange goods and services, with **goods** being tangible items and **services** being intangible actions. The **price** of a good or service is the amount of money required to acquire it. **Scarcity** refers to the fundamental problem of economics, where the needs and wants of individuals are unlimited, but the resources available to satisfy them are limited. **Opportunity cost** is the value of the next best alternative that is given up when a choice is made, illustrating the trade-offs inherent in economic decision-making. **Rational choice** assumes that economic agents, such as **consumers** (households) and **producers** (firms), make decisions that maximize their **utility** (satisfaction) or **profit**, given the resources and information available. **Supply and demand** are the two fundamental forces in a market, with **supply** referring to the amount of a good or service that producers are willing and able to produce and sell at a given price, and **demand** referring to the amount that consumers are willing and able to buy at a given price. Understanding these core definitions and principles is essential for analyzing economic phenomena and making informed decisions in microeconomics.

## Demand Theory And Consumer Choice

The cornerstone is the utility maximization problem:  
\[
\max_{x} U(x_1, x_2, ..., x_n) \quad \text{s.t.} \quad \sum_{i=1}^n p_i x_i \leq M
\]  
where \(U\) is a well-behaved utility function (continuous, monotone, quasi-concave), \(p_i\) prices, and \(M\) income. The solution yields the Marshallian demand functions \(x_i^*(p,M)\). The Slutsky equation decomposes the total effect of a price change into substitution and income effects:  
\[
\frac{\partial x_i}{\partial p_j} = \frac{\partial h_i}{\partial p_j} - x_j \frac{\partial x_i}{\partial M}
\]  
where \(h_i\) is the Hicksian compensated demand. Key tools include Lagrangian optimization and revealed preference theory. Empirically, price elasticities of demand (e.g., own-price elasticity \(\varepsilon_{ii} = \frac{\partial x_i}{\partial p_i} \frac{p_i}{x_i}\)) quantify responsiveness.

## Production And Cost Theory

Firms maximize profit \(\pi = p \cdot q - C(q)\), where \(q = f(L,K)\) is output from inputs labor \(L\) and capital \(K\). The production function is characterized by returns to scale and marginal products:  
\[
MP_L = \frac{\partial f}{\partial L}, \quad MP_K = \frac{\partial f}{\partial K}
\]  
Cost minimization solves:  
\[
\min_{L,K} wL + rK \quad \text{s.t.} \quad f(L,K) = q
\]  
yielding conditional factor demands and the cost function \(C(w,r,q)\). The envelope theorem links cost and input prices:  
\[
\frac{\partial C}{\partial w} = L^*(w,r,q), \quad \frac{\partial C}{\partial r} = K^*(w,r,q)
\]  
Short-run vs. long-run cost curves, economies of scale, and isoquants (convex, smooth) are central. The Cobb-Douglas \(f(L,K) = A L^\alpha K^\beta\) is a canonical form with constant returns if \(\alpha + \beta = 1\).

## Market Structure And Equilibrium

Market types range from perfect competition to monopoly, oligopoly, and monopolistic competition. In perfect competition, firms are price takers, equilibrium price \(p^*\) satisfies:  
\[
D(p^*) = S(p^*)
\]  
with zero economic profits in long run: \(p^* = MC = AC_{min}\). Monopoly maximizes profit where marginal revenue \(MR\) equals marginal cost \(MC\), with \(MR = p(1 + \frac{1}{\varepsilon})\), \(\varepsilon\) the price elasticity of demand. Oligopoly models include Cournot (quantity competition), Bertrand (price competition), and Stackelberg (leader-follower). Nash equilibrium concepts formalize strategic interactions.

## General Equilibrium And Welfare

Arrow-Debreu framework models an economy with consumers and firms trading commodities under complete markets. An equilibrium is a price vector \(p^*\) where all markets clear:  
\[
\sum_i x_i(p^*) = \sum_j y_j(p^*)
\]  
where \(x_i\) are consumer demands and \(y_j\) firm supplies. The First Welfare Theorem states that under perfect competition and convex preferences, equilibria are Pareto efficient. The Second Welfare Theorem guarantees that any Pareto efficient allocation can be decentralized as a competitive equilibrium with transfers. Edgeworth box diagrams illustrate exchange efficiency; contract curves represent Pareto optima.

## Information Economics And Market Failure

Asymmetric information leads to adverse selection and moral hazard. The Rothschild-Stiglitz model of insurance markets demonstrates equilibrium existence and pooling/separating equilibria. Principal-agent problems employ incentive compatibility (IC) and participation constraints (IR):  
\[
\max_{a} U(a) \quad \text{s.t.} \quad IC, IR
\]  
Mechanism design and screening models use revelation principles to characterize implementable allocations. Market failures arise from externalities, public goods, and incomplete markets, necessitating government intervention or Coasean bargaining.

## Game Theory And Strategic Behavior

Non-cooperative game theory analyzes strategic decision-making among rational agents. Key solution concepts include Nash equilibrium, subgame perfect equilibrium, and Bayesian equilibrium. The Prisoner’s Dilemma exemplifies cooperation failure; repeated games enable trigger strategies sustaining cooperation. Extensive form games model sequential moves; mixed strategies allow equilibrium in games without pure strategy equilibria. Applications span auctions (e.g., Vickrey auctions), bargaining, and oligopoly competition.

## Labor Economics And Human Capital

Labor supply derives from utility maximization balancing consumption and leisure:  
\[
\max_{L} U(C, T - L) \quad \text{s.t.} \quad C = wL + V
\]  
where \(T\) is total time, \(w\) wage, \(V\) non-labor income. The labor-leisure tradeoff yields backward-bending labor supply curves at high wages. Human capital theory models investment in education as a present value maximization problem:  
\[
\max_{e} \int_0^T \frac{w(e,t)}{(1+r)^t} dt - C(e)
\]  
where \(e\) is education level, \(r\) discount rate, \(C(e)\) cost. Wage differentials, discrimination, and labor market frictions are analyzed within this framework.

## Mechanisms

In microeconomics, the market mechanism is a process by which the supply and demand for a product or service interact to determine its price and quantity. The causal chain begins with the production decisions of firms, which are based on their expectations of future demand and costs of production. As firms produce goods and services, they supply them to the market, creating a supply curve that slopes upward, indicating that as price increases, quantity supplied also increases. On the other hand, consumers' preferences and budget constraints determine their demand for goods and services, creating a demand curve that slopes downward, indicating that as price increases, quantity demanded decreases. The intersection of the supply and demand curves determines the market equilibrium price and quantity. Changes in market conditions, such as shifts in consumer preferences or changes in production costs, cause the supply and demand curves to shift, leading to a new market equilibrium. The price mechanism plays a crucial role in allocating resources efficiently, as it directs firms to produce goods and services that are in high demand and directs consumers to purchase goods and services that are available at a price they are willing to pay. The market mechanism also responds to external shocks, such as changes in government policies or technological advancements, which can affect the supply and demand curves and lead to changes in the market equilibrium.

In microeconomics, the mechanisms refer to the step-by-step processes that drive economic decisions and outcomes. The price mechanism is a key component, where prices adjust to equilibrate the quantity of a good or service that consumers are willing to buy (demand) with the quantity that producers are willing to supply (supply). This process can be broken down into several steps: 
1. Consumers form expectations about future prices and incomes, influencing their demand for a good or service. 
2. Producers observe current prices and factor costs, influencing their supply decisions. 
3. As consumers and producers interact in the market, they reveal their preferences and production capabilities through their buying and selling behavior. 
4. The intersection of the demand and supply curves determines the market equilibrium price and quantity. 
5. If the market is not in equilibrium, a surplus or shortage arises, prompting producers and consumers to adjust their behavior, leading to changes in prices and quantities until equilibrium is reached. 
The causal chain is as follows: consumer preferences and producer costs influence demand and supply, which in turn determine market prices and quantities, and ultimately, resource allocation in the economy. This mechanism is the foundation of microeconomic analysis, allowing economists to study how markets function and how they respond to changes in underlying conditions.

## Methods And Frameworks

In microeconomics, several methods and frameworks are employed to analyze market behavior and make predictions. The Supply and Demand Model is used to determine market equilibrium, where the quantity of a good or service that suppliers are willing to sell equals the quantity that buyers are willing to buy. The Cobb-Douglas Production Function is utilized to model the relationship between inputs and outputs in production, with the formula Q = AL^αK^β, where Q is output, A is total factor productivity, L is labor, K is capital, and α and β are output elasticities. The Monopolistic Competition Model is applied to industries with many firms producing differentiated products, where firms have some degree of price-setting power. The concept of Elasticity, measured by the formula Ed = (dQ/Q) / (dP/P), is used to analyze how responsive the quantity demanded of a good is to changes in its price. The Failure Mode of these models often arises from assumptions of perfect information, rational behavior, and static market conditions, which may not hold in reality. Additionally, the models may not account for externalities, such as environmental impacts or social costs, which can lead to market failures.

## Worked Examples

To illustrate key microeconomic concepts, consider the following examples. 
1. Demand and Supply: A company supplies widgets at $10 each. The demand function is Q = 100 - 2P, where Q is quantity and P is price. If the company increases the price to $12, what is the new quantity demanded? Substituting P = 12 into the demand function yields Q = 100 - 2*12 = 100 - 24 = 76. 
2. Consumer Behavior: A consumer has a budget of $100 to spend on two goods, X and Y, with prices $5 and $10 respectively. If the consumer's utility function is U = X*Y, and they buy 10 units of X, how many units of Y can they afford? With 10 units of X costing $50, $50 remains for Y, allowing the purchase of 5 units of Y. 
3. Cost Minimization: A firm produces output using labor (L) and capital (K) with a cost function C = 2L + 5K. If the firm wants to produce 48 units of output, and the production function is Q = L*K, what is the least-cost combination of L and K? Setting Q = 48, we get L*K = 48. Possible combinations include (L, K) = (1, 48), (2, 24), (3, 16), (4, 12), or (6, 8). Evaluating the cost function for each, we find C(1, 48) = 2*1 + 5*48 = 242, C(2, 24) = 2*2 + 5*24 = 122, C(3, 16) = 2*3 + 5*16 = 86, C(4, 12) = 2*4 + 5*12 = 72, and C(6, 8) = 2*6 + 5*8 = 52. The least-cost combination is (6, 8).

To illustrate key microeconomic concepts, consider the following examples. 
1. Demand and Supply: A bakery sells 200 loaves of bread at $2 each. If the price increases to $2.50, the quantity demanded decreases to 180 loaves, while the quantity supplied increases to 220 loaves. Using the midpoint method, calculate the price elasticity of demand. 
Price elasticity of demand = ((180-200)/((180+200)/2)) / ((2.50-2)/((2.50+2)/2)) = (-20/190) / (0.50/2.25) = -0.105 / 0.222 = -0.473. 
This indicates that a 1% increase in price leads to a 0.473% decrease in quantity demanded, which is inelastic. 
2. Opportunity Cost: A farmer can produce either 100 units of wheat or 50 units of barley on a plot of land. If the market price of wheat is $10 and barley is $5, calculate the opportunity cost of producing wheat. 
Opportunity cost = (50 units of barley * $5/unit) = $250. This is the revenue foregone by choosing to produce wheat instead of barley. 
3. Consumer Surplus: A consumer is willing to pay $15 for a concert ticket, but the market price is $10. Calculate the consumer surplus. 
Consumer surplus = Willingness to pay - Market price = $15 - $10 = $5. This represents the benefit the consumer gains from purchasing the ticket at the market price rather than their willingness to pay.

## Applications

Microeconomics has numerous practical applications in economics and finance, particularly in understanding consumer and firm behavior. One key application is in the analysis of market structures, such as perfect competition, monopoly, and oligopoly, to determine the optimal price and output levels for firms. This is crucial for businesses to maximize profits and for policymakers to regulate industries. Additionally, microeconomics is used in the evaluation of government policies, such as taxation and subsidies, to assess their impact on market outcomes and welfare. The concept of opportunity cost is also applied in investment decisions, where individuals and firms weigh the costs and benefits of alternative investments. Furthermore, microeconomics informs the development of pricing strategies, such as price discrimination and bundling, which firms use to increase revenue. The understanding of consumer behavior, through the use of indifference curves and budget constraints, allows firms to target specific consumer groups and tailor their products accordingly. Overall, microeconomics provides a framework for analyzing and predicting the behavior of economic agents, enabling more informed decision-making in both the public and private sectors.

Microeconomics has numerous applications in practice, particularly in business decision-making and policy analysis. Firms use microeconomic principles to determine optimal production levels, set prices, and allocate resources efficiently. For instance, a company may apply the concept of marginal analysis to decide whether to increase production, by weighing the marginal revenue against the marginal cost. Additionally, microeconomic theories such as game theory and oligopoly models help firms understand competitive market structures and make informed strategic decisions. In policy analysis, microeconomics is used to evaluate the impact of government interventions, such as taxes and subsidies, on market outcomes and consumer behavior. Furthermore, microeconomic models are employed to analyze the effects of externalities, such as pollution, and to design policies that internalize these externalities, like carbon pricing. The concept of consumer behavior and demand analysis is also crucial in marketing and advertising, as firms seek to understand consumer preferences and tailor their products and services accordingly. Overall, microeconomic principles provide a framework for analyzing and predicting the behavior of individual economic units, allowing for more informed decision-making in both business and policy contexts.

## Common Errors

In microeconomics, several common errors can lead to incorrect conclusions and decisions. One of the primary mistakes is the confusion between absolute and comparative advantage. Practitioners often assume that a country with an absolute advantage in producing a good should specialize in its production, ignoring the principle of comparative advantage, which states that countries should specialize in producing goods for which they have a lower opportunity cost. Another error is the failure to account for externalities, such as environmental degradation or social costs, when evaluating the efficiency of a market. This can lead to incorrect assessments of social welfare and misguided policy interventions. Additionally, the assumption of perfect information is often violated, as consumers and firms may have incomplete or asymmetric information, leading to market failures. The neglect of sunk costs is also a common error, as practitioners may incorrectly assume that past investments should influence current decisions, when in fact, sunk costs are irrelevant to future choices. Furthermore, the mistake of equating equilibrium with optimality is prevalent, as equilibrium does not necessarily imply that the market outcome is socially optimal. These errors can have significant consequences, such as inefficient resource allocation, incorrect policy prescriptions, and poor decision-making.

Practitioners in microeconomics often make mistakes that can lead to incorrect conclusions and decisions. One common error is the confusion between sunk costs and relevant costs. Sunk costs are expenses that have already been incurred and cannot be changed, whereas relevant costs are expenses that will be incurred in the future. Many practitioners incorrectly consider sunk costs when making decisions, which can lead to the sunk cost fallacy. This fallacy occurs when a decision is based on an investment that has already been made, rather than on the expected future returns. For example, if a company has invested $1 million in a project that is no longer viable, it should not continue to invest in the project simply because of the initial investment. Another common error is the failure to consider opportunity costs, which are the benefits that could have been obtained if a different choice had been made. Practitioners may also incorrectly apply the concept of diminishing marginal returns, which states that as the quantity of a variable input increases, the marginal product of that input will eventually decrease. This concept is often misapplied by assuming that it applies to all production processes, when in fact it only applies to production processes with fixed inputs. Additionally, practitioners may make errors when analyzing market structures, such as perfect competition, monopoly, and oligopoly, by incorrectly assuming that firms in these markets have the same goals and behaviors. These errors can lead to incorrect predictions of market outcomes and incorrect policy recommendations.

## Advanced

In graduate-level microeconomics, several advanced topics extend the foundational principles. One key area is general equilibrium theory, which analyzes the behavior of multiple markets in an economy and how they interact to reach a state of equilibrium. Another area is mechanism design, which involves the creation of economic mechanisms and institutions to achieve specific outcomes, such as auctions and voting systems. The field also explores advanced game theory, including dynamic games and games with incomplete information, to better understand strategic decision-making. Additionally, microeconomics incorporates behavioral economics, which examines how psychological and social factors influence economic decisions, often leading to deviations from rational choice theory. Open questions in the field include the development of more realistic models of consumer and firm behavior, the integration of microeconomics with macroeconomics, and the application of microeconomic principles to emerging issues such as climate change and technological innovation. The field is moving towards increased use of experimental and empirical methods, including laboratory experiments and field studies, to test and refine microeconomic theories. Furthermore, advances in computational power and data analysis are enabling researchers to develop more complex and nuanced models of economic behavior, incorporating factors such as network effects and social interactions.

At the graduate level, microeconomics extends into advanced topics such as general equilibrium theory, which analyzes the behavior of multiple markets in an economy and their interactions. Another key area is game theory, which studies strategic decision-making in situations where the outcome depends on the actions of multiple individuals or firms. Mechanism design, a field that applies game theory to the design of economic institutions and systems, is also a crucial aspect of advanced microeconomics. Additionally, graduate-level microeconomics delves into the economics of information, which examines how individuals and firms make decisions under uncertainty and asymmetric information. Open questions in the field include the development of more sophisticated models of consumer behavior, the integration of behavioral economics into traditional microeconomic theory, and the application of microeconomic principles to emerging issues such as climate change and technological innovation. The field is moving towards increased use of experimental and empirical methods to test microeconomic theories and inform policy decisions. Furthermore, the incorporation of computational methods and big data analysis is becoming more prevalent, allowing for more nuanced and detailed analysis of microeconomic phenomena.

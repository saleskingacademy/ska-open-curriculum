# English Composition

> The course covers core concepts, key terminology, and vocabulary of English Composition, assuming no prior study.

## Foundations

English Composition refers to the study and practice of writing effective and coherent texts in the English language. A **text** is a unit of written language, such as an essay, article, or story, that conveys meaning to a reader. **Composition** itself is the process of creating a text, involving the combination of words, phrases, and sentences to convey ideas, express thoughts, and engage audiences. 
Key terms include **rhetoric**, the art of using language effectively to persuade or inform, and **discourse**, a stretch of language that conveys meaning in a particular context. **Syntax** refers to the rules governing the structure of sentences, while **semantics** is the study of meaning in language. 
A practitioner of English Composition must understand **genre**, which refers to a category of texts sharing similar characteristics, such as fiction, nonfiction, or poetry, and **tone**, the attitude or feeling conveyed by a text. **Style** encompasses the unique way a writer uses language to express themselves, including **diction** (word choice), **imagery** (vivid descriptions), and **figurative language** (literary devices like metaphors and similes). 
Understanding these core concepts is essential for effective communication in written English.

## Mechanisms

In English Composition, the writing process involves a series of interconnected mechanisms that facilitate the creation of coherent and effective texts. The process begins with invention, where writers generate ideas through techniques such as brainstorming, freewriting, and research. This stage is crucial as it lays the foundation for the entire composition. Next, writers engage in arrangement, where they organize their ideas into a logical structure, often using outlines or diagrams to visualize the relationships between ideas. The drafting mechanism follows, where writers translate their ideas into written text, focusing on content and organization rather than grammar or syntax. Revision is a critical mechanism, where writers review and refine their draft, making significant changes to content, structure, and tone as needed. Editing and proofreading are subsequent mechanisms, where writers attend to sentence-level concerns, such as grammar, punctuation, and spelling, to ensure clarity and precision. Finally, the publication mechanism involves sharing the written text with an audience, which can take various forms, including print, digital, or oral presentation. Throughout these mechanisms, writers engage in a recursive process, moving back and forth between stages as they refine and improve their composition. Effective English composition requires a deep understanding of these mechanisms and the ability to navigate them in a flexible and adaptive manner. This stage is crucial as it allows students to explore their thoughts and establish a clear purpose for their writing. Next, students engage in drafting, where they translate their ideas into written text, focusing on content and organization rather than grammar and syntax. The drafting stage is followed by revising, which involves evaluating the content, structure, and overall flow of the text, making significant changes as needed to improve clarity and coherence. Editing then occurs, where students refine their text by checking for grammatical errors, punctuation, and spelling mistakes. Finally, proofreading takes place, where students review their text carefully to detect any remaining errors before finalizing their work. Throughout these stages, students may also engage in peer review, receiving feedback from others to inform their revisions and edits. The causal chain is thus: idea generation influences drafting, which in turn affects revising, editing, and proofreading, ultimately resulting in a polished and effective composition. Understanding these mechanisms and their sequence is essential for students to develop their writing skills effectively.

## Methods And Frameworks

In English Composition, several methods and frameworks guide the writing process. The Writing Process Approach, developed by Donald Murray, emphasizes recursive stages of prewriting, writing, and rewriting. Use this approach when teaching novice writers to break down the writing process into manageable tasks. Failure mode: overemphasis on linearity, neglecting the recursive nature of writing. 
The Genre-Based Approach focuses on teaching specific text types, such as narrative or argumentative essays. Apply this method when teaching students to write for specific purposes or audiences. Failure mode: oversimplification of genre conventions, neglecting the complexity of real-world writing contexts. 
The Six Traits Writing Model, developed by Vicki Spandel, assesses writing based on content, organization, voice, sentence fluency, word choice, and conventions. Use this model when evaluating student writing to provide targeted feedback. Failure mode: overreliance on trait-based assessment, neglecting the holistic quality of writing. 
The Toulmin Model of Argumentation, developed by Stephen Toulmin, analyzes arguments based on claims, data, warrants, and backing. Apply this model when teaching students to construct and evaluate arguments. Failure mode: oversimplification of complex arguments, neglecting the nuances of real-world persuasion. 
The MLA and APA formatting guidelines provide standardized frameworks for citing sources and formatting papers. Use these guidelines when teaching students to conduct research and cite sources accurately. Failure mode: overemphasis on formatting, neglecting the importance of content and critical thinking. The Writing Process Approach, which involves prewriting, drafting, revising, editing, and publishing, is effective for generating and refining ideas. The Six Traits Model, comprising ideas, organization, voice, word choice, sentence fluency, and conventions, helps evaluate and improve writing quality. The Toulmin Model, which analyzes arguments in terms of claims, data, warrants, backing, and qualifiers, is useful for constructing persuasive texts. The SOAPSTone method, examining subject, occasion, audience, purpose, speaker, and tone, facilitates analysis of rhetorical situations. Each method has its failure mode: overreliance on the Writing Process Approach can lead to formulaic writing, while the Six Traits Model may overlook contextual considerations. The Toulmin Model can be too rigid for nuanced arguments, and the SOAPSTone method may not account for complexities in rhetorical situations. Understanding these methods and frameworks, including their applications and limitations, enables effective teaching and practice of English Composition. By recognizing when to apply each method and being aware of potential pitfalls, writers and instructors can adapt and combine approaches to suit specific writing tasks and contexts. The failure mode of these methods often lies in their rigid application, neglecting the flexibility and adaptability required in different writing contexts. For instance, overemphasizing the Writing Process Approach can lead to an overly formulaic writing style, while the Six Traits Model may overlook the importance of context and purpose in evaluating writing. The Toulmin Model can be limited by its focus on argumentation, which may not be applicable to all types of writing. Other notable frameworks include the Rhetorical Situation, which considers the writer, audience, purpose, and context, and the Genre Approach, which emphasizes the social and cultural conventions of different text types. Understanding these methods and frameworks, as well as their limitations, enables writers to approach composition in a more informed and effective manner.

## Worked Examples

To illustrate the principles of English composition, consider the following examples. 
1. Analyzing sentence structure: Identify the independent and dependent clauses in the sentence "While I was studying, my phone rang." The independent clause is "my phone rang" because it has a subject (phone) and a verb (rang), and it expresses a complete thought. The dependent clause is "While I was studying" because it has a subject (I) and a verb (was studying), but it does not express a complete thought.
2. Improving paragraph coherence: Revise the paragraph "The city is big. It has many restaurants. The food is delicious" to improve coherence. A revised version could be "The city is big and has many restaurants, which serve delicious food." This revision uses transitional words and phrases to connect the ideas between sentences, creating a more cohesive paragraph.
3. Applying tone and audience awareness: Write a paragraph about a new park in the city, targeting a specific audience (parents with young children) and tone (informative and enthusiastic). Example: "The new park in our city is a fantastic place for families to spend time together. With its playground, picnic areas, and walking trails, there's something for everyone to enjoy. The park also offers a safe and clean environment, making it an ideal spot for young children to play and learn." This paragraph uses a tone and language suitable for the target audience, making it an effective example of English composition. Improving paragraph coherence: Revise the paragraph "The city is big. It is noisy" to improve coherence. A revised version could be "The city is big, with many people contributing to its noisy atmosphere." This revision uses transitional words and phrases to connect ideas and create a clearer narrative.
3. Evaluating tone in a passage: Determine the tone of the passage "The old car creaked and groaned as it made its way up the hill, its rusty body a testament to years of neglect." The tone is nostalgic and slightly melancholic, as evidenced by the use of words like "creaked" and "groaned" to describe the car's movement, and the phrase "a testament to years of neglect" to convey a sense of age and abandonment.

## Common Errors

In English composition, common errors often arise from misunderstandings of grammatical rules, punctuation, and syntax. One prevalent mistake is the incorrect use of commas, particularly with independent and dependent clauses. Many writers fail to use a comma before a conjunction (such as and, but, or) when joining two independent clauses, leading to comma splices or run-on sentences. For instance, "I went to the store and I bought milk" should be "I went to the store, and I bought milk." Another error is the misuse of apostrophes, often confusing possessive and plural forms. The principle to remember is that an apostrophe is used to indicate possession (e.g., "the cat's toy") or to form contractions (e.g., "it's" for "it is"), not to pluralize nouns. Additionally, subject-verb agreement is a frequent issue, where the verb tense or number does not match the subject. This can lead to sentences like "The team are going to the championship," which should be "The team is going to the championship" because "team" is a singular noun. These errors can significantly affect the clarity and coherence of written communication, emphasizing the importance of careful editing and attention to grammatical rules in English composition.

# Information Theory

> The course assumes prior knowledge of probability theory, statistics, and signal processing.

## Foundations

Information theory, pioneered by Claude E. Shannon in 1948, is the mathematical study of quantifying, encoding, transmitting, and decoding information. At its core, it formalizes the concept of information as a measurable entity, abstracted from semantic content, focusing on the statistical properties of messages generated by stochastic sources. The fundamental unit of information is the bit, defined via the binary logarithm of event probabilities. The theory rests on first principles: entropy as a measure of uncertainty, mutual information as shared information between variables, and channel capacity as the maximal reliable communication rate over noisy channels. It bridges probability theory, statistics, and signal processing to optimize data compression and error correction.

Information theory, a branch of mathematics, is concerned with quantifying and analyzing information. A **message** is a sequence of symbols from a finite **alphabet**, which is a set of distinct symbols. An **event** is an occurrence of a particular message or symbol. The **probability** of an event is a real number between 0 and 1, inclusive, representing the likelihood of the event occurring. A **random variable** is a function that assigns a numerical value to each possible outcome of a random experiment.

The **entropy** of a random variable, denoted by H, is a measure of the uncertainty or randomness of the variable. It is defined as the average amount of information produced by the variable. Entropy is typically measured in **bits**, which are units of information. A **bit** is the amount of information required to distinguish between two equally likely outcomes.

A **discrete memoryless source** is a mathematical model of a source that generates a sequence of independent and identically distributed random variables. The **source alphabet** is the set of possible symbols that can be generated by the source. The **probability distribution** of a discrete random variable is a function that assigns a probability to each possible value of the variable.

The **Kolmogorov axioms** are a set of fundamental principles that define the properties of probability measures. These axioms ensure that probability measures are consistent and well-behaved. The **sample space** is the set of all possible outcomes of a random experiment. An **information source** is a system that generates a sequence of messages, which can be modeled using probability theory.

## Entropy And Information Measures

Shannon entropy \( H(X) = -\sum_{x \in \mathcal{X}} p(x) \log_2 p(x) \) quantifies the expected uncertainty of a discrete random variable \(X\). For continuous variables, differential entropy \( h(X) = -\int p(x) \log p(x) dx \) applies, though it lacks some discrete entropy properties. Joint entropy \( H(X,Y) \), conditional entropy \( H(X|Y) \), and relative entropy or Kullback-Leibler divergence \( D_{KL}(P||Q) = \sum p(x) \log \frac{p(x)}{q(x)} \) extend the framework to multivariate and comparative contexts. Mutual information \( I(X;Y) = H(X) - H(X|Y) \) measures the reduction in uncertainty of \(X\) given \(Y\), fundamental for assessing channel efficiency and feature relevance in machine learning.

## Source Coding Theorem And Lossless Compression

Shannon’s source coding theorem states that the entropy \(H(X)\) is the lower bound on the average length of any lossless encoding of \(X\). Huffman coding constructs an optimal prefix code minimizing expected code length, achieving average length \(L\) satisfying \(H(X) \leq L < H(X) + 1\) bits/symbol. Arithmetic coding approaches entropy limits more closely by encoding entire sequences as intervals on the unit line, exploiting cumulative distribution functions. Universal source coding algorithms like Lempel-Ziv (LZ77, LZ78) asymptotically achieve entropy rates without prior knowledge of \(p(x)\).

## Channel Capacity And Noisy Channels

The noisy-channel coding theorem defines channel capacity \(C = \max_{p(x)} I(X;Y)\) as the supremum of achievable rates for reliable communication over a channel characterized by transition probabilities \(p(y|x)\). For the binary symmetric channel (BSC) with crossover probability \(p\), capacity is \(C = 1 - H_2(p)\), where \(H_2\) is the binary entropy function. The Additive White Gaussian Noise (AWGN) channel capacity is \(C = \frac{1}{2} \log_2(1 + \mathrm{SNR})\) bits/channel use, where SNR is signal-to-noise ratio. Coding schemes such as Reed-Solomon, Turbo codes, and LDPC codes approach capacity by adding structured redundancy to enable error correction.

## Rate-Distortion Theory

Rate-distortion theory extends information theory to lossy compression, balancing compression rate \(R\) against distortion \(D\). The rate-distortion function \(R(D) = \min_{p(\hat{x}|x): \mathbb{E}[d(X,\hat{X})] \leq D} I(X;\hat{X})\) defines the minimal rate for a given distortion level under distortion measure \(d\). For Gaussian sources with mean squared error distortion, \(R(D) = \frac{1}{2} \log_2 \frac{\sigma^2}{D}\). The Blahut-Arimoto algorithm numerically computes \(R(D)\) by iterative optimization of conditional distributions.

## Multivariate Information And Networks

Multivariate extensions include total correlation (multi-information) \(C(X_1,\ldots,X_n) = \sum_i H(X_i) - H(X_1,\ldots,X_n)\) quantifying redundancy among variables, and interaction information capturing synergistic and redundant information. Information bottleneck method optimizes representations \(T\) of \(X\) preserving relevant information about \(Y\) by minimizing \(I(X;T)\) subject to \(I(T;Y) \geq \text{const}\), formalized via Lagrangian \( \mathcal{L} = I(X;T) - \beta I(T;Y) \). Directed information and transfer entropy extend mutual information to temporal causal dependencies, critical in neuroscience and communication networks.

## Entropy Rate And Stochastic Processes

Entropy rate \(H' = \lim_{n \to \infty} \frac{1}{n} H(X_1, \ldots, X_n)\) measures average uncertainty per symbol in stationary stochastic processes. For Markov chains with transition matrix \(P\) and stationary distribution \(\pi\), \(H' = -\sum_{i,j} \pi_i P_{ij} \log_2 P_{ij}\). This quantifies predictability and compressibility of time series data. Extensions include Kolmogorov-Sinai entropy in dynamical systems, linking information theory and ergodic theory.

## Quantum Information Theory (Qit)

QIT generalizes classical concepts to quantum states and channels. The von Neumann entropy \(S(\rho) = -\mathrm{Tr}(\rho \log \rho)\) parallels Shannon entropy for density matrix \(\rho\). Quantum mutual information \(I(A;B) = S(\rho_A) + S(\rho_B) - S(\rho_{AB})\) measures correlations including entanglement. The Holevo bound limits classical information extractable from quantum ensembles. Quantum channel capacities (classical, quantum, private) are defined with respect to quantum noise models, with protocols such as superdense coding and quantum error correction codes (e.g., Shor code) enabling robust quantum communication.

## Mechanisms

Information theory, as a mathematical discipline, relies on several key mechanisms to quantify and analyze information. The process begins with the definition of a probability distribution over a set of possible outcomes or messages. This distribution assigns a probability to each outcome, which represents the likelihood of its occurrence. The next step involves calculating the entropy of the distribution, which is a measure of the uncertainty or randomness of the outcomes. Entropy is typically calculated using the Shannon entropy formula, which sums the product of each outcome's probability and the logarithm of that probability. The logarithm is usually taken to the base 2, which gives the entropy in units of bits. The causal chain is as follows: the probability distribution determines the entropy, which in turn determines the amount of information in the message. The mechanism of data compression, for example, relies on reducing the entropy of the message by representing the most likely outcomes using shorter codes. Conversely, the mechanism of error correction relies on adding redundancy to the message, which increases its entropy, but allows for the detection and correction of errors. The noisy channel coding theorem, a fundamental result in information theory, describes the maximum rate at which information can be transmitted over a noisy channel, given the entropy of the channel. This theorem establishes a causal link between the entropy of the channel, the entropy of the message, and the maximum achievable transmission rate.

Conversely, the mechanism of error-correcting codes involves adding redundancy to a signal to increase its entropy, making it more resilient to errors during transmission. Throughout these mechanisms, the mathematical framework of probability theory and linear algebra provides the foundation for the analysis and manipulation of information.

## Methods And Frameworks

In information theory, several methods and frameworks are employed to quantify and analyze information. The Shannon-Hartley theorem, for instance, provides a formula to calculate the maximum rate at which information can be transmitted over a communication channel, given by C = B log2(1 + S/N), where C is the channel capacity, B is the bandwidth, S is the signal power, and N is the noise power. This theorem is used when designing communication systems, but its failure mode occurs when the signal-to-noise ratio is low, resulting in a significant decrease in channel capacity. 
The Kullback-Leibler divergence is another crucial method, used to measure the difference between two probability distributions, given by D(P || Q) = ∑P(x) log(P(x)/Q(x)). This method is employed when comparing the similarity between two distributions, but its failure mode occurs when the distributions have different support sets, resulting in an undefined value. 
The entropy formula, H = -∑p(x) log2 p(x), is used to calculate the amount of uncertainty or randomness in a probability distribution, and is commonly used in data compression and source coding. However, its failure mode occurs when the probability distribution is not well-defined, resulting in an inaccurate calculation of entropy. 
The Bayesian framework is also widely used in information theory, providing a method for updating probabilities based on new information, given by P(H|E) = P(E|H)P(H)/P(E). This framework is used when making inferences about a system, but its failure mode occurs when the prior probabilities are not well-defined, resulting in an inaccurate posterior distribution. 
The rate-distortion theory, which provides a framework for analyzing the trade-off between the rate of compression and the distortion of the compressed signal, is another key method in information theory. This theory is used when designing lossy compression algorithms, but its failure mode occurs when the distortion measure is not well-defined, resulting in a suboptimal compression scheme.

## Worked Examples

To illustrate the principles of information theory, consider the following examples.

1. Suppose we have a binary symmetric channel with a probability of error p = 0.01. If we send a sequence of 1000 bits, what is the expected number of bits in error? 
Using the formula for the expected value of a binomial distribution, we have E(X) = np, where n is the number of trials (1000 bits) and p is the probability of error (0.01). Thus, E(X) = 1000 * 0.01 = 10.

2. A discrete memoryless source produces symbols from the alphabet {a, b, c} with probabilities P(a) = 0.5, P(b) = 0.3, and P(c) = 0.2. Calculate the entropy of this source. 
Using the formula for entropy H = -∑P(x) log2 P(x), we have H = -[0.5 log2 0.5 + 0.3 log2 0.3 + 0.2 log2 0.2]. Evaluating this expression gives H ≈ 1.49 bits per symbol.

3. Consider a channel with a capacity of 1000 bits per second and a signal-to-noise ratio of 10 dB. If we use a code with a rate of 500 bits per second, what is the maximum achievable error probability? 
Using the formula for the channel capacity C = B log2 (1 + SNR), where B is the bandwidth and SNR is the signal-to-noise ratio, we can calculate the capacity. However, since the code rate is below the channel capacity, reliable transmission is possible. The maximum achievable error probability depends on the specific code used and is limited by the Shannon-Hartley theorem.

1. **Calculating Entropy**: Suppose we have a discrete random variable X with possible outcomes {A, B, C} and corresponding probabilities {0.2, 0.5, 0.3}. The entropy H(X) is calculated as -∑(p(x) * log2(p(x))) = -[0.2*log2(0.2) + 0.5*log2(0.5) + 0.3*log2(0.3)] = -[0.2*(-2.32) + 0.5*(-1) + 0.3*(-1.74)] = -[-0.464 + -0.5 + -0.522] = 1.486 bits.

2. **Conditional Entropy**: Given two discrete random variables X and Y, the conditional entropy H(X|Y) is the entropy of X given that Y is known. If X has possible outcomes {0, 1} with probabilities {0.4, 0.6} when Y=0, and {0.7, 0.3} when Y=1, and Y has probabilities {0.3, 0.7}, then H(X|Y) = ∑(p(y) * H(X|Y=y)) = 0.3 * [-0.4*log2(0.4) - 0.6*log2(0.6)] + 0.7 * [-0.7*log2(0.7) - 0.3*log2(0.3)] = 0.3 * [0.528 + 0.258] + 0.7 * [0.360 + 0.521] = 0.3 * 0.786 + 0.7 * 0.881 = 0.235 + 0.616 = 0.851 bits.

3. **Mutual Information**: The mutual information I(X;Y) between two discrete random variables X and Y is the reduction in uncertainty about X given Y. If X has outcomes {0, 1} with probabilities {0.2, 0.8} and Y has outcomes {0, 1} with probabilities {0.4, 0.6}, and the joint probability distribution is given by p(X=0,Y=0)=0.1, p(X=0,Y=1)=0.1, p(X=1,Y=0)=0.2, p(X=1,Y=1)=0.6, then I(X;Y) = H(X) + H(Y) - H(X,Y) = [-0.2*log2(0.2) - 0.8*log2(0.8)] + [-0.4*log2(0.4) - 0.6*log2(0.6)] - [-0.1*log2(0.1) - 0.1*log2(0.1) - 0.2*log2(0.2) - 0.6*log2(0.6)] = [0.464 + 0.258] + [0.528 + 0.258] - [-0.332 - 0.332 - 0.464 - 0.258] = 0.722 + 0.786 - (-1.386) = 0.722 + 0.786 + 1.386 = 2.894 bits.

## Applications

Information theory has numerous applications in various fields, including data compression, cryptography, and channel coding. In data compression, techniques such as Huffman coding and arithmetic coding utilize the principles of entropy and source coding to reduce the amount of data required to represent a message. This is achieved by assigning shorter codes to more frequently occurring symbols, thereby reducing the overall length of the encoded message. In cryptography, information theory is used to analyze the security of encryption algorithms, such as the one-time pad, which is proven to be unbreakable using the principles of entropy and mutual information. Channel coding, which involves the detection and correction of errors in digital data, relies heavily on information theory concepts like error-correcting codes, such as Reed-Solomon codes and turbo codes. Additionally, information theory is applied in statistical inference, where it is used to quantify the amount of information gained from a sample of data, and in machine learning, where it is used to develop algorithms for clustering, classification, and dimensionality reduction. The principles of information theory are also used in telecommunications, where they are applied to the design of communication systems, including modulation schemes and error-control mechanisms.

This is achieved through techniques like Huffman coding and arithmetic coding, which assign shorter codes to more frequently occurring symbols. In channel coding, information theory is used to detect and correct errors that occur during data transmission over noisy channels. This is done using error-correcting codes like Reed-Solomon codes and low-density parity-check codes, which add redundancy to the data to enable error detection and correction. Information theory is also used in cryptography to develop secure encryption algorithms, such as the Advanced Encryption Standard (AES), which rely on the principles of entropy and mutual information to ensure the confidentiality and integrity of data. Additionally, information theory has applications in statistical inference, where it is used to estimate the probability distributions of random variables and to make inferences about the underlying data-generating process. In machine learning, information theory is used to develop algorithms for clustering, dimensionality reduction, and feature selection, such as the information bottleneck method, which aims to minimize the mutual information between the input and output variables while preserving the relevant information.

## Common Errors

In information theory, several common mistakes arise from misunderstandings of fundamental concepts. One error is confusing entropy with physical entropy, despite both being measured in the same units (e.g., bits or nats). Mathematical entropy, introduced by Claude Shannon, quantifies the uncertainty or information content in a probability distribution, whereas physical entropy relates to thermodynamic systems. Another mistake involves incorrectly applying the chain rule for mutual information, which can lead to errors in calculating the information transmitted between variables. Additionally, practitioners often mistakenly assume that data compression ratios directly imply a certain level of entropy reduction, neglecting that compression algorithms exploit statistical structure rather than directly reducing entropy. A further error lies in misinterpreting the data processing inequality, which states that processing cannot increase mutual information, as implying that processing data always reduces information, when in fact it may preserve or even increase certain types of information, depending on the context. These mistakes stem from a lack of clarity about the probabilistic foundations of information theory and its distinctness from other fields.

One such error is the confusion between entropy and physical entropy. While entropy in information theory, denoted by H, measures the uncertainty or randomness in a probability distribution, physical entropy is a concept from thermodynamics that relates to the disorder or randomness of a physical system. Although both concepts share a similar name and intuitive meaning, they are distinct and should not be used interchangeably.

Another mistake is the incorrect application of Shannon's theorem, which states that the entropy of a discrete random variable is bounded by the entropy of its binary representation. Some practitioners mistakenly assume that this theorem implies that entropy is a direct measure of the information content of a message, when in fact, it measures the average amount of information produced by a stochastic source.

Furthermore, the error of assuming independence between events or random variables when calculating mutual information or joint entropy is common. Mutual information, a measure of the dependence between two random variables, is often miscalculated due to incorrect assumptions about the independence of the variables. The chain rule for entropy, which states that the joint entropy of two variables is equal to the sum of the conditional entropy of one variable given the other and the entropy of the other variable, is also frequently misapplied.

Lastly, the mistake of neglecting the difference between self-information and entropy is significant. Self-information, a measure of the information content of a specific outcome, is distinct from entropy, which is a measure of the average information content of a random variable. While related, these concepts serve different purposes in information theory and should be applied accordingly.

These errors stem from a lack of understanding of the underlying mathematical principles and the distinct definitions of key concepts in information theory. A thorough grasp of these principles is essential for accurate application and interpretation of information theoretic measures.

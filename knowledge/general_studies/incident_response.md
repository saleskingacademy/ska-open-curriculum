# Incident Response

## Foundations

Incident response refers to the process by which an organization responds to and manages a security incident, defined as a violation or imminent threat of violation of computer security policies, acceptable use policies, or standard security practices. A security incident can be a malicious act, such as a cyber attack, or an unintentional act, like a data breach due to human error. Key terms include threat, defined as a potential occurrence that could compromise the security of an organization's assets, and vulnerability, which refers to a weakness in a system's design, implementation, or operation that could be exploited by a threat. Risk is the likelihood that a threat will occur and exploit a vulnerability, causing harm to the organization. Incident response is guided by first principles, including containment, to prevent further damage, eradication, to remove the root cause of the incident, recovery, to restore systems and data, and post-incident activities, to review the response and improve future incident handling. Vocabulary essential to practitioners includes incident, event, and breach, with an incident being a confirmed security event that has caused harm, an event being a potential security incident that requires investigation, and a breach being the unauthorized access or disclosure of sensitive information.

## Mechanisms

Incident response mechanisms involve a series of steps that are triggered by the detection of a security incident. The process begins with identification, where monitoring tools and systems detect anomalies or suspicious activity, triggering an alert. This alert is then reported to the incident response team, who initiate the response process by assessing the situation to determine the scope and severity of the incident. The team then contains the incident by isolating affected systems or networks to prevent further damage. Eradication follows, where the root cause of the incident is identified and removed. Recovery involves restoring systems and data to a known good state, and post-incident activities include lessons learned and incident documentation to improve future response efforts. Throughout this process, communication and coordination among team members and stakeholders are crucial for effective incident response. The causal chain is as follows: detection leads to reporting, which triggers assessment, leading to containment, eradication, recovery, and finally post-incident activities, with each step informing and influencing the next.

## Methods And Frameworks

Incident response employs various methods and frameworks to manage and mitigate the impact of security incidents. The NIST Cybersecurity Framework provides a structured approach to incident response, emphasizing preparation, detection, response, and recovery. The Incident Response Life Cycle model outlines the stages of incident response, including identification, containment, eradication, recovery, and lessons learned. The OODA Loop (Observe, Orient, Decide, Act) is a decision-making model used in incident response to facilitate rapid and effective response. The MITRE ATT&CK framework provides a knowledge base of adversary tactics, techniques, and procedures to inform incident response strategies. The ISO/IEC 27035 standard offers guidelines for incident response, including incident response planning, detection, and response. Each method and framework has its strengths and weaknesses, and the choice of which to use depends on the organization's specific needs and incident type. Failure modes include inadequate preparation, insufficient resources, and poor communication, which can lead to delayed or ineffective response, exacerbating the incident's impact.

## Worked Examples

1. **Data Breach Response**: A company with 10,000 customer records experiences a data breach, resulting in the exposure of 2,000 records. The cost of notifying each affected customer is $50, and the cost of providing credit monitoring services for each customer is $100. If the company decides to notify all 10,000 customers and provide credit monitoring services to the 2,000 affected customers, the total cost of the response would be: 10,000 * $50 (notification) + 2,000 * $100 (credit monitoring) = $500,000 + $200,000 = $700,000.

2. **Ransomware Attack**: A hospital's network is infected with ransomware, resulting in the encryption of 500 GB of sensitive data. The attackers demand a ransom of $100,000 in exchange for the decryption key. However, the hospital has backups of 80% of the encrypted data. The cost of restoring the data from backups is $5,000, and the cost of paying the ransom is $100,000. If the hospital decides to restore the data from backups, the total cost of the response would be: $5,000 (restoration) + $0 (no ransom paid). In contrast, paying the ransom would result in a total cost of $100,000.

3. **Denial-of-Service (DoS) Attack**: A company's website is experiencing a DoS attack, resulting in 50% downtime and a loss of $10,000 per hour in revenue. The company's incident response team estimates that it will take 4 hours to mitigate the attack. The cost of engaging a third-party DoS mitigation service is $5,000 per hour. If the company decides to engage the third-party service, the total cost of the response would be: 4 hours * $5,000 per hour (mitigation service) = $20,000. In contrast, not engaging the service would result in a loss of $10,000 per hour * 4 hours = $40,000 in revenue.

1. **Network Intrusion**: A company's security team detects a network intrusion with 500 compromised user accounts. The incident response team must contain the damage, eradicate the threat, and recover from the incident. Assuming a 2-hour containment time, 4-hour eradication time, and 6-hour recovery time, calculate the total incident response time. 
Total response time = Containment time + Eradication time + Recovery time = 2 hours + 4 hours + 6 hours = 12 hours.

2. **Data Breach**: A retail company experiences a data breach with 10,000 customer records stolen, including sensitive information such as credit card numbers and addresses. The incident response team must notify affected customers and provide credit monitoring services. Assuming a cost of $50 per customer for credit monitoring, calculate the total cost of the incident response.
Total cost = Number of affected customers * Cost per customer = 10,000 customers * $50/customer = $500,000.

3. **Ransomware Attack**: A hospital's computer system is infected with ransomware, resulting in 200 encrypted medical records. The incident response team must pay a ransom of $10,000 to restore access to the records or spend 10 hours restoring from backups. Calculate the cost-benefit analysis of paying the ransom versus restoring from backups, assuming a labor cost of $100 per hour.
Cost of paying ransom = $10,000
Cost of restoring from backups = Labor cost * Number of hours = $100/hour * 10 hours = $1,000
In this scenario, restoring from backups is the more cost-effective option.

Consider a scenario where a company's database is compromised, resulting in the exposure of 10,000 customer records. The incident response team must contain, eradicate, recover, and post-incident activities.

1. Containment: If the team takes 2 hours to contain the breach and the average cost per hour is $500, the total containment cost is 2 * $500 = $1,000. 
2. Eradication and Recovery: Assuming it takes 10 hours to eradicate the malware and recover the system at an average cost of $750 per hour, the total cost is 10 * $750 = $7,500. 
3. Post-Incident Activities: A post-incident review and implementation of additional security measures may take 5 hours at $1,000 per hour, totaling 5 * $1,000 = $5,000.

The total cost of incident response is $1,000 + $7,500 + $5,000 = $13,500. This example illustrates the importance of prompt and effective incident response in minimizing costs and mitigating the impact of a security breach.

## Applications

Incident response is applied in various domains, including cybersecurity, emergency management, and business continuity. In cybersecurity, incident response involves responding to security breaches, such as malware outbreaks or unauthorized access to sensitive data. This includes identifying the incident, containing the damage, eradicating the threat, recovering systems, and post-incident activities like reporting and lessons learned. 
In emergency management, incident response involves coordinating responses to natural disasters, such as hurricanes or wildfires, and man-made disasters, like industrial accidents. This includes activating emergency operations centers, deploying response teams, and providing aid to affected populations. 
In business continuity, incident response focuses on minimizing the impact of disruptions to business operations, such as supply chain disruptions or IT system failures. This includes activating business continuity plans, switching to backup systems, and communicating with stakeholders. 
Key principles of incident response include preparation, swift action, and continuous improvement. Preparation involves developing incident response plans, conducting training and exercises, and establishing incident response teams. Swift action involves quickly responding to incidents, containing damage, and minimizing impact. Continuous improvement involves reviewing incident response efforts, identifying areas for improvement, and updating plans and procedures accordingly.

## Common Errors

Practitioners often make mistakes during incident response due to inadequate planning, insufficient training, or failure to follow established protocols. One common error is the premature escalation of incidents, which can lead to unnecessary resource allocation and distraction from the actual problem. Another mistake is the failure to properly contain and isolate affected systems, allowing the incident to spread and causing further damage. Insufficient documentation and communication are also prevalent, making it difficult to track the incident's progression and coordinate response efforts. Furthermore, neglecting to involve relevant stakeholders, such as legal or public relations teams, can result in reputational damage and non-compliance with regulatory requirements. Additionally, the lack of continuous monitoring and post-incident review can prevent organizations from identifying the root cause of the incident and implementing effective measures to prevent similar incidents from occurring in the future. These errors can be attributed to a lack of understanding of incident response principles, inadequate training, and insufficient resources, highlighting the importance of thorough planning, training, and execution in effective incident response.

## Advanced

Incident response is evolving to address complex, high-impact threats. Advanced persistent threats (APTs) and zero-day exploits require specialized expertise and tools. Artificial intelligence (AI) and machine learning (ML) are being applied to enhance incident detection, response, and prediction. The use of security orchestration, automation, and response (SOAR) systems is becoming more prevalent, enabling organizations to streamline and accelerate incident response processes. Open questions in the field include the development of effective countermeasures against emerging threats, such as quantum computer-based attacks, and the integration of incident response with other security disciplines, like threat intelligence and security information and event management (SIEM). The field is moving towards more proactive and predictive approaches, incorporating threat hunting, adversary emulation, and continuous monitoring to stay ahead of potential incidents. Additionally, the increasing adoption of cloud computing, Internet of Things (IoT), and industrial control systems (ICS) is creating new challenges and opportunities for incident response, requiring specialized knowledge and expertise to address unique security concerns in these environments.

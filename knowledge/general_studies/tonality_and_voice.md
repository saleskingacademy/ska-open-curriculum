## Foundations

Tonality refers to the system of organization of pitches and harmonies in music, based on a central note or tone, known as the tonic. Voice, in this context, denotes the unique sound or expression of a musical instrument, singer, or composer. A practitioner must understand key terms: pitch, the perceived highness or lowness of a sound; harmony, the combination of multiple pitches sounding simultaneously; and timbre, the distinctive tone color or quality of a sound. The tonic, a foundational concept, is the central note of a key or scale, providing a sense of stability and finality. Scales, series of pitches in a specific pattern of whole and half steps, are the building blocks of tonality. Intervals, the distances between pitches, are classified as harmonic (simultaneous) or melodic (successive). Chord progressions, series of harmonies in a specific order, underpin the harmonic structure of music. Understanding these core definitions and principles is essential for working with tonality and voice in music.

## Mechanisms

Tonality and voice in music are generated through a complex interplay of cognitive, perceptual, and acoustic mechanisms. The process begins with the listener's perception of pitch, which is influenced by the frequency and harmonic content of sound waves. When a series of pitches are heard in succession, the listener's brain organizes them into a hierarchical structure based on their relative stability and tension. This hierarchy is rooted in the acoustic properties of sound, particularly the harmonic series and the pattern of whole-number frequency ratios that underlie it. As the listener hears a sequence of pitches, they infer a tonal center or key, which serves as a reference point for evaluating the stability and tension of subsequent pitches. The voice, or melodic line, is then perceived as a trajectory through this tonal space, with its own internal hierarchy of pitch and rhythm. The causal chain is as follows: sound waves stimulate the ear, which sends signals to the brain, where they are processed and organized into a percept of pitch and tonality, which in turn influences the perception of voice and melody. This process is mediated by cognitive biases, such as the expectation of tonal resolution and the tendency to hear patterns and hierarchies in sound. The resulting experience of tonality and voice is a dynamic interplay between the acoustic properties of sound, the cognitive processes of the listener, and the cultural and historical context in which the music is heard.

## Methods And Frameworks

The analysis of tonality and voice in music and speech employs various methods and frameworks. The Praat software utilizes a range of algorithms, including autocorrelation and cepstral analysis, to extract pitch and voice quality features from audio signals. When to use: for detailed acoustic analysis of speech and singing. Failure mode: sensitive to noise and recording quality. 
The Voice Onset Time (VOT) framework measures the timing of voice onset relative to the release of a stop consonant, useful for distinguishing between voiced and voiceless consonants. When to use: for phonetic analysis of consonant production. Failure mode: assumes a clear distinction between voiced and voiceless categories. 
The Tonality Framework by Lerdahl and Jackendoff provides a hierarchical model of tonal structure, analyzing the relationships between pitches and chords. When to use: for understanding the harmonic and melodic structure of Western tonal music. Failure mode: limited to Western tonal music and may not account for cultural or stylistic variations. 
The Spectral Centroid formula calculates the weighted average of frequency components in a signal, useful for analyzing the timbre and brightness of a voice or instrument. When to use: for comparing the timbre of different voices or instruments. Failure mode: sensitive to the choice of weighting function and frequency range. 
These methods and frameworks provide a foundation for analyzing tonality and voice, but their limitations and assumptions must be considered to ensure accurate and meaningful results.

Tonality and voice analysis employ various methods, including the Osgood Semantic Differential, which measures connotative meaning using bipolar adjective scales, suitable for assessing overall tone in texts. The Valence-Arousal-Dominance (VAD) model evaluates emotional tone based on valence, arousal, and dominance dimensions, applicable when analyzing emotional expressions in speech or writing. The Linguistic Inquiry and Word Count (LIWC) method uses dictionaries to categorize words into psychological categories, useful for examining language use in social media or personal narratives. However, LIWC's reliance on predefined dictionaries can lead to failure modes such as missing context-dependent expressions or newly coined words. The Tone-Analyzing Lexicon (TAL) assesses tone using a dictionary of words with associated tone scores, effective for analyzing tone in product reviews or customer feedback, but may fail to account for sarcasm, irony, or figurative language. The Multidimensional Scaling (MDS) framework visualizes tone and voice relationships as spatial maps, useful for identifying patterns in large datasets, but can be limited by its reliance on numerical data and potential for dimensionality reduction errors.

The analysis of tonality and voice in music and speech employs several methods and frameworks. 
Pentatonic analysis is used to examine the five-note scale patterns in melodies, applicable when identifying folk or cultural influences, but may fail to account for chromaticism or atonality. 
The Krumhansl-Schmuckler key-finding algorithm is utilized to determine the tonal center of a piece, effective for Western classical music, yet may struggle with modal interchange or ambiguous tonality. 
Spectral analysis, such as Fast Fourier Transform (FFT), is employed to examine the frequency content of audio signals, suitable for investigating timbre and voice quality, but may be limited by window size and overlap. 
The Source-Filter Theory of speech production models the vocal tract as a filter modifying the glottal source, useful for understanding voice production mechanisms, but may oversimplify the complexities of human articulation. 
The Mel-Frequency Cepstral Coefficients (MFCCs) method is used to extract spectral features from speech signals, applicable in automatic speech recognition and speaker identification, yet may be sensitive to noise and channel effects. 
Each method has its strengths and limitations, and the choice of framework depends on the specific research question, data type, and desired outcome.

## History

Oldest known map — Babylonian clay tablet ~600 BCE (world as flat disc). Ptolemy (150 CE — latitude/longitude grid, Geographia — used until Age of Exploration). Portolan charts (13th century — compass navigation, Mediterranean). Mercator projection (1569 — straight rhumb lines for navigation, but distorts size — Greenland looks as big as Africa, but Africa is 14× larger). PROJECTIONS: The fundamental problem — projecting a sphere onto a flat surface ALWAYS distorts something. Properties: conformal (preserves shape — Mercator), equal-area (preserves size — Mollweide), equidistant (preserves distance from center — Azimuthal). Peters projection controversy (1974 — "fairer" to Global South). GIS (GEOGRAPHIC INFORMATION SYSTEMS): Digital mapping + spatial analysis. Layers of data (topography, roads, population, land use, environmental) stacked and analyzed. ESRI ArcGIS, QGIS (open source). Spatial queries, geoprocessing, network analysis. REMOTE SENSING: Satellite imagery (Landsat since 1972, Sentinel, commercial — Planet, Maxar). Aerial photography. LiDAR (laser scanning — penetrates forest canopy, discovered Maya cities). Drone mapping. GNSS (GPS/GLONASS/Galileo/BeiDou). GPS TECHNOLOGY: 24+ satellites at 20,200km altitude. Trilateration from 4+ satellites. Civilian accuracy ~2m (was 100m until Selective Availability turned off in 2000). Differential GPS ~1cm. RTK sub-centimeter. MODERN: Google Maps/Earth, OpenStreetMap (collaborative), Story maps, Web GIS, spatial data science, location intelligence. Machine learning on satellite imagery (deforestation detection, building footprints, crop classification). APPLIED: $15B+ GIS market. Used in urban planning, disaster response, logistics, environmental monitoring, military, real estate, agriculture, public health, epidemiology.

## Methods And Frameworks

In Cartography GIS, several methods and frameworks are employed to analyze and visualize spatial data. The Spatial Autocorrelation method, such as Moran's I, is used to measure the degree of similarity between neighboring values, indicating clustering or dispersion patterns. It is useful for identifying hotspots and cold spots in crime rates, disease outbreaks, or environmental phenomena. However, its failure mode lies in assuming stationarity, where the spatial process is assumed to be constant across the study area.

The Buffer Analysis method is utilized to examine the spatial relationships between features within a specified distance, helping to identify areas of influence or impact. It is commonly applied in urban planning, emergency response, and environmental impact assessments. Nevertheless, its limitation arises when dealing with complex or irregularly shaped features, where the buffer zone may not accurately represent the true area of influence.

The Network Analysis method, including the shortest path and closest facility models, is employed to study the movement and interaction of people, goods, and services within a network. It is essential in transportation planning, logistics, and emergency response. However, its failure mode occurs when the network is incomplete, inaccurate, or dynamic, leading to incorrect routing or allocation of resources.

The Overlay Analysis method, using techniques such as intersection and union, is used to combine multiple datasets and examine the spatial relationships between them. It is useful for identifying areas of overlap, proximity, or coincidence between different phenomena. Nevertheless, its limitation arises when dealing with datasets of varying scales, resolutions, or projections, which can lead to errors in the overlay process.

The Geographically Weighted Regression (GWR) model is used to examine the spatial variation in relationships between variables, providing a local perspective on global patterns. It is useful for identifying areas where the relationship between variables is strong or weak. However, its failure mode lies in assuming a fixed bandwidth, where the spatial weighting scheme may not accurately capture the local variability in the data.

## Common Errors

In cartography and GIS within the social sciences, several common errors can lead to misleading or inaccurate representations of data. One such error is the misuse of choropleth maps, where the choice of classification method and number of classes can significantly impact the interpretation of the data. For instance, using a large number of classes can create a visually appealing map but may obscure the underlying patterns, while using too few classes can oversimplify the data. Another error is the failure to account for spatial autocorrelation, where the assumption of independence between neighboring observations is violated, leading to incorrect statistical inferences. Additionally, the use of inappropriate spatial weights, such as using a simple Euclidean distance measure in a network-based analysis, can also lead to flawed conclusions. Furthermore, the lack of consideration for the modifiable areal unit problem (MAUP) can result in erroneous findings, as the aggregation of data to different spatial units can produce varying results. These errors can be mitigated by carefully evaluating the research question, selecting appropriate mapping and analysis techniques, and considering the limitations and potential biases of the data.

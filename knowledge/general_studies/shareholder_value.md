# Shareholder Value

> The course assumes prior knowledge of financial concepts and applies principles to real situations.

## Overview

Professional discipline for the sovereign specialist: shareholder value. Applied practice used to generate revenue.

## Foundations

Shareholder value refers to the financial return generated by a company for its shareholders, typically measured by the increase in the company's stock price and dividends paid. A key concept is **total shareholder return (TSR)**, which is the total return a shareholder receives from a stock, including **capital appreciation** (the increase in the stock's value) and **dividend yield** (the ratio of annual dividend payment to the stock's current price). **Economic value added (EVA)** is another metric used to evaluate a company's performance, calculated as **net operating profit after taxes (NOPAT)** minus the **cost of capital** (the minimum return required by shareholders and debt holders) multiplied by the company's **capital employed** (the total amount of capital used to generate profits). Understanding these concepts is crucial for practitioners to analyze and create value for shareholders. **Free cash flow (FCF)**, the amount of cash available to shareholders after a company has paid its operating expenses and capital expenditures, is also a vital metric in assessing a company's ability to generate shareholder value.

A key concept in this context is **total shareholder return (TSR)**, which is the total return a shareholder receives from a stock, including **capital appreciation** (the increase in the stock's value) and **dividends** (a portion of the company's earnings distributed to shareholders). **Economic value added (EVA)** is another important metric, representing the difference between a company's **net operating profit after taxes (NOPAT)** and its **cost of capital** (the minimum return required by shareholders and debt holders). Understanding these concepts requires familiarity with **financial statements**, including the **balance sheet** (a snapshot of a company's assets, liabilities, and equity at a given time), **income statement** (a summary of a company's revenues and expenses over a specific period), and **cash flow statement** (a report of a company's inflows and outflows of cash). A practitioner must also know the principles of **discounted cash flow (DCF) analysis**, which estimates a company's value by discounting its expected future cash flows using the **weighted average cost of capital (WACC)**, a measure of the average cost of capital for a company, calculated as the weighted average of the costs of debt and equity.

## Mechanisms

Shareholder value is created through a series of mechanisms that ultimately lead to an increase in the company's stock price. The process begins with the board of directors and executive management setting clear, measurable financial goals, such as return on equity (ROE) or economic value added (EVA). These goals are then communicated to employees and stakeholders, who work to achieve them through strategic planning and implementation. As the company achieves its financial goals, it generates increased earnings and cash flows, which can be used to invest in new opportunities or distribute to shareholders through dividends or share repurchases. The increased earnings and cash flows lead to higher net income, which in turn increases the company's retained earnings and book value. As the company's book value and earnings grow, investors become more confident in the company's ability to generate future returns, leading to an increase in the stock price. This increase in stock price reflects the increased shareholder value, as investors are willing to pay more for a share of the company's future earnings. The causal chain is as follows: financial goals → strategic planning → increased earnings and cash flows → higher net income → increased book value → increased investor confidence → higher stock price → increased shareholder value.

The creation and delivery of shareholder value involves a series of interconnected steps. It begins with the board of directors and senior management setting the company's overall strategy, which includes defining its mission, vision, and objectives. This strategy is then translated into specific financial goals, such as revenue growth, profit margins, and return on equity (ROE). The management team develops and implements plans to achieve these goals, which may involve investing in new projects, expanding into new markets, or improving operational efficiency. As the company executes its plans, it generates earnings, which are then distributed to shareholders in the form of dividends or reinvested in the business to fuel further growth. The retained earnings can be used to reduce debt, invest in new opportunities, or repurchase shares, all of which can increase the company's stock price and, consequently, shareholder value. The stock price, in turn, is influenced by the company's financial performance, as well as external factors such as the overall state of the economy, industry trends, and investor sentiment. Ultimately, the delivery of shareholder value is measured by the total return to shareholders, which includes both dividend income and capital appreciation. This total return is the culmination of a causal chain that begins with strategic planning, extends through operational execution, and ends with the creation of wealth for shareholders.

## Methods And Frameworks

The creation and measurement of shareholder value can be approached through various methods and frameworks. The Discounted Cash Flow (DCF) model is a widely used method, which estimates the present value of future cash flows using a discount rate, such as the Weighted Average Cost of Capital (WACC). This method is suitable for companies with stable cash flows, but may fail if the discount rate is inaccurately estimated or if the company's cash flows are highly volatile. 
The Economic Value Added (EVA) framework measures the difference between net operating profit after taxes (NOPAT) and the cost of capital, providing a dollar amount of value created. EVA is useful for evaluating company performance, but may be misleading if the cost of capital is not accurately calculated. 
The Market Value Added (MVA) framework calculates the difference between the market value of a company's debt and equity and the book value of its capital, providing a measure of the total value created for shareholders. MVA is useful for comparing companies, but may be influenced by market fluctuations and investor sentiment. 
The Capital Asset Pricing Model (CAPM) is a formula used to estimate the cost of equity, which is a key input in the DCF model and EVA framework. CAPM is suitable for companies with publicly traded stock, but may fail if the market beta is inaccurately estimated or if the company's stock price is highly volatile. 
Each of these methods and frameworks has its strengths and weaknesses, and the choice of which to use depends on the specific company and situation being analyzed.

## Worked Examples

To illustrate the concept of shareholder value, consider the following examples. 
1. A company has 1 million shares outstanding, with a current market price of $50 per share. If the company's management team implements a new strategy that increases earnings per share by 15%, and the price-to-earnings ratio remains constant at 20, calculate the new market price per share and the resulting increase in shareholder value. 
Initial market capitalization = 1,000,000 shares * $50/share = $50,000,000. 
Assuming initial earnings per share of $2.50, the new earnings per share would be $2.50 * 1.15 = $2.875. 
With a price-to-earnings ratio of 20, the new market price per share would be $2.875 * 20 = $57.50. 
New market capitalization = 1,000,000 shares * $57.50/share = $57,500,000. 
Increase in shareholder value = $57,500,000 - $50,000,000 = $7,500,000. 
2. Suppose a firm has a weighted average cost of capital (WACC) of 10% and expects to generate cash flows of $100,000, $120,000, and $150,000 over the next three years. Calculate the present value of these cash flows and determine the shareholder value created. 
Present value of cash flows = $100,000 / (1 + 0.10)^1 + $120,000 / (1 + 0.10)^2 + $150,000 / (1 + 0.10)^3. 
Present value of cash flows = $90,909 + $99,173 + $123,964 = $314,046. 
If the initial investment was $250,000, the shareholder value created would be $314,046 - $250,000 = $64,046. 
3. A company has a return on equity (ROE) of 18% and a dividend payout ratio of 30%. If the current stock price is $80 and the company pays an annual dividend of $3 per share, calculate the expected growth rate in dividends and the resulting increase in shareholder value. 
Expected growth rate in dividends = ROE * (1 - dividend payout ratio) = 0.18 * (1 - 0.30) = 0.126. 
Expected dividend per share in one year = $3 * (1 + 0.126) = $3.378. 
With a constant dividend payout ratio, the expected increase in earnings per share would also be 12.6%. 
Assuming a constant price-to-earnings ratio, the new stock price would be $80 * (1 + 0.126) = $90.048. 
Increase in shareholder value per share = $90.048 - $80 = $10.048.

To illustrate the concept of shareholder value, let's consider three concrete examples.

1. **Calculating Shareholder Value**: Suppose a company has 1 million shares outstanding, with a current market price of $50 per share. The company's expected earnings per share (EPS) for the next year is $5, and the expected growth rate is 10%. Using the dividend discount model, we can calculate the present value of future dividends. If the company pays out 50% of its earnings as dividends, the dividend per share would be $2.50. Assuming a discount rate of 12%, the present value of future dividends can be calculated as $2.50 / (0.12 - 0.10) = $125. Since the current market price is $50, the shareholder value is $125, indicating that the company's shares are undervalued.

2. **Economic Value Added (EVA)**: Consider a company with a net operating profit after taxes (NOPAT) of $100 million, and a cost of capital of 10%. If the company's total capital employed is $500 million, the EVA would be $100m - (10% * $500m) = $50 million. This means that the company has created $50 million of value for its shareholders. To calculate the shareholder value, we can use the EVA formula: Shareholder Value = Current Market Value + Present Value of Future EVA. Assuming a discount rate of 12%, and an expected EVA of $50 million per year for the next 5 years, the present value of future EVA would be $50m / (1 + 0.12)^1 + $50m / (1 + 0.12)^2 + ... + $50m / (1 + 0.12)^5 = $191 million.

3. **Shareholder Value Analysis**: A company has two projects with the following expected returns: Project A has an expected return of 15%, with a cost of capital of 12%, and an initial investment of $100 million. Project B has an expected return of 18%, with a cost of capital of 12%, and an initial investment of $150 million. To calculate the shareholder value created by each project, we can use the net present value (NPV) formula. For Project A, the NPV would be $100m * (1 + 0.15) / (1 + 0.12) - $100m = $25.4 million. For Project B, the NPV would be $150m * (1 + 0.18) / (1 + 0.12) - $150m = $49.1 million. This indicates that Project B creates more value for shareholders than Project A.

## Applications

Shareholder value is a crucial concept in corporate finance, guiding decision-making to maximize returns for investors. In practice, companies use various metrics to measure shareholder value, including Total Shareholder Return (TSR), which calculates the change in stock price plus dividends paid. Firms also employ valuation models, such as the Discounted Cash Flow (DCF) model, to estimate their intrinsic value and make strategic decisions. Additionally, shareholder value is used in executive compensation plans, where incentives are tied to performance metrics like TSR or Economic Value Added (EVA), to align management's interests with those of shareholders. Furthermore, investors and analysts use shareholder value metrics to evaluate investment opportunities, comparing the returns of different companies and industries to inform portfolio decisions. By focusing on shareholder value, companies can prioritize initiatives that drive long-term growth and profitability, such as investing in research and development, expanding into new markets, or optimizing operations to improve efficiency and reduce costs. Effective application of shareholder value principles helps companies create sustainable value for their investors and maintain a competitive edge in the market.

Furthermore, shareholder value is a critical factor in corporate governance, as boards of directors and management teams are responsible for making decisions that maximize long-term value for shareholders, while also considering the interests of other stakeholders. By focusing on shareholder value, companies can ensure that their strategies and actions are aligned with the interests of their owners, ultimately driving long-term success and sustainability.

## Common Errors

Practitioners often mistakenly prioritize short-term gains over long-term value creation, focusing on quarterly earnings per share (EPS) rather than sustainable growth. This myopic approach can lead to decisions that compromise a company's future, such as underinvesting in research and development or overleveraging the balance sheet. Another error is conflating shareholder value with stock price, failing to recognize that the latter is a market-based reflection of expected future cash flows, rather than a direct measure of value. Additionally, some practitioners incorrectly assume that maximizing shareholder value requires maximizing profits, ignoring the importance of risk management and social responsibility in creating long-term value. Furthermore, the failure to account for the time value of money and the cost of capital can lead to flawed investment decisions, as can the misuse of metrics such as return on equity (ROE) or return on assets (ROA) without considering their limitations. These errors can be attributed to a lack of understanding of the principles of corporate finance and the nuances of value creation, highlighting the need for a more comprehensive and informed approach to managing shareholder value.

This can result in managers manipulating these metrics through accounting gimmicks or financial engineering, rather than focusing on the underlying drivers of value creation. Furthermore, the failure to account for externalities, such as environmental and social impacts, can lead to decisions that destroy value in the long run. Additionally, the conflation of shareholder value with stock price can lead to a misguided focus on share price manipulation, rather than genuine value creation. These errors stem from a flawed understanding of the concept of shareholder value, which is often reduced to a simplistic mantra of "maximizing profits" rather than a nuanced approach that balances the interests of various stakeholders.

## Advanced

The concept of shareholder value is multifaceted and has undergone significant evolution. At the graduate level, extensions of this concept delve into the nuances of value creation, the role of agency theory, and the impact of external factors such as market efficiency and regulatory environments. One key area of exploration is the trade-off between short-term and long-term value creation, where managers must balance immediate financial returns with strategic investments that may yield future growth. The principle of shareholder value maximization is also challenged by stakeholder theory, which posits that corporations have responsibilities to a broader range of constituents beyond just shareholders. Open questions in the field include how to effectively measure and prioritize shareholder value in the context of environmental, social, and governance (ESG) considerations, and the extent to which shareholder value should be the primary objective of corporate governance. Furthermore, the increasing recognition of intangible assets and their contribution to firm value raises complex issues regarding valuation and reporting. As the field continues to evolve, it is likely that advancements in data analytics and machine learning will play a critical role in enhancing our understanding of shareholder value creation and the development of more sophisticated valuation models.

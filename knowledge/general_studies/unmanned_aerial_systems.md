# Unmanned Aerial Systems

> The course teaches core concepts, key terminology, and vocabulary of the field, assuming no prior study.

## Foundations

In the context of aviation aerospace, Unmanned Aerial Systems (UAS) refer to a composite system consisting of an Unmanned Aerial Vehicle (UAV), also known as a drone, a ground-based controller, and a system of communications connecting the two. A UAV is defined as a powered, aerial vehicle that does not carry a human operator, uses aerodynamic forces to generate lift, and can fly autonomously or be piloted remotely. The ground-based controller, also referred to as the Ground Control Station (GCS), is the component from which the UAV is operated, either manually by a human operator or autonomously through pre-programmed flight plans. The communication system, which can include radio frequencies, satellite links, or other data links, enables the exchange of commands, telemetry data, and sometimes video or sensor data between the UAV and the GCS. Key vocabulary includes "autonomy," referring to the UAV's ability to operate without real-time human input, and "payload," which denotes the cargo, sensors, or equipment the UAV is designed to carry. Understanding these core definitions and first principles is essential for practitioners in the field of UAS, as they form the basis for designing, operating, and regulating these systems within aviation aerospace. A UAV is defined as a power-driven, aerodynamic vehicle that operates without a human pilot on board, using aerodynamic forces to generate lift and propulsion to generate thrust. UAS operation involves the integration of these components to achieve autonomous or remotely controlled flight.

## Mechanisms

The operation of Unmanned Aerial Systems (UAS) involves a complex interplay of mechanical, electrical, and software components. The causal chain begins with the launch and propulsion system, which can be either a catapult, a runway, or a hand launch, depending on the UAS design. Once airborne, the UAS is controlled by a flight control system, which consists of a combination of sensors, actuators, and control algorithms. The sensors, such as accelerometers, gyroscopes, and GPS, provide data on the UAS's state, including its position, velocity, and attitude. This data is then processed by the control algorithms, which generate commands to the actuators, such as servos and motors, to adjust the UAS's control surfaces and propulsion system. The control surfaces, including ailerons, elevators, and rudder, control the UAS's orientation and trajectory, while the propulsion system, typically a propeller or jet engine, generates thrust. The UAS's autopilot system, which can be pre-programmed or remotely controlled, sends commands to the flight control system to execute a planned mission, such as surveillance or inspection. The UAS's communication system, which can include radio frequency (RF) or satellite links, enables communication with the ground control station, allowing for real-time monitoring and control of the UAS. The ground control station, which can be a portable or fixed system, provides the interface for the operator to monitor and control the UAS, as well as to plan and execute missions. Throughout the operation, the UAS's systems are powered by a battery or fuel system, which must be carefully managed to ensure safe and efficient operation. The sensors provide data on the UAS's position, orientation, and velocity, which is used by the autopilot system to adjust the control surfaces and maintain stable flight. The autopilot system, typically a fly-by-wire (FBW) system, sends signals to the actuators, which control the movement of the ailerons, elevators, and rudder. The actuators, in turn, adjust the control surfaces to achieve the desired orientation and trajectory. The UAS's propulsion system, typically an electric motor or internal combustion engine, provides the necessary thrust to overcome drag and maintain airspeed. The onboard computer continuously monitors the UAS's state and adjusts the control surfaces and propulsion system as needed to ensure stable and efficient flight. The UAS also communicates with the ground control station (GCS) via a radio link, transmitting telemetry data and receiving commands from the operator. The GCS provides real-time monitoring and control of the UAS, allowing operators to adjust the mission plan or intervene in case of an emergency.

## Methods And Frameworks

In Unmanned Aerial Systems (UAS), several methods and frameworks are employed to ensure safe and efficient operation. The Federal Aviation Administration (FAA) recommends the use of the Systems Engineering Process, which involves defining requirements, designing, testing, and validating UAS systems. The Failure Mode and Effects Analysis (FMEA) method is used to identify potential failure modes and their effects on the system. The Fault Tree Analysis (FTA) model is applied to evaluate the probability of failure and identify critical components. The Six Degrees of Freedom (6DoF) model is utilized to simulate UAS flight dynamics, taking into account factors such as wind resistance and air density. The Kalman Filter formula is used for state estimation and navigation, providing accurate positioning and velocity data. However, the Kalman Filter's failure mode can occur when the system is subjected to high levels of noise or uncertainty, leading to divergence from the true state. The Autoregressive Integrated Moving Average (ARIMA) model is used for forecasting and predicting UAS performance, but its failure mode can occur when the underlying assumptions are violated, resulting in inaccurate predictions. The use of these methods and frameworks requires a thorough understanding of UAS systems and their components, as well as the ability to analyze and mitigate potential failure modes.

## Worked Examples

To illustrate the application of Unmanned Aerial Systems (UAS) in aviation aerospace, consider the following examples:

1. **UAS Mission Planning**: A UAS with a cruise speed of 50 m/s and endurance of 2 hours is tasked with surveying a rectangular area of 5 km x 2 km. If the UAS operates at an altitude of 1000 ft, what is the required number of passes to cover the entire area, assuming a swath width of 500 m? 
Given: Area = 5 km x 2 km = 10 km^2, Swath width = 500 m, UAS speed = 50 m/s. 
First, convert the area to square meters: 10 km^2 x (1000 m/km)^2 = 10,000,000 m^2. 
Then, calculate the number of passes: Number of passes = Total area / (Swath width x Pass length). 
Since the UAS endurance is 2 hours, the maximum distance it can cover is: Distance = Speed x Time = 50 m/s x 7200 s = 360,000 m. 
Thus, the number of passes = 10,000,000 m^2 / (500 m x 360,000 m) = 0.0556. However, since the number of passes must be a whole number, we round up to the nearest whole number, resulting in 1 pass being insufficient. The UAS would need to make multiple passes, taking into account turn times and potential overlap. Assuming the pass length is equal to the longer side of the area (5 km = 5000 m), the number of passes is: Number of passes = 10,000,000 m^2 / (500 m x 5000 m) = 4.

2. **UAS Performance**: A small UAS has a mass of 20 kg, including payload, and is powered by an electric motor with a thrust-to-weight ratio of 3:1. If the UAS is required to climb at a rate of 5 m/s, what is the required power output of the motor? 
Given: UAS mass = 20 kg, Thrust-to-weight ratio = 3:1, Climb rate = 5 m/s. 
First, calculate the thrust: Thrust = (Thrust-to-weight ratio) x (UAS mass x g), where g = 9.81 m/s^2. 
Thus, Thrust = 3 x (20 kg x 9.81 m/s^2) = 588.6 N. 
Then, calculate the power output: Power = Thrust x Velocity = 588.6 N x 5 m/s = 2943 W.

3. **UAS Sensor Integration**: A UAS is equipped with a camera having a resolution of 10 megapixels and a field of view of 60 degrees. If the UAS operates at an altitude of 500 m, what is the ground resolution of the camera in cm/pixel? 
Given: Camera resolution = 10 megapixels, Field of view = 60 degrees, UAS altitude = 500 m. 
First, calculate the ground resolution: Ground resolution = (UAS altitude x Pixel size) / (Focal length x Sensor size). 
However, since the exact camera specifications are not provided, we can use the following approximation: Ground resolution ≈ (UAS altitude x 180) / (π x Field of view x Camera resolution). 
Thus, Ground resolution ≈ (500 m x 180) / (π x 60 x 10,000,000) ≈ 4.77 cm/pixel. **UAS Sensor Integration**: A UAS is equipped with a multispectral sensor that can capture images in 4 spectral bands: blue, green, red, and near-infrared. If the sensor has a spatial resolution of 10 cm and a spectral resolution of 10 nm, what is the total data rate required to transmit the images in real-time? Since there are 4 spectral bands, the total number of pixels per image is: 1,000,000 pixels * 4 = 4,000,000 pixels. Assuming 12-bit resolution per pixel, the total data rate required is: 4,000,000 pixels * 12 bits/pixel * 1 frame/second = 48,000,000 bits/second = 48 Mbps.

3. **UAS Sensor Payload**: A UAS is equipped with a camera payload that has a resolution of 10 megapixels and a field of view of 50 degrees. If the UAS operates at an altitude of 500 m, what is the ground resolution of the camera? 
Given: Camera resolution = 10 megapixels, Field of view = 50 degrees, UAS altitude = 500 m. 
First, calculate the ground resolution: Ground resolution = (UAS altitude x Field of view) / Camera resolution. 
Assuming a 4:3 aspect ratio for the camera, the number of pixels per meter is: Pixels per meter = sqrt(10,000,000 pixels / (4/3)) / (500 m x tan(25 degrees)) = 12.2 pixels/m. 
Then, the ground resolution is: Ground resolution = 1 / 12.2 pixels/m = 0.082 m/pixel.

If the UAS is equipped with a camera that can capture images at a rate of 1 frame per second, and the operator wants to achieve a ground sampling distance (GSD) of 5 cm, what is the required flying height? 
First, calculate the total number of images required: 10,000 m² / (0.05 m * 0.05 m) = 4,000,000 images. Then, calculate the total flying time required: 4,000,000 images / 1 image/second = 11.1 hours. Since the UAS endurance is 2 hours, multiple flights will be required. To calculate the required flying height, use the formula: flying height = (GSD * focal length) / pixel size. Assuming a focal length of 4 mm and a pixel size of 3.2 μm, the required flying height is: flying height = (0.05 m * 4 mm) / 3.2 μm = 62.5 m.

2. **UAS Performance Calculation**: A fixed-wing UAS has a mass of 20 kg, a wing area of 1 m², and a wing aspect ratio of 6. If the UAS is cruising at 100 km/h at an altitude of 1000 m, what is its lift-to-drag ratio? 
First, calculate the air density at 1000 m: ρ = 1.225 kg/m³ * (1 - 0.0000225577 * 1000) ^ 5.2551 = 1.112 kg/m³. Then, calculate the lift force: L = 0.5 * ρ * V² * Cl * A, where Cl is the lift coefficient. Assuming Cl = 1, L = 0.5 * 1.112 kg/m³ * (100 km/h * 1000 m/km / 3600 s/h)² * 1 * 1 m² = 192.5 N. The weight of the UAS is: W = m * g = 20 kg * 9.81 m/s² = 196.2 N. Since L = W, the UAS is in steady flight. The drag force can be calculated using the formula: D = 0.5 * ρ * V² * Cd * A, where Cd is the drag coefficient. Assuming Cd = 0.05, D = 0.5 * 1.112 kg/m³ * (100 km/h * 1000 m/km / 3600 s/h)² * 0.05 * 1 m² = 9.625 N. The lift-to-drag ratio is: L/D = 192.5 N / 9.625 N = 20.

## Applications

Unmanned Aerial Systems (UAS) have numerous applications in the aviation aerospace domain, including aerial surveillance, inspection, and monitoring. In practice, UAS are used for border patrol and surveillance, where they can provide real-time video feed and detect intrusions. They are also used for inspection of critical infrastructure such as pipelines, bridges, and transmission lines, allowing for quick and efficient identification of potential issues. Additionally, UAS are used in search and rescue operations, disaster response, and environmental monitoring, such as tracking wildfires, monitoring wildlife populations, and detecting oil spills. In the military domain, UAS are used for reconnaissance, combat, and logistics support. Furthermore, UAS are being used in the commercial sector for package delivery, aerial photography, and precision agriculture, where they can be used to monitor crop health and apply targeted fertilizers and pesticides. The use of UAS in these applications is governed by regulations such as those set by the Federal Aviation Administration (FAA) in the United States, which dictate rules for safe operation, airspace management, and pilot certification. ## Common Errors

In the operation and management of Unmanned Aerial Systems (UAS), several common errors are prevalent among practitioners. One of the primary mistakes is the failure to conduct thorough pre-flight checks, which can lead to system failures and accidents. This oversight often stems from inadequate training or complacency, highlighting the importance of rigorous adherence to standard operating procedures. Another error is the misinterpretation of weather conditions, which can significantly impact UAS performance and safety. Practitioners must accurately assess weather factors such as wind, visibility, and precipitation to ensure safe and efficient operation. Additionally, the incorrect configuration of UAS components, such as autopilot systems and sensors, can result in reduced system performance or complete failure. This emphasizes the need for meticulous attention to detail during the setup and calibration of UAS. Furthermore, the lack of situational awareness and failure to maintain line-of-sight with the UAS can lead to loss of control or collisions with obstacles. This underscores the importance of maintaining a safe distance and being aware of the surroundings during UAS operation. Lastly, non-compliance with regulatory requirements, such as flying in restricted airspace or exceeding altitude limits, can result in legal consequences and compromise safety. It is essential for practitioners to stay informed about relevant regulations and adhere to them strictly. By understanding and addressing these common errors, UAS operators can minimize risks and ensure safe and effective operation.

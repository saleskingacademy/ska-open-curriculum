# Nft Digital Assets

> The course assumes basic knowledge of economics and finance, and builds on foundational concepts to explain the principles of NFT digital assets.

## Nft Basics

Non-Fungible Token — unique digital asset on blockchain proving ownership/authenticity. Unlike cryptocurrency (fungible — one Bitcoin = any other Bitcoin), each NFT is distinct. ERC-721 standard (Ethereum). TECHNOLOGY: Smart contracts (self-executing code on blockchain), metadata (points to content — image, video, music), IPFS/Arweave (decentralized storage), royalties (creator gets % on secondary sales — programmatic). HISTORY: CryptoPunks (2017, Larva Labs — 10,000 generative pixel art), CryptoKitties (2017 — clogged Ethereum), Beeple "Everydays" ($69.3M at Christie's March 2021 — third-most expensive living artist). 2021 boom: $25B market. 2022-23 crash: 95%+ value decline. CATEGORIES: Art (generative, 1/1), Collectibles (PFP projects — BAYC, Azuki), Music (Royal, Sound.xyz — fractional ownership), Gaming (in-game assets, Axie Infinity), Virtual real estate (Decentraland, The Sandbox), Domain names (ENS), Ticketing, Identity/credentials. CRITICISM: Environmental (proof-of-work energy — largely addressed by Ethereum's merge to proof-of-stake, Sept 2022, 99.95% energy reduction). Speculation/scams (rug pulls, wash trading). Copyright confusion (owning NFT ≠ owning copyright). Market manipulation. RIGHT-CLICK-SAVE critique — confuses file with provenance. REAL VALUE: Digital provenance, creator royalties, programmable ownership, interoperability across platforms. APPLIED: NFT technology (not necessarily JPEGs) will likely underpin digital identity, ticketing, real estate records, supply chain verification. The infrastructure matters more than the speculation.

## Foundations

In the context of economics and finance, NFT Digital Assets refer to unique, non-interchangeable digital tokens stored on a blockchain, a decentralized, distributed ledger technology. A **blockchain** is a digital ledger that records transactions across a network of computers, allowing for secure, transparent, and tamper-proof data storage. **NFTs** (Non-Fungible Tokens) are distinct from **fungible tokens**, which are interchangeable and have identical value, such as cryptocurrencies like Bitcoin. NFTs are created through a process called **minting**, where a digital asset is tokenized and assigned a unique identifier on the blockchain. **Tokenization** is the process of converting a physical or digital asset into a digital token, representing ownership or rights to the underlying asset. **Digital assets** refer to intangible assets that exist in digital form, such as images, videos, music, or collectibles. In the context of NFTs, digital assets are often **rare** or **scarce**, meaning their supply is limited, which can contribute to their value. **Smart contracts**, self-executing contracts with the terms of the agreement written directly into lines of code, are often used to facilitate the creation, buying, and selling of NFTs, ensuring secure and automated transactions. Understanding these core definitions and concepts is essential for practitioners in the field of economics and finance to navigate the emerging market of NFT digital assets. A **non-fungible token (NFT)** is a digital asset that is distinguishable from others due to its unique characteristics, such as a specific identifier, scarcity, or provenance. **Digital goods** are intangible items that exist only in electronic form and can be stored, transferred, and traded digitally. **Ownership** in this context refers to the exclusive right to control, use, and transfer the digital asset. Key concepts include **tokenization**, the process of converting a digital good into a unique digital token, and **blockchain**, a decentralized, distributed ledger technology that enables secure, transparent, and tamper-proof recording of NFT transactions. **Smart contracts**, self-executing contracts with the terms of the agreement written directly into lines of code, are often used to facilitate NFT transactions and ensure ownership transfer. A **wallet** is a software program that allows users to store, send, and receive NFTs, while a **marketplace** is a platform that enables buyers and sellers to trade NFTs. Understanding these core definitions and principles is essential for practitioners to navigate the economics and finance of NFT digital assets.

## Methods And Frameworks

In the context of NFT digital assets, several methods and frameworks are employed to evaluate and manage their economic and financial aspects. The Capital Asset Pricing Model (CAPM) is used to assess the risk and expected return of NFT investments, taking into account the asset's beta and the market's risk premium. The Arbitrage Pricing Theory (APT) is applied to identify mispricings in NFT markets, exploiting deviations from equilibrium prices. The Black-Scholes model is utilized to value NFT options and other derivatives, considering factors such as volatility and time to expiration. The Gordon Growth Model is used to estimate the present value of future cash flows generated by NFTs, assuming a constant growth rate. Each of these models has its failure mode, such as CAPM's assumption of a single-factor market, APT's reliance on accurate factor identification, Black-Scholes' simplification of complex market dynamics, and Gordon Growth Model's sensitivity to growth rate estimates. Additionally, the Efficient Market Hypothesis (EMH) framework is used to analyze NFT market efficiency, with its three forms (weak, semi-strong, and strong) providing insights into the incorporation of information into prices. The Modern Portfolio Theory (MPT) framework is applied to optimize NFT portfolio diversification, minimizing risk while maximizing returns.

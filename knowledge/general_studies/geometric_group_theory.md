# Geometric Group Theory

> The course assumes significant prior knowledge of algebraic properties of groups, geometric topology, and combinatorial techniques.

## Foundations

Geometric Group Theory (GGT) studies finitely generated groups via the geometry of metric spaces on which they act, primarily through the Cayley graph construction and quasi-isometric invariants. Rooted in the interplay between algebraic properties of groups and geometric/topological structures, GGT leverages coarse geometry, hyperbolic geometry, and combinatorial techniques to classify groups up to quasi-isometry and understand their large-scale geometry. The foundational principle is that finitely generated groups can be endowed with word metrics derived from finite generating sets, turning algebraic problems into geometric ones. Key early results include Milnor–Švarc lemma, which relates group actions on proper geodesic metric spaces to quasi-isometries, and Gromov’s seminal theorem characterizing groups of polynomial growth as virtually nilpotent.

Geometric Group Theory is a branch of mathematics that studies the interplay between geometry and group theory. A **group** is a set G equipped with a binary operation (often called multiplication) that satisfies four axioms: **closure**, meaning the result of combining any two elements is also an element of G; **associativity**, meaning the order in which elements are combined does not change the result; **identity**, meaning there exists an element e in G such that combining e with any element a in G leaves a unchanged; and **invertibility**, meaning for each element a in G, there exists an element b in G such that combining a and b yields the identity element e.

A **finitely generated group** is a group that can be generated by a finite set of elements, called **generators**. The **Cayley graph** of a group G with respect to a set of generators S is a graph whose vertices are the elements of G, and two vertices are connected by an edge if the corresponding elements differ by a generator or its inverse. The **word metric** on a group G with respect to a set of generators S is a metric that assigns to each pair of elements the minimum number of generators or their inverses needed to express one element in terms of the other.

A **geodesic** in a Cayley graph is a path that realizes the word metric between its endpoints. A group is **finite** if it has a finite number of elements, and **infinite** otherwise. The **order** of a group is the number of elements it contains. A **subgroup** is a subset of a group that is itself a group under the same operation. These foundational concepts form the basis of Geometric Group Theory, allowing for the study of groups through their geometric and combinatorial properties.

## Section

Cayley Graphs and Word Metrics  
Given a finitely generated group \( G \) with finite symmetric generating set \( S \), the Cayley graph \(\Gamma(G,S)\) is a 1-complex with vertex set \( G \) and edges \(\{(g,gs) : g \in G, s \in S\}\). Equipped with the graph metric (edges length 1), \(\Gamma(G,S)\) is a proper geodesic metric space on which \( G \) acts freely and transitively by left multiplication. The word metric \( d_S(g,h) = \min \{ n : g^{-1}h = s_1 \cdots s_n, s_i \in S \} \) coincides with the graph distance. Central to GGT is the quasi-isometry class of \(\Gamma(G,S)\), which is independent of the finite generating set \( S \). This construction enables algebraic properties to be studied via geometric invariants.

Quasi-Isometries and Coarse Geometry  
A map \( f : X \to Y \) between metric spaces is a \((\lambda, \epsilon)\)-quasi-isometric embedding if for all \( x,x' \in X \),  
\[
\frac{1}{\lambda} d_X(x,x') - \epsilon \le d_Y(f(x), f(x')) \le \lambda d_X(x,x') + \epsilon,
\]  
and a quasi-isometry if \( f(X) \) is \(\epsilon\)-dense in \( Y \). Quasi-isometries preserve large-scale geometry and are the equivalence relation for classifying groups in GGT. The Milnor–Švarc lemma states: if \( G \) acts properly, cocompactly, and isometrically on a proper geodesic metric space \( X \), then \( G \) is finitely generated and quasi-isometric to \( X \). This bridges algebraic group theory and geometric topology.

Hyperbolic Groups and Gromov’s δ-Hyperbolicity  
A geodesic metric space \( X \) is \(\delta\)-hyperbolic (Gromov hyperbolic) if for any geodesic triangle, each side lies within a \(\delta\)-neighborhood of the union of the other two sides. A finitely generated group \( G \) is hyperbolic if its Cayley graph is \(\delta\)-hyperbolic for some \(\delta\). Hyperbolic groups exhibit linear isoperimetric inequalities, exponential growth, and have well-defined boundaries at infinity \(\partial G\). Key tools include the slim triangle property, the Gromov product  
\[
(x|y)_w = \frac{1}{2}(d(w,x) + d(w,y) - d(x,y)),
\]  
and the boundary topology defined via equivalence classes of geodesic rays. Examples: free groups \( F_n \), fundamental groups of closed hyperbolic manifolds.

Dehn Functions and Isoperimetric Inequalities  
The Dehn function \( \delta_G(n) \) measures the difficulty of filling loops of length \( n \) in the Cayley complex of \( G \) with discs. Formally, for a finite presentation \( \langle S | R \rangle \), \(\delta_G(n)\) is the minimal area of van Kampen diagrams filling loops of length at most \( n \). Hyperbolic groups have linear Dehn functions, while nilpotent groups have polynomial Dehn functions of degree related to their nilpotency class. Dehn functions classify groups by their algorithmic complexity of the word problem and geometric filling properties.

Bass–Serre Theory and Group Actions on Trees  
Bass–Serre theory studies groups acting on simplicial trees, encoding splittings of groups as amalgamated free products or HNN extensions. Given a group \( G \) acting without edge inversions on a tree \( T \), the quotient graph of groups decomposition yields a fundamental group isomorphic to \( G \). This framework provides algebraic decompositions and geometric insights, e.g., the structure theorem for free products with amalgamation:  
\[
G = A *_C B,
\]  
where \( C \leq A,B \). Bass–Serre theory underpins JSJ decompositions and accessibility results.

CAT(0) Spaces and Non-Positive Curvature  
A CAT(0) space is a geodesic metric space where geodesic triangles are thinner than or equal to their comparison triangles in Euclidean space. Groups acting geometrically (properly, cocompactly, isometrically) on CAT(0) spaces are CAT(0) groups, generalizing non-positively curved manifolds. The Flat Torus Theorem guarantees existence of flat subspaces corresponding to abelian subgroups. CAT(0) cube complexes, introduced by Sageev, provide combinatorial models for many groups including right-angled Artin groups (RAAGs) and Coxeter groups, enabling powerful cubulation techniques.

Boundaries and Rigidity Phenomena  
Boundaries at infinity \(\partial G\) encode asymptotic geometry of hyperbolic and CAT(0) groups. For hyperbolic groups, \(\partial G\) is a compact, metrizable space with a canonical quasi-conformal structure. Rigidity theorems (Mostow, Bourdon–Pajot) establish that quasi-isometries induce boundary homeomorphisms, often implying algebraic isomorphisms. These boundaries facilitate classification and quasi-isometric rigidity results, e.g., hyperbolic lattices in rank-one symmetric spaces.

## Mechanisms

Geometric Group Theory operates through several key mechanisms that enable the study of groups using geometric and topological techniques. The process begins with the choice of a group, often denoted as G, and a metric space, X, on which G acts. This action is typically by isometries (distance-preserving transformations), which allows for the preservation of geometric properties. The Cayley graph of G with respect to a generating set S is a fundamental tool, where vertices represent group elements and edges connect elements that differ by an element of S. This graph provides a geometric representation of the group's structure.

The action of G on X induces a quotient space, X/G, which can be used to study the group's properties. The quotient map from X to X/G is a crucial step, as it allows for the passage from the geometry of the space X to the algebraic properties of G. The mechanism of covering space theory also plays a significant role, where the universal cover of X/G can be used to reconstruct the group G.

Furthermore, the concept of a group's deformation space, which parameterizes the possible actions of G on X, is essential in understanding the geometric and topological constraints imposed on the group. The mechanisms of Geometric Group Theory, including the use of Gromov-hyperbolic spaces, quasi-isometries, and the study of group boundaries, provide a comprehensive framework for analyzing the interplay between geometric and algebraic properties of groups.

Through these mechanisms, Geometric Group Theory provides a powerful approach to studying groups, enabling the application of geometric and topological techniques to problems in abstract algebra and vice versa, thus deepening our understanding of the intricate relationships between geometry, topology, and algebra.

## Methods And Frameworks

Geometric Group Theory employs various methods and frameworks to study the interplay between geometric and algebraic properties of groups. The Dehn-Nielsen-Baer theorem provides a framework for understanding the relationship between a group's geometry and its action on a space. The Cayley graph, a combinatorial object, is used to visualize and analyze the structure of a group, with its application depending on the group's presentation. 
The word metric, induced by a finite generating set, allows for the study of a group's large-scale geometry, while the concept of quasi-isometry provides a way to compare the geometry of different spaces. 
The Bass-Serre theory, which involves the use of trees and graphs of groups, is utilized to study the structure of groups acting on trees, with its failure mode often related to the complexity of the tree or the group action. 
The Bestvina-Feighn combination theorem provides a framework for combining the geometry of subgroups to understand the overall geometry of a group, with its application limited by the requirement of a malnormal subgroup. 
The Gromov-hyperbolicity model, characterized by the thin triangle condition, is used to study groups with negative curvature properties, with its failure mode often related to the presence of flat subgroups or other non-hyperbolic behavior. 
The JSJ decomposition, which involves the decomposition of a group along a set of canonical subgroups, is utilized to study the structure of groups with a more complex geometry, with its application depending on the group's JSJ tree. 
Each of these methods and frameworks has its own strengths and limitations, and the choice of which to use depends on the specific properties of the group being studied.

## Worked Examples

To illustrate key concepts in Geometric Group Theory, consider the following examples. 
1. Let G be the group of isometries of the Euclidean plane, and let H be the subgroup of translations. The Cayley graph of H with respect to the generators {τ₁, τ₂}, where τ₁ and τ₂ are translations by one unit in the x and y directions respectively, is a grid. The word length of an element h = τ₁^a τ₂^b in H is given by ℓ(h) = |a| + |b|, which represents the distance from the origin to the point (a, b) in the grid.
2. The fundamental group π₁(S) of a surface S, such as a torus or a sphere, can be studied using Geometric Group Theory. For example, the fundamental group of a torus is isomorphic to ℤ², which can be represented as a Cayley graph with two loops, one for each generator. The word problem for this group can be solved by reducing words to their normal form, which represents the coordinates of a point on the torus.
3. Let G be the group SL(2, ℤ) of 2x2 matrices with integer entries and determinant 1. The Cayley graph of G with respect to the generators {A, B}, where A = [1, 1; 0, 1] and B = [1, 0; 1, 1], can be used to study the geometry of the group. The word length of an element g = A^a B^b in G is related to the norm of the matrix, which represents the distance from the origin to the point in the hyperbolic plane represented by g.

## Applications

Geometric Group Theory has numerous applications in mathematics and computer science. In topology, it is used to study the fundamental groups of spaces and the properties of covering spaces. The theory of ends, developed by Freudenthal and Hopf, is a fundamental concept in geometric group theory, which has applications in the study of infinite groups and their actions on spaces. In geometric topology, geometric group theory is used to study the properties of hyperbolic 3-manifolds and the geometry of knot complements. In computer science, geometric group theory is used in the study of algorithms for solving the word problem and the conjugacy problem in finitely generated groups. The theory of automatic groups, developed by Epstein and Thurston, uses geometric group theory to study the properties of groups with finite presentations and has applications in computer science and cryptography. Additionally, geometric group theory has connections to number theory, particularly in the study of arithmetic groups and their actions on symmetric spaces. The study of group actions on trees, developed by Bass and Serre, has applications in algebra and geometry, and is used to study the properties of graphs and their automorphism groups.

## Common Errors

In Geometric Group Theory, several common mistakes can lead to incorrect conclusions. One such error is confusing the notions of quasi-isometry and isometry. Quasi-isometry is a weaker notion that allows for a bounded distortion in the metric, whereas isometry requires a precise preservation of distances. Practitioners may mistakenly assume that quasi-isometric groups are isomorphic, which is not necessarily true. Another error is neglecting the distinction between a group's Cayley graph and its geometric realization, leading to incorrect interpretations of the group's geometric properties. Additionally, some may incorrectly apply the Svarc-Milnor lemma, which states that a group acting properly discontinuously and cocompactly on a geodesic metric space is quasi-isometric to the space, without verifying the necessary conditions. Furthermore, the mistake of assuming that all finitely generated groups have a well-defined geometric boundary can lead to incorrect conclusions about the group's behavior at infinity. These errors often arise from a lack of careful consideration of the definitions and hypotheses underlying the theorems and techniques used in Geometric Group Theory.

## Advanced

Geometric Group Theory has undergone significant developments in recent years, with various extensions and open questions driving current research. One key area of study is the theory of Out(Fn), the outer automorphism group of a free group, which has connections to the study of mapping class groups and the geometry of moduli spaces. The work of Culler and Vogtmann on Outer Space, a contractible space with a proper action of Out(Fn), has been particularly influential. Another active area of research is the study of CAT(0) groups, which are groups that act geometrically on CAT(0) spaces, and their relationship to the theory of non-positive curvature. The notion of hierarchically hyperbolic groups, introduced by Behrstock, Hagen, and Sisto, provides a framework for studying groups with a more complex geometric structure. Open questions in the field include the conjecture that all hyperbolic groups are residually finite, and the study of the asymptotic properties of group invariants, such as the growth rate of the number of subgroups of a given index. Researchers are also exploring the connections between Geometric Group Theory and other areas of mathematics, such as Teichmüller theory, low-dimensional topology, and geometric analysis. The development of new tools and techniques, such as the use of machine learning and computational methods, is also an area of ongoing research, with potential applications to problems in cryptography, coding theory, and network analysis.

# Media Buying

> The course teaches applied practice of media buying, assuming foundational knowledge of marketing and advertising concepts.

## Overview

Professional discipline for the amplify specialist: media buying. Applied practice used to generate revenue.

## Foundations

Media buying refers to the process of purchasing advertising space or time in various media channels, such as television, radio, print, outdoor, or digital media. A **media channel** is a platform that delivers content to an audience, whereas **advertising space** or **time** refers to the specific slot or placement where an advertisement is displayed or broadcast. **Media planning** is the strategic process of selecting the most effective media channels to achieve an advertiser's goals, considering factors such as **target audience**, **reach**, **frequency**, and **cost**. The **target audience** is the specific group of people an advertiser aims to reach with their message. **Reach** refers to the number of unique individuals exposed to an advertisement, while **frequency** is the number of times an individual is exposed to the same advertisement. **Cost** encompasses the financial expenditure associated with purchasing media space or time, including **media rates**, which are the prices charged by media owners for advertising space or time. Understanding these core definitions and principles is essential for a media buying practitioner to effectively navigate the complex media landscape and make informed decisions. A media buyer is a professional responsible for planning, negotiating, and purchasing media inventory, which is the available space or time for advertising in a particular medium. The goal of media buying is to reach a target audience with a specific message, known as an advertisement or ad, at the most effective cost. Key terms include: 
- Impressions: the number of times an ad is displayed or heard
- Reach: the number of unique individuals exposed to an ad
- Frequency: the average number of times an individual is exposed to an ad
- Gross Rating Points (GRPs): a measure of the total audience reached by an ad, calculated by multiplying reach by frequency
- Cost Per Thousand (CPM): the cost of reaching 1,000 individuals with an ad, used to compare the efficiency of different media channels.

Key terms include: 
- **Rating points (RPs)**: a measure of the percentage of a target audience that is exposed to an advertisement, with one rating point equal to one percent of the target audience. 
- **Impressions**: the number of times an advertisement is displayed or heard, regardless of whether the viewer or listener is part of the target audience. 
- **Cost per thousand (CPM)**: a pricing model that calculates the cost of displaying an advertisement to one thousand people, often used in digital media buying. 
- **Gross rating points (GRPs)**: the total rating points achieved by an advertisement or campaign, calculated by multiplying the rating points of each individual advertisement by the number of times it is aired. 
- **Media plan**: a document that outlines the media buying strategy, including the target audience, media channels, and budget allocation. 
- **Media mix**: the combination of media channels used to achieve the marketing objectives, such as television, radio, and digital media. 
Understanding these core definitions and principles is essential for effective media buying and planning.

## Mechanisms

Media buying involves a series of steps that facilitate the purchase of advertising space or time from media owners. The process begins with planning and research, where advertisers or their agencies identify target audiences and select appropriate media channels to reach them. This is followed by the development of a media plan, which outlines the specific media vehicles to be used, the frequency and duration of advertisements, and the budget allocated to each channel. 
The next step involves the issuance of a request for proposal (RFP) to media owners or their representatives, who then provide proposals outlining the available inventory, pricing, and any additional value-added services. Advertisers or their agencies then evaluate these proposals, considering factors such as audience reach, cost, and the potential return on investment (ROI). 
Once a media owner is selected, the advertiser or agency negotiates the terms of the purchase, including the price, ad placement, and any performance guarantees. This is typically formalized through an insertion order, which serves as a contract between the parties. 
The media owner then provides the advertiser with ad specifications and any necessary technical requirements, and the advertiser supplies the ad creative and targeting instructions. The media owner is responsible for delivering the ad to the target audience, and the advertiser monitors the campaign's performance, using metrics such as impressions, clicks, and conversions to assess its effectiveness. 
Throughout the process, advertisers and media owners may utilize various technologies, including ad exchanges, supply-side platforms, and demand-side platforms, to facilitate the buying and selling of media inventory. These platforms enable real-time bidding, automated ad placement, and enhanced targeting capabilities, increasing the efficiency and effectiveness of media buying.

The media buying process involves a series of steps that facilitate the purchase of ad space from media owners. It begins with planning, where advertisers define their target audience, campaign objectives, and budget. Next, media buyers conduct research to identify suitable media channels, such as television, radio, print, or digital platforms, that align with the target audience and campaign goals. 
The buyer then negotiates the ad space price with the media owner, considering factors like ad format, placement, and timing. Upon agreement, the buyer secures the ad space through a purchase order or insertion order, which outlines the terms of the agreement, including ad specifications, run dates, and costs. 
The causal chain is as follows: the advertiser's campaign objectives and budget determine the media channel selection, which in turn affects the ad space price negotiation. The negotiated price and secured ad space then influence the ad's potential reach and impact, ultimately driving the campaign's return on investment (ROI). 
Throughout this process, media buyers must balance factors like ad visibility, audience engagement, and cost efficiency to optimize the campaign's performance. By understanding these mechanisms, advertisers can make informed decisions to maximize their media buying efforts and achieve their marketing goals.

## Methods And Frameworks

Media buying utilizes various methods, models, and formulas to optimize advertising campaigns. The Gross Rating Point (GRP) method measures the total audience reached by an ad, calculated as the sum of ratings across different media platforms. Use GRP when evaluating campaign reach and frequency. However, its failure mode lies in not accounting for audience duplication and potential overexposure. 
The Cost Per Thousand (CPM) model calculates the cost of reaching 1,000 people, useful for comparing ad prices across platforms. Apply CPM when evaluating ad efficiency, but be aware of its limitation in not considering ad engagement or conversion rates. 
The Effective Reach model estimates the percentage of the target audience exposed to an ad at least once, useful for planning campaigns with specific reach and frequency goals. Use Effective Reach when aiming for optimal ad exposure, but beware of its assumption that all exposures have equal impact. 
The Recency planning model prioritizes reaching consumers close to the point of purchase, useful for driving immediate sales. Apply Recency when targeting consumers with a short purchase cycle, but be cautious of its potential to overlook long-term brand building. 
The RFM (Recency, Frequency, Monetary) analysis evaluates customer behavior based on purchase recency, frequency, and monetary value, useful for identifying high-value customers. Use RFM when segmenting audiences and tailoring ad messages, but be aware of its reliance on accurate customer data. 
Each method and model has its strengths and weaknesses, and media buyers must carefully select and combine them to achieve campaign objectives. Use this model when aiming for broad awareness, but beware of its failure mode in potentially overestimating actual reach due to audience duplication. The Recency theory prioritizes ad exposure closest to the purchase decision, useful for campaigns with short sales cycles. Apply recency when targeting immediate conversions, but be cautious of its limitation in not considering long-term brand building. The Frequency theory aims to optimize the number of ad exposures per user, balancing reach and frequency. Use frequency when seeking to maximize ad recall, but be aware of its failure mode in potential ad fatigue and decreased effectiveness. Use Effective Reach when optimizing campaign frequency and reach, but note its failure mode: assuming a uniform frequency distribution. The Recency theory prioritizes ads seen closest to the purchase decision, emphasizing the importance of timing. Apply Recency when targeting customers in the decision-making phase, but be cautious of its failure mode: overemphasizing short-term conversions. Use RFM when segmenting audiences, but be aware of its failure mode: not accounting for external factors influencing purchase behavior. Each method and model has its strengths and limitations, and media buyers must consider these factors when selecting the most suitable approach for their campaign goals.

## Worked Examples

To illustrate the media buying process, consider the following examples. 
1. A company wants to advertise on a local radio station with a audience of 100,000 listeners, and a cost per spot of $50. If the company wants to achieve a frequency of 3.5, and the station offers a discount of 10% for bulk purchases, how much will the company pay for a 4-week campaign with 5 spots per week? 
First, calculate the total number of spots required: 3.5 frequency * 4 weeks * 100,000 listeners / 100,000 listeners per spot = 140 spots. 
Then, calculate the cost per spot with the discount: $50 * 0.9 = $45. 
Finally, calculate the total cost: 140 spots * $45 per spot = $6,300. 
2. An online publisher offers a cost per thousand impressions (CPM) of $5. If a company wants to reach 500,000 people with a 2% click-through rate, and the publisher offers a 15% discount for purchases over $1,000, how much will the company pay for the campaign? 
First, calculate the total number of impressions required: 500,000 people * 1,000 impressions / 1,000 people = 500,000 impressions. 
Then, calculate the cost of the impressions: 500,000 impressions * $5 CPM / 1,000 = $2,500. 
Finally, apply the discount: $2,500 * 0.85 = $2,125. 
3. A TV station offers a 30-second spot for $1,000, with a rating of 5 and a share of 10. If a company wants to achieve a reach of 70% of the target audience, with a frequency of 2.5, and the station offers a package deal of 10 spots for $9,000, is the package deal a better option? 
First, calculate the total number of spots required: 2.5 frequency * 70% reach * 100,000 viewers / (5 rating * 10 share) = 35 spots. 
Then, calculate the cost per spot: $1,000 per spot. 
Finally, compare the cost of the required spots to the package deal: 35 spots * $1,000 per spot = $35,000, which is more than the package deal of $9,000 for 10 spots, indicating the package deal is a better option. However, since this calculation does not account for the actual reach and frequency of the ads, a more realistic approach would involve considering the station's audience duplication and the company's budget. 
2. An online advertising campaign aims to deliver 1 million impressions across a network of websites with an average cost per thousand impressions (CPM) of $5. If the campaign's budget is $5,000, the maximum number of impressions that can be delivered is: $5,000 / ($5 CPM) = $5,000 / $5 = 1,000 CPMs, which translates to 1,000 * 1,000 = 1,000,000 impressions. 
3. A media buyer is tasked with purchasing TV ad space for a new product launch. The target audience is women aged 25-54, and the buyer has a budget of $10,000. The available ad slots are: a daytime TV show with a rating of 2.5 and a cost of $1,500 per 30-second ad, and a primetime TV show with a rating of 5.0 and a cost of $3,000 per 30-second ad. To maximize reach, the buyer should consider the cost per rating point (CPP) for each option: daytime CPP = $1,500 / 2.5 = $600, primetime CPP = $3,000 / 5.0 = $600. Given the equal CPP, the buyer may choose to allocate the budget across both options to achieve a balanced reach and frequency.

To illustrate the principles of media buying, consider the following examples. 
1. A company wants to advertise on a local radio station with a weekly audience of 100,000 listeners. The station offers a 30-second ad slot for $500. If the company's goal is to reach 80% of the audience at least once, and assuming each ad exposure reaches 20% of the audience, the total number of ad slots required can be calculated as follows: 
Total ad slots = (Desired reach / Ad exposure per slot) = (0.8 / 0.2) = 4 slots. 
The total cost would be 4 * $500 = $2000. 
2. An online publisher offers a cost-per-thousand impressions (CPM) of $10 for a banner ad. If a company wants to reach 500,000 people and assumes an average of 2 ad exposures per person, the total number of impressions required is 500,000 * 2 = 1,000,000. 
The total cost would be (1,000,000 / 1000) * $10 = $10,000. 
3. A TV station offers a 30-second ad slot during a prime-time show for $10,000, with a potential audience of 1,000,000 viewers. If the company's goal is to achieve a frequency of 3 (i.e., each viewer sees the ad 3 times), and assuming each ad exposure reaches 50% of the audience, the total number of ad slots required can be calculated as follows: 
Total ad slots = (Desired frequency / Ad exposure per slot) = (3 / 0.5) = 6 slots. 
The total cost would be 6 * $10,000 = $60,000.

## Applications

Media buying is utilized in various industries, including advertising, marketing, and public relations. In practice, media buyers analyze target audience demographics, preferences, and behaviors to determine the most effective media channels for reaching them. This involves assessing the reach, frequency, and impact of different media platforms, such as television, radio, print, digital, and outdoor advertising. Media buyers also consider factors like ad placement, ad format, and timing to optimize campaign performance. For instance, a media buyer for a retail company may purchase ad space on social media platforms during peak shopping seasons to target consumers who are likely to make purchases. Additionally, media buyers use data and analytics tools to track campaign metrics, such as click-through rates, conversion rates, and return on investment (ROI), to evaluate the effectiveness of their media buying strategies and make data-driven decisions for future campaigns. In digital media buying, techniques like programmatic buying, real-time bidding, and private marketplaces are used to automate and streamline the ad buying process, allowing for more precise targeting and increased efficiency. For instance, a media buyer for a fashion brand may purchase ad space in fashion magazines, online lifestyle websites, and social media platforms to reach their target audience of young adults. In television advertising, media buyers consider factors such as program ratings, audience composition, and ad placement to optimize the reach and frequency of their ads. In digital media buying, they use data and analytics tools to target specific audiences, track ad performance, and adjust their campaigns accordingly. Media buyers also negotiate ad rates, placements, and added-value promotions with media vendors to ensure the best possible return on investment for their clients. Additionally, they must stay up-to-date with industry trends, such as the rise of programmatic advertising, to effectively navigate the complex media landscape and achieve their campaign objectives.

## Common Errors

A key error is failing to set clear campaign objectives, leading to ineffective metrics and poor optimization. Another mistake is inadequate audience targeting, resulting from insufficient data analysis or reliance on broad demographics rather than nuanced behavioral insights. Overemphasis on cost per thousand impressions (CPM) as the primary metric can also lead to suboptimal campaign performance, as it neglects other crucial factors such as ad viewability, click-through rates, and conversion rates. Furthermore, neglecting to regularly monitor and adjust campaign bids, budgets, and ad creatives can cause campaigns to stagnate or underperform. Additionally, insufficient consideration of ad placement and contextual relevance can result in wasted impressions and poor brand alignment. Lastly, not accounting for cross-device and cross-channel user behavior can lead to overcounting or undercounting of campaign reach and frequency, ultimately affecting campaign efficacy. Overreliance on a single metric, such as click-through rate (CTR), can also be misleading, as it neglects other crucial indicators like conversion rates and return on ad spend (ROAS). Furthermore, inadequate consideration of ad placement and context can lead to ad waste and decreased campaign effectiveness. Additionally, not regularly monitoring and optimizing campaigns can result in suboptimal performance and inefficient use of budget. Practitioners must also avoid overvaluing reach and undervaluing frequency, as well as failing to account for ad fraud and viewability issues, which can significantly impact campaign ROI. By understanding these common errors, media buyers can refine their strategies and improve campaign outcomes.

The mistakes practitioners actually make in media buying often stem from a lack of understanding of the target audience, inadequate research, and poor campaign optimization. One common error is failing to set clear, measurable objectives for the campaign, leading to difficulty in evaluating its success. Another mistake is not considering the audience's media consumption habits, resulting in misplaced ad spend. For example, allocating a large budget to print media when the target audience is more active online. Additionally, practitioners often neglect to monitor and adjust their campaigns in real-time, failing to capitalize on opportunities and mitigate potential losses. Overreliance on a single metric, such as click-through rate (CTR), can also lead to misguided decisions, as it does not provide a comprehensive picture of the campaign's performance. Furthermore, not accounting for ad fraud and viewability issues can result in wasted budget and inaccurate metrics. Lastly, insufficient consideration of the customer journey and the role of each media channel within it can lead to inefficient allocation of resources and a lack of cohesion across the campaign.

## Advanced

The field of media buying is evolving rapidly, driven by technological advancements, changing consumer behaviors, and the increasing importance of data-driven decision-making. At the graduate level, media buying extends into advanced topics such as programmatic buying, real-time bidding, and the use of artificial intelligence (AI) and machine learning (ML) algorithms to optimize media campaigns. Open questions in the field include the balance between human judgment and algorithmic decision-making, the role of transparency and accountability in programmatic buying, and the impact of ad blocking and viewability on media buying strategies. Furthermore, the integration of media buying with other marketing disciplines, such as data analytics and customer experience management, is becoming increasingly important. The field is moving towards greater emphasis on attribution modeling, cross-device targeting, and the use of advanced data management platforms (DMPs) to manage and activate large datasets. Additionally, the rise of connected TV (CTV) and over-the-top (OTT) media is creating new opportunities for media buyers to reach audiences in innovative ways, while also raising new challenges related to measurement, targeting, and ad effectiveness. At the graduate level, media buying involves advanced concepts such as multi-touch attribution modeling, which seeks to quantify the impact of each marketing touchpoint on consumer behavior. Another key area of study is the application of artificial intelligence and machine learning algorithms to optimize media buying strategies, including predictive modeling and real-time bidding. Open questions in the field include the development of effective metrics for measuring the ROI of media buying campaigns, particularly in the context of emerging channels such as social media and influencer marketing. Additionally, the increasing use of programmatic advertising raises important questions about transparency, accountability, and the potential for ad fraud. As the field continues to evolve, media buyers must stay up-to-date with the latest developments in areas such as header bidding, server-side ad insertion, and the integration of media buying with broader marketing strategies, including content marketing and customer experience management.

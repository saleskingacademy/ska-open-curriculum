# Community Management

Community management is the professional practice of designing, operating and governing a group of people who interact with one another, not only with an organization, around a shared purpose. For a business, a well-run community can answer customers' questions faster than a support team, surface product insight earlier than a survey, turn customers into advocates and make a product harder to leave. A badly run one can become a public archive of complaints, a legal liability or a toxic space that harms members and the brand. This chapter is written at the master's level. It treats community management as a discipline with its own theory (social identity, social capital, participation inequality, the governance of shared resources), its own competing frameworks, its own metrics and economics, and its own ethical and legal obligations. It asks the learner to exercise independent judgement: to choose a community model that fits a business goal, to design the member journey and governance system, to measure health and value without fooling oneself, and to make defensible moderation decisions under uncertainty.

## Learning objectives

1. Define a community precisely and distinguish it from an audience, a customer base and a crowd, using social identity and social capital theory.
2. Explain the causal mechanisms by which communities form, grow, mature and decline, and identify the manager's levers at each stage.
3. Select a community model (support, product, acquisition, contribution, engagement, success, or a combination) to fit a stated organizational objective, and justify the choice against alternatives.
4. Compare the principal frameworks of the field, including the ladder of engagement, participation inequality, the community maturity model and commons governance principles, and state the limits of each.
5. Design an onboarding journey and a contributor pathway that move members from first visit to sustained contribution.
6. Build a governance system: guidelines, moderation roles, graduated sanctions, appeals and escalation, consistent with applicable law and platform rules.
7. Construct a measurement system with defined formulas for activity, responsiveness, retention and business value, and recognise vanity metrics and measurement traps.
8. Estimate the economic value of a community, including support deflection, retention lift and product insight, and present it with honest uncertainty.
9. Resolve conflicts and handle crises with procedures that protect members, staff and the organization.
10. Evaluate emerging practices, including AI-assisted moderation and decentralized governance, against evidence and ethics.

## Overview

Professional discipline for the signal specialist: community management. Applied practice used to generate revenue.

The phrase "signal specialist" names the role well. A community manager reads signals that no dashboard fully captures: the tone of a thread, the silence of a once-active member, the rising frequency of a feature request, the first sign of a dispute that will escalate. The work alternates between strategic design (what is this community for, and for whom?), operational execution (onboarding, programming, moderation, reporting) and judgement calls that no policy can fully anticipate. Revenue enters through several channels: lower support costs, higher retention, faster product adoption, referrals and, in some models, direct membership or event income. The manager's job is to create conditions in which members get enough value from each other that they keep coming back, and to connect that member value to organizational value without exploiting members.

## Foundations

Community management refers to the process of building, engaging, maintaining and facilitating online or offline communities, which are groups of individuals connected by a shared interest, goal, or identity. A community is a social unit with commonalities, such as geography, culture, interests or ideology, that fosters a sense of belonging and social interaction among its members. Its members typically share values, norms and goals. Core principles of community management include engagement, the act of participating in and contributing to community activities, and moderation, the process of guiding and regulating community behavior to ensure a positive and respectful environment.

Key vocabulary includes **community builder**, an individual responsible for creating and developing a community; **community guidelines**, a set of rules and expectations that outline acceptable behavior within the community; **social capital**, the networks, norms, and trust that enable cooperation and collective action within a community; and **community metrics**, quantitative measures used to assess community health, engagement, and growth. Further terms are **community engagement**, the process of interacting with and involving community members; **community outreach**, efforts to include groups that are marginalized or underrepresented in the community; and **community governance**, the systems and processes that guide decision-making and behavior within a community.

Practitioners must recognise the difference between a community and a **crowd**, a loose collection of individuals without a shared sense of identity or purpose. Two further distinctions matter in business settings. An **audience** is a set of people who receive communication from an organization, such as followers of a brand account; communication runs mainly one-to-many. A **customer base** is the set of people who buy; they may never interact with one another. A community exists only when members interact with each other and develop relationships, norms and identity that would persist, at least for a while, even if the sponsoring organization stopped posting. The test is many-to-many interaction plus shared identity.

**Social identity theory**, developed in social psychology, holds that individuals derive part of their self-concept from the groups they belong to, and that they favor their in-group and adopt its norms. For community management this explains why visible group identity (names, rituals, shared language, recognition) strengthens commitment, and also why communities can become exclusionary toward newcomers or outsiders. **Social capital** has two faces described in the sociological literature: **bonding** social capital is the dense trust among similar people in a close group, and **bridging** social capital is the weaker but wider set of ties across different groups. Bonding supports mutual help and loyalty; bridging supports the flow of new information and opportunities. Network research on the "strength of weak ties" shows that new information often arrives through acquaintances rather than close friends, which is one reason large, loosely connected professional communities are valuable for discovering jobs, ideas and solutions.

A practitioner should also understand **sense of community** as studied in community psychology, commonly described through four elements: membership (a feeling of belonging and boundaries), influence (members matter to the group and the group matters to them), integration and fulfilment of needs (members get something they value), and shared emotional connection (common history and experiences). These four elements form a practical diagnostic: when a community feels hollow, one or more of them is usually missing.

## Mechanisms

Community management operates through a series of interconnected mechanisms that foster engagement, facilitate communication, and promote a sense of belonging among members. The process begins with the establishment of a shared platform or space, physical or virtual, where members can interact. The original course gave two overlapping versions of the causal chain; they are merged here into one sequence.

1. **Initialization.** A community manager or founder initiates the community by defining its purpose, scope, audience and rules of engagement. This foundational step sets the tone for the community's culture and values. Communities started without a clear purpose drift toward either silence or an unfocused stream of promotion.
2. **Member acquisition.** Members are attracted through invitations, product touchpoints, events, social media, search and word of mouth. The community's value proposition, which states the benefits of membership in the member's terms, plays a crucial role in attracting and retaining members.
3. **Onboarding.** New members are integrated through a structured process: a welcome, an orientation to purpose and guidelines, a small first action and, ideally, a first response from another person. Effective onboarding reduces barriers to entry and encourages participation; most members who will ever contribute make their first contribution early or not at all.
4. **Content creation and sharing.** Members create and share content (posts, questions, answers, comments, media, code, events), which serves as the primary driver of engagement. The manager facilitates content creation by providing prompts, programming, templates and recognition.
5. **Interaction and feedback.** Members respond to each other's content with answers, support and constructive criticism. This reciprocity creates relationships and the felt value of membership. A question that receives no reply is a small failure of the mechanism and a strong predictor that the asker will not return.
6. **Norm establishment.** Over time, community norms and expectations emerge, shaping member behavior and the community's culture. Norms are learned mostly by example: newcomers copy what they see rewarded and tolerated, which is why early moderation decisions have outsized long-term effects.
7. **Moderation, governance and conflict resolution.** The manager, moderators and in mature communities members themselves enforce norms, resolve disputes fairly and constructively, and make decisions about rules and direction. This maintains a safe and inclusive environment.
8. **Evaluation and adaptation.** The community continually evaluates its effectiveness through metrics and member feedback, and adapts to changing circumstances, ensuring its long-term viability and relevance.

Throughout these mechanisms, community managers play a crucial role, facilitating communication, managing conflict, and keeping the community aligned with its purpose and goals.

Two feedback loops drive whether a community thrives. The **value loop**: contributions create value (answers, ideas, connections), value attracts and retains members, and members create more contributions. The **norm loop**: visible good behavior and fair enforcement make members feel safe, safety invites more open participation, and broader participation reinforces the norms. Either loop can run in reverse. When questions go unanswered, contributors stop visiting, so more questions go unanswered. When bad behavior goes unchecked, thoughtful members leave, so bad behavior becomes the visible norm. Much of community management is the work of keeping both loops turning in the right direction, especially in the early period when the community has not yet reached the critical mass at which members can sustain each other.

## The Community Lifecycle

Communities move through recognisable stages. Lifecycle models differ in naming, but most practitioners recognise four phases, each with a different job for the manager.

**Inception.** The community has few members, and nearly all activity is initiated by the manager. The job is to seed: invite a small group of carefully chosen founding members who have a real need and something to offer, personally welcome each newcomer, start and answer discussions, and gather feedback on purpose and format. Trying to grow quickly at this stage usually fails because newcomers arrive to an empty room.

**Establishment.** Members begin to initiate a meaningful share of discussions and responses, and recognisable regulars appear. The job shifts toward building processes (onboarding sequences, regular programming, guidelines refined from real cases) and recruiting the first volunteer moderators or champions.

**Maturity.** The community largely sustains itself; members answer most questions, generate most content and enforce many norms. The job becomes governance, programming at scale, integration with the business (product feedback channels, support workflows, marketing collaboration) and preventing stagnation. Sub-groups form, and the manager must balance their autonomy with the coherence of the whole.

**Mitosis or renewal, or decline.** Large mature communities may split into specialised sub-communities (mitosis), which can be healthy, or stagnate as founding members leave and newcomers find the culture closed. Signs of decline include a shrinking share of new contributors, rising response times, recurring arguments among a small core, and a growing ratio of complaints to helpful content. Renewal requires new programs, new leaders and sometimes a restatement of purpose.

The **Community Maturity Model** published by the practitioner research organization The Community Roundtable offers a more detailed assessment of maturity across several competencies (such as strategy, leadership, culture, community management, content and programming, policies and governance, tools, and measurement). Its value is in showing that a community can be mature in one dimension and immature in another; for example, a large, active community with no measurement or governance practice is fragile despite its size.

## Choosing a Community Model

A business community must have a primary purpose, because purpose decides platform, programming, metrics and staffing. A widely taught classification, the SPACES model popularised by the community-industry organization CMX, distinguishes six business value models:

- **Support**: members help each other solve problems; value is measured in questions answered and support cost avoided.
- **Product**: members provide ideas, feedback and testing; value is measured in insight quality and product adoption.
- **Acquisition**: the community attracts and converts new customers through advocacy, referrals and visibility; value is measured in attributed leads and revenue.
- **Contribution**: members create assets the organization or ecosystem uses, such as content, code, templates, translations or plug-ins; value is measured in contributions and their use.
- **Engagement**: members deepen their relationship with the brand and each other; value is measured in retention and loyalty.
- **Success**: members learn to get more from the product through education, peer coaching and best practices; value is measured in adoption, expansion and renewal.

Most communities serve two or three of these, but a community that tries to serve all six at once usually serves none well. The manager should name a primary model, at most two secondary ones, and the metrics for each. The choice should follow the organization's most pressing problem: a company drowning in repetitive support tickets has a clear case for a support model; a company with low feature adoption may need a success model; an open-source project depends on contribution.

The **SKA CONTEST checklist**, used in this course for strategy design, organizes the questions a manager must answer before launching or re-planning a community: **C**ontext (what is happening in the market, the organization and the existing member base?), **O**bjectives (what business and member outcomes will define success?), **N**eeds (what do prospective members need that they cannot easily get elsewhere?), **T**echnology (which platform and integrations fit those needs and the organization's capacity?), **E**nvironment (what legal, cultural and competitive conditions constrain the design?) and **S**trategy (what model, programs, staffing and milestones follow from all of the above?). A strategy that cannot answer the Needs question convincingly will struggle regardless of budget.

**Platform choice** follows from needs. Forums and threaded Q&A platforms suit support and knowledge communities because content is searchable and persists. Real-time chat platforms suit high-intimacy, fast-moving communities but bury knowledge and exhaust moderators. Social-network groups reach people where they already are but give the organization little control over data, features or rules. Owned platforms provide data ownership and integration with customer systems at higher cost. Offline and event-based communities create the strongest bonds per hour of contact but scale slowly. Many mature programs combine an owned searchable hub with chat for real-time connection and periodic in-person events.

## Methods And Frameworks

Community management employs various methods and frameworks to foster engagement, resolve conflicts, and promote a sense of belonging. Each has strengths and limitations, and managers must adapt them to their specific context. The original course listed them in one paragraph; they are separated, corrected and compared here.

**The Ladder of Engagement** (sometimes drawn as a commitment curve or contributor funnel) tracks member participation from lurkers to leaders, typically through stages such as visitor, member, participant, contributor, leader. It is used to tailor strategies that encourage progression: lower the cost of the next step, recognise each step, and offer a clear invitation to the one after. Its limitation is that it implies everyone should climb; in reality most members rationally stay at low levels and still receive value, and a community that pressures everyone to contribute can feel transactional.

**Participation inequality and the 90-9-1 rule.** The 90-9-1 heuristic (roughly 90% of users only read, 9% contribute occasionally and 1% create most content), associated with usability research on online communities, captures a robust pattern: contribution is highly unequal. The exact ratios vary widely by platform and community type, and some well-designed communities have far higher contribution rates, so it should be used as a reminder rather than a benchmark. Its practical lessons are that lurkers are not failures (they are often the majority who receive value), that the small creator group needs disproportionate care, and that making contribution easier (one-click reactions, simple answer formats) can widen the middle tier.

**The Community Maturity Model** assesses community development stages across multiple competencies, helping managers identify areas for improvement, as described in the lifecycle section. Its limitation is that maturity is not an end in itself; a small, early community can be exactly right for its purpose.

**The Social Identity Model of Deindividuation Effects (SIDE)**, from social psychology, explains how people in online groups, especially where individual identity is less visible, can orient strongly toward the group's identity and norms. It informs strategies to promote inclusivity: when group identity is salient and its norms are positive, anonymity can coexist with good behavior; when the salient norm is hostility, anonymity amplifies it. The design lesson is to make positive group norms visible and to ensure newcomers encounter them first.

**Diffusion of Innovations** explains how new ideas, practices and tools spread within social systems through communication channels over time, with adoption influenced by perceived relative advantage, compatibility, complexity, trialability and observability. In communities it guides how to introduce a new feature, program or norm: recruit respected early adopters, make results visible and let members try before committing.

**The Technology Acceptance Model (TAM)** predicts adoption of a new tool from its perceived usefulness and perceived ease of use. It helps a manager anticipate resistance to a platform migration: members will move only if the new space is clearly more useful and not harder to use, and a migration that fails either test will lose members.

**Commons governance principles.** Research on how communities successfully govern shared resources, most associated with the political economist Elinor Ostrom, identified recurring design principles of long-lasting self-governing institutions: clearly defined boundaries; rules suited to local conditions; arrangements that let those affected by rules participate in changing them; monitoring; graduated sanctions; accessible conflict-resolution mechanisms; recognition by outside authorities of the community's right to organize; and, for larger systems, nested layers of governance. Although the original research concerned physical resources such as fisheries and irrigation systems, online community scholars have found these principles a useful lens for moderation and governance, because an online community's attention, trust and shared knowledge are also a commons that can be overused or polluted.

**A note on growth arithmetic.** The original course presented a "formula for community growth, M = (N × F) / D", where M is membership, N new members, F feedback and D dropout. That expression is not a valid model: multiplying member counts by "feedback" has no defined unit, and it produces nonsense results. The correct relationship is a stock-and-flow model. If M_t is active membership at the start of a period, N is new active members per period and c is the churn rate (the share of active members who become inactive in a period), then M_(t+1) = M_t + N − c × M_t. Its steady state, where inflow equals outflow, is M* = N / c. The original insight survives in corrected form: retention (lower c) is as powerful as acquisition (higher N), and feedback matters because it is one of the main ways to reduce churn. Worked example 3 applies this model.

**Comparing the frameworks.** The ladder of engagement and participation inequality describe individual behavior; the maturity and lifecycle models describe the community as a whole; SIDE and social identity theory explain why norms spread; diffusion and TAM explain adoption of new things; commons principles explain governance. A competent manager uses them together and does not mistake any one for a complete theory. Failure modes common to all include neglecting to adapt strategies to changing community needs, failing to address conflicts promptly, neglecting community feedback, inadequate moderation, inconsistent communication, and prioritizing growth over member engagement, which leads to a shallow or toxic community.

## Designing Onboarding and Contributor Pathways

Because most members who will ever contribute do so early, onboarding is the highest-leverage process a manager owns. A strong onboarding design has five parts.

1. **Clear promise.** The sign-up page and welcome message state what the member will get and what the community expects, in a few sentences.
2. **Small first action.** Ask for something easy and meaningful: introduce yourself in a structured thread, answer one profile question, react to a pinned post, or ask your first question. Avoid long forms before the member has seen any value.
3. **Fast human response.** A first contribution that receives a warm, specific response from a person (not only an automated message) is the strongest predictor of a second contribution. Many communities assign volunteer "welcomers" to make this reliable.
4. **Orientation to norms.** Show, briefly, what good participation looks like: a model question, a model answer, the three most important guidelines. Link to the full guidelines rather than front-loading them.
5. **Next-step invitation.** After the first action, offer a relevant next step: a group to join, an event, a thread where the member's expertise is needed.

**Contributor pathways** extend onboarding into a progression for the members most willing to give. Typical rungs are helpful member, recognised expert, moderator or champion, and program leader. Each rung should have a clear description, a transparent way in, recognition that members value (status, access, learning, influence over the product, occasionally tangible rewards), and an easy way to step back. Recognition must be designed carefully: points and leaderboards can motivate some members while encouraging low-quality volume from others, and paying members for contributions can crowd out the intrinsic motivations that made them contribute in the first place. A good rule is to reward quality and helpfulness over quantity and to ask contributors what recognition they actually value.

**Volunteer health.** Volunteers who moderate or answer at scale are the community's most precious and most fragile resource. Burnout is common when workload is unbounded, decisions are unsupported by staff, and abuse directed at moderators is tolerated. Healthy programs set expectations for time commitment, rotate duties, give moderators private channels to consult each other and staff, back their reasonable decisions publicly, and thank them in ways they value.

## Governance and Moderation

Governance is the system by which a community makes and enforces decisions. Moderation is the daily practice of enforcing norms. Both should be designed before they are needed.

**Guidelines.** Good guidelines state the community's purpose, describe expected behavior positively, list prohibited behavior specifically (harassment, hate, spam, doxxing, illegal content, off-topic promotion), explain consequences and appeals, and are short enough to be read. They should be written in plain language, applied consistently and revised as new cases reveal gaps. Guidelines that exist only to protect the organization legally, without guiding members, are not read and do not shape norms.

**Roles.** Typical roles include staff community managers (accountable for strategy and final decisions), volunteer moderators (day-to-day enforcement within defined powers), subject experts or champions (answering and modeling good behavior) and trust-and-safety or legal specialists (handling serious cases such as threats or illegal content). Each role needs clear powers and escalation paths.

**Graduated sanctions.** Consistent with the commons principles above, sanctions should escalate in proportion to severity and repetition: a private reminder, a content edit or removal with explanation, a formal warning, a temporary suspension, and a permanent ban. Severe violations such as threats, illegal content or doxxing skip directly to removal and, where appropriate, reporting to authorities. Graduated sanctions preserve members who make honest mistakes while protecting the community from persistent bad actors.

**Due process.** Members accept enforcement more readily when decisions are explained, when they can appeal to someone other than the original moderator, and when similar cases are treated similarly. Keeping a private moderation log of actions and reasons supports consistency and helps train new moderators.

**Moderation capacity.** Moderation can be pre-moderation (content reviewed before publication; safe but slow), post-moderation (reviewed after publication; fast but riskier), reactive moderation (reviewed when reported) or distributed moderation (members' votes and flags influence visibility). Most communities combine automated filters for obvious spam and abuse, member reporting, and human review prioritized by risk. Worked example 2 shows how to size capacity.

**Legal and platform obligations.** Community managers do not need to be lawyers, but they must know when to involve one. In the United States, Section 230 of the Communications Decency Act generally shields online services from liability as publishers of users' content and protects good-faith moderation decisions, with exceptions such as federal criminal law and intellectual property. Intellectual property claims are commonly handled through the notice-and-takedown process of the Digital Millennium Copyright Act. Services directed at children under 13, or that knowingly collect their personal information, are subject to the Children's Online Privacy Protection Act. In the European Union, the Digital Services Act sets due-diligence obligations for hosting services and online platforms, such as notice-and-action mechanisms and statements of reasons for content restrictions, with heavier duties for very large platforms. Data protection law, including the GDPR in Europe, governs member data. Communities hosted on third-party platforms must also follow those platforms' terms, which may be stricter than the law. Because these regimes change and their application depends on facts, an organization running a public community should have counsel review its terms, privacy notice, moderation process and handling of law-enforcement requests.

**Ethics.** Beyond legal duties, a manager holds power over members' voice and reputation. Ethical practice includes transparency about who runs the community and for what commercial purpose, honesty about how member content and data are used, refusing to manipulate members (for example, by covert brand accounts posing as ordinary members or undisclosed paid endorsements), protecting vulnerable members, and making accessibility a design requirement so that people with disabilities can participate fully.

## Conflict Resolution and Crisis Management

Conflict is normal in any group that cares about something. The aim is not to eliminate disagreement but to keep it productive and to stop it from becoming personal or harmful.

**Interpersonal conflict.** A practical sequence for moderators: (1) de-escalate publicly with a short, neutral note reminding everyone of the guidelines and inviting a cooler tone; (2) move the discussion to private messages with each party; (3) listen actively, acknowledging each member's concerns and restating them accurately; (4) identify the underlying interest (a member who keeps attacking a feature may feel unheard about a real problem); (5) agree on a path, such as a clarified public statement, an apology, or simply separate threads; (6) document the outcome. If behavior continues, apply graduated sanctions.

**Community-wide disputes.** When a whole community splits over a decision (a pricing change, a policy change, a moderator action), the manager should acknowledge the issue quickly, give a clear timeline for a fuller response, consolidate discussion into one place, separate facts from rumors, involve decision-makers directly, and report back on what changed and what did not. Silence is interpreted as indifference.

**Crises.** A crisis is an event that threatens members' safety, the community's survival or the organization's reputation: a data breach, a harassment campaign, a member in danger, a viral complaint, a serious product failure. A crisis plan, prepared in advance, names who decides, who speaks, which internal teams (legal, security, communications, support) are contacted, the first public message templates, and how moderators are supported during heavy load. Safety comes first: credible threats of harm to self or others should be escalated immediately according to the organization's safety procedures and, where appropriate, to emergency services.

## Measuring Community Health and Value

A measurement system needs metrics at three levels: activity (is anything happening?), health (is the community working for members?) and value (is it achieving organizational objectives?). Each metric must have a precise definition, because "engagement" means different things to different people.

**Activity metrics.**

- **Active members**: members who performed a defined meaningful action (post, reply, reaction, event attendance) in a period. Define the action explicitly; logins alone overstate activity.
- **Stickiness** = daily active members / monthly active members (DAU/MAU). It indicates how habitual participation is. For example, 900 DAU and 6,000 MAU gives 15%.
- **Engagement rate (social content)** = (reactions + comments + shares) / audience size, or per impression, depending on the platform's convention. Always state the denominator.

**Health metrics.**

- **Response rate** = threads that received at least one reply / threads started.
- **Time to first response**: the time between a question and its first reply. Report the median and a percentile (such as the share answered within one hour), because a few extreme waits distort the mean.
- **Member-answer share** = answers given by members / all answers. In a support community this shows whether the community is self-sustaining.
- **Contributor ratio** = members who contributed in a period / active members.
- **Cohort retention**: the share of members who joined in a given period and are still active after one, three and six months.
- **New-contributor share**: the share of contributors in a period who are contributing for the first time; it signals whether the community is renewing or closing.
- **Sentiment and safety**: share of content removed or reported, volume of guideline violations, and member-reported feeling of safety in surveys.

**Value metrics.**

- **Support deflection**: questions resolved in the community that would otherwise have become support tickets, multiplied by cost per ticket. Measuring the "would otherwise have become a ticket" share requires surveys or experiments and is uncertain.
- **Retention and expansion lift**: comparison of renewal or expansion rates between community members and similar non-members. Because more committed customers are more likely to join communities in the first place, a simple comparison overstates the community's effect; matching on account size, tenure and product usage, or using experiments, gives a more honest estimate.
- **Product insight**: ideas or bug reports from the community that were adopted, and the speed at which feedback reached product teams.
- **Acquisition**: referred leads and revenue attributed to community activity, with an explicit attribution method.

**Vanity metrics and traps.** Total registered members is the most common vanity metric: it never falls, so it always looks good, and it says nothing about activity. Page views can rise because search engines index old threads while the community itself dies. Reactions can be inflated by low-effort prompts. Any metric that becomes a target for staff will be gamed; pairing activity metrics with health metrics (for example, posts with response rate and sentiment) limits this. The strongest discipline is to report a small, stable set of metrics tied to the stated model, with definitions published so they cannot quietly change.

## The Economics of Community

Community programs compete for budget with paid acquisition, support staff and product work, so a manager must be able to describe their economics. Costs include staff time, platform licences, events, rewards and recognition, moderation tooling and the time product, support and marketing colleagues spend in the community. Benefits fall into the value categories above.

A credible business case has four properties. It uses conservative assumptions and says so. It separates measured effects (questions answered, tickets avoided in an experiment) from estimated ones (retention lift from matched comparison). It shows a range rather than a single number. And it includes the slower, harder-to-measure benefits (product insight, brand trust, recruiting) qualitatively rather than inventing numbers for them. Executives tend to trust a modest, well-reasoned case more than a spectacular one, and a manager who overclaims early loses credibility when results arrive. ## Worked Examples

To illustrate the application of community management principles, consider the following scenarios. The original course included two sets of examples, one of which contained a calculation error; each scenario now appears once with verified numbers. Numbers are illustrative.

### Worked example 1: Calculating engagement and stating targets correctly

A community manager for a brand's social media presence wants the engagement rate of a recent post. The account has 10,000 followers, and the post received 500 likes, 200 comments and 50 shares.

- Engagement rate = (500 + 200 + 50) / 10,000 = 750 / 10,000 = 7.5%.

The original example reported 0.75%, an arithmetic error by a factor of ten that would have led to the wrong conclusion. On a follower-based denominator, 7.5% is high for a single post; the manager should check whether the post reached an unusually large share of followers or was amplified by sharing.

A second part of the original example asked: if the current engagement rate on a forum is 2% and the goal is 5%, what increase is required? The answer is 3 percentage points, which is a 150% relative increase ((5 − 2) / 2 = 1.5). Stating the goal as "an increase of 3%" is ambiguous and invites misunderstanding, because a 3% relative increase on 2% would only reach 2.06%. Professional reports always distinguish percentage points from percent change. To achieve the target, the manager could improve content relevance, reduce friction in replying, or run programming that invites response, and should track response rate alongside it to make sure the increase reflects real conversation.

### Worked example 2: Sizing moderation capacity

A community receives 500 posts per day. A moderator can review 50 posts per hour and works 8 hours a day.

- Posts reviewed per day = 50 × 8 = 400.
- Share reviewed = 400 / 500 = 80%.

If post volume grows 5% per month for six months, daily volume becomes 500 × 1.05^6 = 670 posts. Reviewing all of them would need 670 / 50 = 13.4 moderator-hours per day, or 13.4 / 8 = 1.68 full-time moderator-days per day.

Interpretation: the community is already under-moderated and will become more so. Options include automated filters that remove obvious spam before human review (so humans see fewer posts), risk-based prioritization (review all posts by new members and reported posts first, sample the rest), recruiting and training volunteer moderators, or adding staff. The manager should choose a combination and set a target, such as all reported posts reviewed within two hours and all first posts by new members reviewed before or shortly after publication.

### Worked example 3: Membership as stock and flow

A community has 2,000 active members. It gains 300 new active members a month, and 8% of active members become inactive each month.

- Steady-state membership = N / c = 300 / 0.08 = 3,750 active members.
- Membership after 12 months, applying M_(t+1) = M_t + 300 − 0.08 × M_t each month, is about 3,107. The same result follows from the closed form M_12 = 3,750 − (3,750 − 2,000) × 0.92^12.

Interpretation: no amount of acquisition at 300 a month can take this community beyond 3,750 active members while churn stays at 8%. Reducing churn to 6% would raise the ceiling to 300 / 0.06 = 5,000 without any new acquisition effort. This is the precise sense in which the original course's intuition, that feedback and retention matter as much as new members, is correct.

A cohort view adds detail: if 1,000 members join in January, 450 are active after one month and 300 after three months, then three-month retention is 30%, and 300 / 450 = 66.7% of those still active after the first month were still active two months later. The steep first-month drop shows that onboarding, not long-term programming, is where most members are lost.

### Worked example 4: Event budgeting and allocation

A community manager has $1,000 for a meetup with a maximum of 50 attendees. Food and materials cost $20 per attendee.

- Attendee costs at full capacity = 50 × $20 = $1,000, leaving $0 for venue, accessibility, or speaker costs.

The manager therefore either caps attendance at 40 (40 × $20 = $800, leaving $200 for other expenses), reduces per-attendee cost, finds a venue partner or sponsor, or requests a larger budget. A related allocation scenario from the original course: a community center with $100,000 and member interests of 40% sports, 30% arts and 30% education might allocate $40,000, $30,000 and $30,000 respectively. Allocation in proportion to stated interest is a reasonable default, but a manager exercising judgement would also weigh cost per participant, existing alternatives in the area, the needs of under-served groups and evidence of impact, rather than treating interest shares as the only input.

### Worked example 5: An honest support-deflection estimate

A software company's community resolves 1,200 questions a month through member answers. A survey of askers suggests that about 30% would otherwise have opened a support ticket. The fully loaded cost of handling a ticket is $15 (an illustrative internal figure). The community program costs $4,000 a month.

- Tickets deflected = 1,200 × 0.30 = 360 per month.
- Deflection value = 360 × $15 = $5,400 per month.
- Net benefit = $5,400 − $4,000 = $1,400 per month; return on cost = 1,400 / 4,000 = 35%.

Interpretation: the case is positive but modest, and it depends heavily on the 30% assumption. A sensitivity check is essential: if only 20% would have opened tickets, deflection value falls to 240 × $15 = $3,600, below cost. The manager should present the range, explain how the survey was done, and add the qualitative benefits (product insight, retention) without assigning invented numbers to them.

### Worked example 6: Reading response-time data

Ten questions in a week received first responses after 5, 12, 15, 20, 30, 45, 60, 90, 240 and 600 minutes.

- Median time to first response = average of the 5th and 6th values = (30 + 45) / 2 = 37.5 minutes.
- Mean = 1,117 / 10 = 111.7 minutes.
- Share answered within 60 minutes = 7 / 10 = 70%.

Interpretation: the mean is three times the median because two questions waited four and ten hours. Reporting only the mean would suggest the typical member waits nearly two hours, which is false; reporting only the median would hide the members who were left waiting. The right report gives both the median and the share answered within the service target, and investigates the long waits (for example, questions posted overnight, or questions in a topic area with no experts).

### Worked example 7: Resolving a forum conflict

In an online forum, a community manager notices a heated exchange between two long-standing members about whether a workaround they each recommend is safe. By applying active listening, the manager posts a short public note thanking both for their expertise and asking for a cooler tone, then messages each privately, acknowledging their concerns and restating them accurately. One member feels the other dismissed their experience publicly; the other worries newcomers will follow unsafe advice. The manager proposes that both co-author a pinned post comparing the two approaches with the risks of each. Both agree, the thread is locked with a link to the new post, and the episode becomes a resource rather than a grudge. The principle: separate people from the problem, find the shared interest (here, members' safety), and turn the conflict into a contribution.

## Case Study

### Case study: Harborline Software revives a stalled developer community

Harborline Software is a fictional company that sells an application-integration platform; the people, figures and events in this case are invented for teaching.

**Problem.** Harborline launched a developer forum to reduce support load. Two years later it had 8,000 registered accounts but only about 600 monthly active members. Thirty-eight percent of questions received no reply, and the median time to first response was 19 hours. A handful of power users answered most questions and were showing signs of burnout; two of them publicly announced they were leaving after a heated thread in which a staff member contradicted them without explanation. Leadership was considering shutting the forum and moving all questions to paid support.

**What was done.** A newly hired community lead used the CONTEST checklist and chose a primary support model with a secondary product model. Over six months the team:

1. Rewrote the guidelines into a single page with a clear purpose statement, specific prohibited behaviors and a graduated sanctions ladder, and published an appeals route.
2. Created a champion program with clear criteria, early access to product betas, a private channel with product managers, and public recognition based on accepted answers rather than post counts.
3. Set up a triage rota in which support engineers and champions shared responsibility for any question unanswered after four hours, with automated alerts.
4. Redesigned onboarding: a three-question welcome, a prompt to post a first question using a template, and a volunteer welcomer assigned to reply to every first post.
5. Agreed with the product team that staff would never overrule a member publicly without explanation and would acknowledge feature requests with a status.
6. Replaced registered-member counts in executive reporting with monthly active members, response rate, median time to first response, member-answer share and three-month cohort retention.

**Measured results.** After six months, unanswered questions fell from 38% to 12%, and median time to first response fell from 19 hours to 3.5 hours. Monthly active members rose from about 600 to about 1,100, an increase of 83%. Members, rather than staff, provided 70% of accepted answers. Both departed power users returned as champions. Support leadership estimated, from a survey of askers, a meaningful reduction in "how do I" tickets, and presented the estimate as a range rather than a single figure.

**Lessons.**

- The forum was not failing because developers did not want a community; it was failing because the value loop had reversed. Fixing responsiveness restarted it.
- Volunteers left because of how they were treated, not because of workload alone. Respect and influence were the recognition they valued most.
- Changing what was reported changed what staff paid attention to. Registered accounts had hidden the decline for two years.
- A clearly chosen primary model kept the program focused; product feedback improved as a by-product of a healthy support community.

## Applications

Community management is applied in many domains, including customer communities, professional associations, open-source projects, online forums, social media platforms, education, and offline local communities. In online forums, community managers moderate discussions, enforce guidelines and foster engagement through content programming and member interaction. On large discussion platforms, volunteer moderators typically run individual sub-communities under platform-wide rules enforced by the platform's own staff, a nested governance structure that mirrors the commons principles described earlier.

In brand social media, community managers build relationships, manage customer conversations publicly and handle crises, using social media management and listening tools to monitor conversations, respond to inquiries and analyse engagement. Strictly, much of this work is audience management, and the skills transfer: tone, responsiveness and moderation judgement. In customer communities for software and services, community management joins support, customer success and product management. In open-source projects, community management determines whether volunteer contributors stay, and governance (who can merge code, how decisions are made, how conflicts are resolved) is often formal and public. In membership organizations and creator communities, the community itself is the product and may generate direct revenue through subscriptions and events.

In offline communities, community managers work with local organizations, governments and stakeholders to develop programs, events and initiatives that promote social cohesion, economic development and civic engagement. Effective community management in every domain involves understanding the community's needs, values and norms, and developing strategies that balance individual and collective interests. By doing so, community managers build trust, foster collaboration and create a sense of belonging. Key skills include communication, facilitation, problem-solving and conflict resolution, data analysis, program design, and the ability to explain community value to executives in business terms.

## Common Errors

Practitioners of community management often make mistakes that hinder growth and engagement. The original two lists are merged and extended here.

1. **No clear purpose or model.** A community "for everything" becomes a community for nothing in particular.
2. **Neglecting clear guidelines and expectations,** leading to confusion, inconsistent enforcement and conflict.
3. **Failing to listen and respond to member feedback,** resulting in a sense of disregard, lost trust and disengagement.
4. **Prioritizing quantity over quality,** chasing membership numbers rather than meaningful interaction, which produces a large, shallow and unengaged community.
5. **Launching to an empty room,** inviting large numbers before a seeded core exists to welcome and respond.
6. **Not recognizing contributors and moderators,** or recognizing them only with points, leading to burnout and departure of the most valuable members.
7. **Assuming one approach fits all,** when different communities and sub-groups need tailored strategies.
8. **Over-reliance on automation** without human presence, so members feel processed rather than welcomed.
9. **Reporting vanity metrics,** which hides decline until it is severe.
10. **Overstating value,** claiming retention or revenue effects that are really selection effects, and losing credibility with executives.
11. **Inconsistent or opaque moderation,** which members perceive as favoritism and which undermines legitimacy.
12. **Using the community as a broadcast channel** for marketing messages, which drives out the member-to-member interaction that defines a community.
13. **Neglecting ongoing evaluation and adaptation,** prioritizing short-term goals over long-term health and resilience.

Effective community management requires a nuanced understanding of these pitfalls and a commitment to ongoing learning and improvement.

## Advanced

Community management is evolving to incorporate emerging technologies and research. Advanced practice involves using these developments to create more personalized, adaptive and resilient communities without losing the human relationships that define them.

**AI-assisted moderation and support.** Machine-learning classifiers and large language models are increasingly used to flag likely spam, harassment and policy violations, to suggest answers from past threads, to summarize long discussions and to route questions to likely experts. Used well, they reduce moderator load and response times. Their risks are well documented: classifiers can be less accurate for dialects, minority languages and reclaimed terms, leading to unequal enforcement; generated answers can be confidently wrong; and members may feel deceived if they cannot tell whether they are talking to a person. Sound practice keeps humans responsible for consequential decisions, audits automated decisions for bias, discloses automation, and measures error rates rather than assuming accuracy. Machine-learning analysis of community dynamics to predict member churn or conflict is promising but raises privacy questions about how member behavior is profiled.

**Decentralized and member-owned governance.** Some communities experiment with member voting, elected councils, cooperative ownership and, in the blockchain sphere, token-based voting and decentralized autonomous organizations (DAOs). These raise real questions about ownership, accountability and decision-making. Token-weighted voting can concentrate power in the largest holders, low participation can let small groups decide, and legal status is unsettled in many jurisdictions. The older commons research suggests that what matters is less the technology than whether affected members can genuinely participate in rule-making, whether monitoring and sanctions are fair, and whether external authorities recognize the arrangement.

**Network analysis.** Social network analysis maps who interacts with whom, identifying bridges between sub-groups, isolated newcomers and over-dependence on a few central members. It supports better decisions about where to invest (for example, protecting the handful of members who connect otherwise separate clusters) and gives a structural measure of health that complements activity counts.

**Measurement of health and resilience.** Developing valid measures of community health remains an open problem. Activity metrics are easy but shallow; survey measures of sense of community are richer but costly and subject to response bias; causal estimates of business value require experiments that organizations rarely run. Progress is likely to come from combining behavioral, network and survey data and from more willingness to run controlled tests.

**Public discourse and platform governance.** Communities increasingly shape public discourse, and managers face questions about misinformation, harassment campaigns and social inequality in who gets heard. The balance between community autonomy and platform governance, and between cohesion and diversity, is an active debate. Regulation such as the European Digital Services Act is formalizing expectations for transparency and due process in content moderation, and these expectations are spreading to smaller communities through platform terms.

**Interdisciplinarity and co-creation.** The field draws on sociology, psychology, economics, computer science and political philosophy. There is a growing emphasis on community-led research and co-creation, recognizing that members should help set research agendas and governance rules that affect them. As digital communities become a primary form of social life for many people, community management is likely to carry greater responsibility for the well-being of members and the quality of shared public spaces.

## Summary

A community exists when members interact with each other and share identity, norms and purpose; it is more than an audience or a customer base. Its mechanisms form two feedback loops, a value loop of contributions and responses and a norm loop of visible behavior and fair enforcement, which the manager must keep turning the right way, especially before critical mass. Communities move through inception, establishment, maturity and renewal or decline, each needing a different managerial focus. A business community should have a primary value model, such as support, product, acquisition, contribution, engagement or success, chosen from the organization's most pressing problem and designed with a structured checklist. The ladder of engagement, participation inequality, maturity models, SIDE, diffusion, technology acceptance and commons governance principles each illuminate part of the picture, and none is complete alone. Growth is a stock-and-flow process in which retention matters as much as acquisition, with steady state N / c. Onboarding is the highest-leverage process, and volunteer contributors need protection from burnout. Governance requires clear guidelines, defined roles, graduated sanctions, due process and awareness of legal duties. Measurement must combine activity, health and value metrics with precise definitions and honest treatment of uncertainty and selection effects. Emerging tools, from AI moderation to member-owned governance, offer real benefits and real risks and should be evaluated on evidence and ethics.

## Key terms

- **Community**: a group whose members interact with one another and share identity, norms and purpose.
- **Audience**: people who receive one-to-many communication from an organization without necessarily interacting with each other.
- **Social identity theory**: the theory that people derive part of their self-concept from group membership and adopt group norms.
- **Bonding social capital**: dense trust and reciprocity among similar members of a close group.
- **Bridging social capital**: weaker ties that connect different groups and carry new information.
- **Sense of community**: members' felt membership, influence, fulfilment of needs and shared emotional connection.
- **Value loop**: the reinforcing cycle in which contributions create value that retains members who contribute more.
- **Critical mass**: the level of participation at which members can sustain each other's activity without constant staff initiation.
- **Ladder of engagement**: a model of member progression from passive visitor to leader.
- **Participation inequality (90-9-1)**: the pattern in which a small minority creates most content while most members read.
- **SPACES model**: a classification of business community value into support, product, acquisition, contribution, engagement and success.
- **Graduated sanctions**: enforcement that escalates in proportion to the severity and repetition of violations.
- **Commons governance principles**: design principles of durable self-governing institutions identified in research on shared resources.
- **Churn rate**: the share of active members who become inactive in a period.
- **Steady-state membership**: the level at which new active members equal those lost, N / c.
- **Stickiness (DAU/MAU)**: daily active members divided by monthly active members.
- **Time to first response**: the delay between a member's question and the first reply.
- **Member-answer share**: the proportion of answers provided by members rather than staff.
- **Support deflection**: questions resolved in the community that would otherwise have become paid support contacts.
- **Vanity metric**: a measure that looks impressive but does not indicate health or value, such as total registered accounts.
- **Selection effect**: bias that arises when more committed people are more likely to join, making the community appear to cause outcomes it may not cause.

## Review questions

1. What distinguishes a community from an audience and from a crowd?
2. How do bonding and bridging social capital differ, and why does a professional community need both?
3. What are the value loop and the norm loop, and how can each run in reverse?
4. Why is rapid growth during the inception stage usually counterproductive?
5. Which SPACES model would you choose as primary for a company overwhelmed by repetitive support tickets, and what metrics would you track?
6. What are the limits of the 90-9-1 rule as a benchmark?
7. Why is M = (N × F) / D not a valid growth model, and what model should replace it?
8. If a community gains 300 active members a month and loses 8% of active members monthly, what is its steady-state size, and what would it be at 6% churn?
9. Why is a member's first contribution and the response to it so important in onboarding?
10. What are graduated sanctions, and which commons governance principle do they reflect?
11. Why should time to first response be reported as a median and a within-target share rather than only a mean?
12. Why does a simple comparison of renewal rates between community members and non-members overstate the community's effect?
13. If an engagement rate rises from 2% to 5%, how should the change be described in percentage points and in percent?
14. What safeguards should accompany the use of AI tools for moderation?
15. What legal questions should prompt a community manager to involve counsel?

## Answer key

1. A community's members interact with one another and share identity, norms and purpose; an audience receives one-to-many communication without necessarily interacting; a crowd is a loose collection of people without shared identity or purpose.
2. Bonding capital is dense trust among similar, closely tied members and supports mutual help and loyalty; bridging capital is weaker ties across different groups and carries new information and opportunities. A professional community needs bonding to sustain help and bridging to keep ideas and opportunities flowing.
3. The value loop links contributions to value to retention to more contributions; it reverses when unanswered questions drive contributors away. The norm loop links visible good behavior and fair enforcement to safety to broader participation; it reverses when unchecked bad behavior drives thoughtful members out and becomes the visible norm.
4. Newcomers arriving before a seeded core exists find little activity and no response, so they leave and form a negative first impression; growth is better pursued after members can reliably welcome and help each other.
5. A support model, tracking response rate, time to first response, member-answer share, resolved questions and an estimate of support deflection.
6. The ratios vary widely across platforms and communities, so they are a reminder that participation is unequal rather than a target; well-designed communities can exceed the contribution levels it implies, and treating it as a benchmark can excuse poor design.
7. Multiplying members by "feedback" has no defined unit and produces meaningless numbers. The stock-and-flow model M_(t+1) = M_t + N − c × M_t, with steady state N / c, correctly represents acquisition and churn.
8. Steady state is 300 / 0.08 = 3,750 active members; at 6% churn it is 300 / 0.06 = 5,000.
9. Most members who will ever contribute do so early, and a warm, specific human response to the first contribution strongly predicts a second; a first contribution that is ignored usually ends participation.
10. Graduated sanctions escalate from reminders to removal, warnings, suspensions and bans according to severity and repetition; they reflect the commons principle that sanctions should be proportionate and graduated.
11. Response-time data is skewed by a few very long waits, which inflate the mean; the median shows the typical experience and the within-target share shows how many members were served on time.
12. More committed customers are more likely to join communities in the first place, so their higher renewal reflects pre-existing commitment as well as any community effect; matched comparisons or experiments are needed.
13. It is an increase of 3 percentage points and a relative increase of 150%.
14. Humans stay responsible for consequential decisions, automated decisions are audited for accuracy and bias across groups, automation is disclosed to members, appeals are available, and error rates are measured rather than assumed.
15. Questions about liability for user content, intellectual property takedown notices, children's data, data protection obligations, obligations under laws such as the Digital Services Act, law-enforcement requests, threats of harm and the drafting of terms and privacy notices.

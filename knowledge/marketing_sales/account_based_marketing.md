# Account Based Marketing

Account-based marketing (ABM) turns the usual marketing funnel on its side. Instead of attracting as many leads as possible and filtering them down to the few worth selling to, an ABM team starts by naming the specific organizations it wants as customers, learns how each one buys, and then coordinates marketing, sales and customer success to win and grow those accounts. This chapter assumes you already know basic marketing and sales vocabulary and focuses on applying account-based principles to real situations: choosing accounts, mapping buying groups, building plays, aligning teams, budgeting by tier and proving results with account-level metrics. By the end you should be able to design, run and evaluate an ABM program for a B2B company of modest size, and to recognize when ABM is the wrong tool.

## Learning objectives

1. Define account-based marketing and explain how it differs from lead-based demand generation in its unit of measure, its sequence and its team structure.
2. Distinguish the one-to-one, one-to-few and one-to-many tiers of ABM and match each tier to account value, resources and personalization depth.
3. Build an ideal customer profile from closed-won data and convert it into a scored, tiered target account list.
4. Map a buying group, identify the roles that shape a B2B decision and plan coverage of each role.
5. Design multi-channel account plays that combine insight-led content, advertising, sales outreach and events in a coordinated sequence.
6. Set up the sales and marketing agreements, cadences and data flows that ABM depends on.
7. Calculate and interpret account-level metrics such as coverage, engagement, opportunity rate, win rate, deal size, pipeline velocity and program return.
8. Design an ABM pilot with a matched control group so that results can be attributed with reasonable confidence.
9. Apply privacy, consent and ethical principles to account research, tracking and personalization.
10. Diagnose the common failure modes of ABM programs and propose corrective actions.

## Overview

Account-based marketing is a professional discipline for the prospecting specialist: an applied practice used to generate and grow revenue from a deliberately chosen set of organizations. It is practiced mostly in business-to-business (B2B) markets where deals are large, buying decisions involve several people, and the lifetime value of a single customer can justify serious individual attention. The practitioner who succeeds in ABM is part researcher, part strategist, part producer and part project manager, because the work spans data analysis, message design, campaign execution and close coordination with sellers.

## Foundations

Account-based marketing refers to a strategic approach to B2B marketing in which a company focuses its marketing efforts on a specific set of target accounts and the decision-makers inside them, rather than casting a wide net to appeal to a broad audience. The approach rests on personalization: marketing efforts are tailored to the needs, goals and buying behavior of each account or group of similar accounts. That requires a real understanding of each account's business goals, challenges and decision process.

The core vocabulary of the discipline is as follows.

**Target account**: a specific company or organization identified as a high-value potential customer, typically selected on factors such as industry, company size, strategic fit and revenue potential. Some writers call this a **key account** when the relationship already exists or the account is strategically vital.

**Account list**: the curated collection of target accounts that the program will pursue during a defined period.

**Ideal customer profile (ICP)**: a description of the characteristics of the organizations most likely to buy, succeed with and stay loyal to your offer. An ICP describes companies, not people.

**Buyer persona**: a semi-fictional representation of a type of person inside the account, such as the operations director or the IT security lead, including that person's goals, worries and information habits. Personas describe people; the ICP describes organizations.

**Decision-makers**: individuals inside an account with authority to approve the purchase. In practice they sit alongside influencers, users, gatekeepers and blockers, all of whom shape the outcome.

**Personalization**: tailoring messages, content and experiences to address the specific needs, interests and context of an account or person.

**Account-centric**: organizing marketing and sales effort around the needs and goals of the target account, rather than around products, channels or campaigns.

**One-to-one marketing**: an approach that treats each target account as a market of one, with a customized strategy, content and team.

**One-to-few marketing**: an approach that groups a small number of similar accounts, often by industry or shared problem, and applies a tailored strategy to each group.

**One-to-many marketing**: traditionally, a single strategy applied to a broad audience through channels such as advertising, email and social media. Within ABM, the term usually means a programmatic tier that uses technology to apply light personalization to hundreds or thousands of named accounts.

**Account selection** is the process of identifying and prioritizing target accounts, and **account segmentation** is the process of grouping accounts by shared characteristics such as industry, size, region, technology in use or business problem. **Account scoring** evaluates and ranks accounts with a defined formula. **Account insights** are the data and intelligence gathered about each account. The **customer journey** is the series of interactions an organization has with your brand, from first awareness through purchase, adoption, renewal and advocacy.

A first principle of ABM is the alignment of sales and marketing. Because the two functions pursue the same named accounts, they must agree on which accounts matter, what counts as progress, who does what and when, and how results will be judged. ABM metrics, including account coverage, engagement and conversion rates, are used to measure the effectiveness of the strategy, and later sections of this chapter define them precisely.

A second first principle is quality over quantity. A lead-based program can succeed by producing a large volume of inquiries, even if most are poor. An ABM program succeeds only if the right accounts move forward. A hundred downloads from companies that will never buy are worth nothing to an account-based team; a single meaningful conversation with the chief financial officer of a top-tier account may be worth a great deal.

A third first principle is that organizations, not individuals, buy. In a complex B2B sale, one enthusiastic contact rarely closes a deal. A group of people with different priorities must reach agreement, and the account-based team succeeds by helping that group reach a decision it can defend internally.

## Origins and Place of ABM in the B2B Go-to-Market

The ideas behind ABM are old. Key account management, in which a dedicated seller or team owns a strategic customer relationship and plans its growth, has existed for decades in industries such as industrial equipment, professional services and enterprise technology. What changed in the 2000s and 2010s was that marketing gained the data and tools to participate in that account-level work directly. The term account-based marketing is generally credited to ITSMA, a professional association for technology and services marketers, which promoted it in the early-to-mid 2000s as a way for marketing to support strategic account teams. Later, software for advertising to named companies, tracking anonymous company-level website visits and buying third-party intent data allowed the account-based approach to scale beyond the largest accounts.

It helps to place ABM against the alternatives. In a **lead-based demand generation** model, marketing creates content and campaigns that attract individuals, scores those individuals on fit and behavior, and hands qualified leads to sales. The unit of measure is the lead, and the funnel narrows from many leads to few customers. In an **account-based** model, the funnel is inverted: the team starts with a defined set of accounts, expands contact coverage inside each account, increases engagement, and converts accounts into opportunities and customers. The unit of measure is the account. In a **key account management** model, the emphasis is on existing strategic customers and is led by sales or account management, with marketing playing a supporting role.

These models are not mutually exclusive. Most mature B2B companies run a blend: broad demand generation to capture in-market buyers they could not have predicted, ABM for the accounts with the highest value or strategic importance, and key account management for the largest existing customers. The skill is to decide where each approach earns its cost.

ABM tends to fit when the following conditions hold. Average contract values are high enough to justify research and personalization per account. The total addressable market can be named, at least approximately, as a finite list of organizations. Purchases involve several stakeholders and long cycles. The company sells a considered solution rather than a self-service commodity. And sales and marketing leadership are willing to commit to shared goals. ABM tends to fit poorly when the product is low priced, bought by individuals on impulse, sold to a market of millions of tiny businesses, or when the company cannot yet describe which customers succeed with it.

## Mechanisms

Account-based marketing operates through a cycle of coordinated steps that align sales and marketing effort on high-value accounts. Each step feeds the next, and the cycle repeats as the team learns.

**Identification and selection.** Firms use data and judgement to determine which accounts have the highest potential for conversion and revenue growth. Inputs include firmographic attributes such as industry and size, technographic attributes such as the systems the account runs, behavioral signals such as engagement with your content, and strategic considerations such as a reference value in a new market.

**Research and profiling.** Detailed information about each selected account is gathered: its business priorities, current challenges, recent events, decision-making process, key stakeholders and buying behavior. The depth of research depends on the tier.

**Personalization and content creation.** Content, messages and experiences are designed to resonate with each account's or segment's specific needs. The aim is relevance: a message that shows the account you understand its situation and have something useful to say about it.

**Channel selection and multi-channel engagement.** Marketing and sales choose the channels most likely to reach each buying group, such as email, social media, account-based advertising, direct mail, events, webinars and personal outreach. Multi-channel engagement ensures the message is delivered consistently and reinforced from several directions, which raises the likelihood of capturing the attention of busy stakeholders.

**Nurturing and qualification.** As engagement grows, interactions must become more meaningful, and the account must be qualified before significant sales resources are committed. In ABM, qualification is judged at the account level: is a real initiative forming, and is a buying group engaged?

**Sales and marketing alignment.** Throughout the process, both teams adjust their approach based on feedback and account responses. Marketing learns from sales conversations; sales uses marketing intelligence to time and shape outreach.

**Measurement and feedback.** Data and analytics are used to track progress, providing insights into account behavior and preferences. This feedback loop allows continuous refinement of targeting, personalization and engagement.

The ultimate purpose is to drive conversion and revenue growth by building deep, useful relationships with key accounts, which leads in turn to loyalty, retention, expansion and advocacy. By focusing on accounts that matter and tailoring the approach to their needs, firms aim to improve the return on marketing and sales investment. The causal chain can be summarized as: right accounts, then relevant insight, then coordinated engagement across the buying group, then qualified opportunity, then won and expanded revenue, with measurement feeding back into account selection.

## The ABM Tier Model: One-to-One, One-to-Few and One-to-Many

Personalization is expensive, so ABM programs ration it. The standard approach divides target accounts into tiers, each with a different depth of research, customization and team involvement. The three-tier structure described here is widely used; teams rename the tiers to suit themselves, but the logic is consistent.

### Tier 1: one-to-one (strategic ABM)

One-to-one ABM treats each account as a market of one. It is reserved for a small number of accounts, often between five and twenty-five for a mid-sized company, whose potential value is large enough to justify a dedicated cross-functional team. Typical activities include a written account plan, deep research into the account's strategy and financial results, interviews with existing contacts, custom content such as a point of view on the account's specific challenge, executive briefings, bespoke events, a microsite or personalized proposal, and executive sponsorship from your own leadership. The marketer assigned to a Tier 1 account often works almost as part of the account team, attending planning meetings and reviewing deal strategy.

The strength of one-to-one ABM is depth: it can change how an account perceives its own problem. The weakness is cost and fragility. If the account list is chosen poorly, or a reorganization at the account removes the sponsor, a large investment can be lost.

### Tier 2: one-to-few (ABM lite or cluster ABM)

One-to-few ABM groups accounts that share an industry, a business problem, a technology environment or a buying trigger, and personalizes at the cluster level. A cluster might be "regional hospital systems moving to a new electronic health record" or "mid-sized logistics firms expanding cold-chain capacity." The team writes content for the cluster, adds light account-level personalization such as the account name, a relevant local example or a reference customer in the same segment, and coordinates outreach by sellers who know the segment. A Tier 2 program might cover thirty to two hundred accounts.

The strength of one-to-few ABM is efficiency with real relevance. The weakness is that clusters can become generic if they are defined by convenient data fields rather than by shared problems.

### Tier 3: one-to-many (programmatic ABM)

One-to-many ABM uses technology to reach hundreds or thousands of named accounts with targeted advertising, dynamic website content, intent-triggered email and sales alerts. Personalization is light, usually at the level of industry or role. The aim is to build familiarity, detect accounts that become active and route them to the right seller at the right moment. Accounts that show strong engagement may be promoted to Tier 2 or Tier 1.

The strength of programmatic ABM is scale. The weakness is that it can drift back into ordinary demand generation with a target list attached, measured by clicks rather than account progress.

### Choosing tier boundaries

Tier assignment should follow a few rules. First, value: higher potential lifetime value justifies a higher tier. Second, readiness: an account showing active buying signals may deserve more attention than a larger account that is dormant. Third, capacity: a Tier 1 account needs real people, and a team that assigns forty Tier 1 accounts to two marketers is running Tier 3 with a different label. Fourth, review cadence: tiers should be revisited, typically every quarter, so that accounts move up when they warm and down when they go cold or are lost.

## Building the Ideal Customer Profile and Target Account List

### Start from evidence, not aspiration

An ICP should describe the customers who already succeed with you, not the logos the leadership team would like to have. The most reliable method is to analyze closed-won and closed-lost deals and current customers over the past two or three years.

A practical procedure:

1. Export closed-won and closed-lost opportunities with their account attributes: industry, employee count, revenue band, region, technology in use, ownership type, growth rate, and any other field you trust.
2. Add outcome measures for customers: retention, expansion, time to value, support burden and gross margin. A customer that buys but churns in a year is not ideal.
3. Compare the attribute distributions of your best customers against lost deals and poor-fit customers. Look for attributes where the best customers are clearly over-represented.
4. Interview a handful of your best customers and sellers to understand why those patterns exist. Data tells you that mid-sized manufacturers succeed; interviews tell you it is because they have a specific compliance requirement your product handles well.
5. Write the ICP as a short set of criteria with clear thresholds, plus a statement of the problem the ideal customer has, and the triggers that make the problem urgent.
6. Define disqualifiers explicitly: characteristics that predict failure, such as a required integration you do not support.

### From ICP to account list

With an ICP in hand, the team sources candidate accounts from the CRM, from commercial company databases and from sellers' knowledge. It then removes accounts that are current customers (unless the program is for expansion), accounts in active opportunities owned by another program, and accounts that fail disqualifiers. The result is the **total addressable account list**. From it, the team selects the accounts it will actively pursue in the coming period, which is the **target account list**.

Two sizes matter. The total addressable list tells leadership how large the opportunity is. The target list must match capacity: if sellers can work only a certain number of accounts well, the list should not be much larger than that number. A common failure is a target list so large that every account receives the same thin treatment.

### Data quality

Account lists depend on clean account records. Before launch, deduplicate accounts, establish parent and child relationships for subsidiaries, standardize industry codes, and confirm ownership in the CRM. An ABM program that cannot tell whether two records belong to the same company will misreport engagement and send conflicting messages.

## Account Scoring and Prioritization

Account scoring converts many signals into a single comparable number so that the team can rank accounts and assign tiers. A transparent weighted model is easier to defend and improve than a black box, especially early in a program.

### A weighted scoring model

The general form is:

Account score = sum over factors of (weight of factor multiplied by factor score)

where each factor score is normalized to a common scale such as 0 to 100 and the weights sum to 1. Typical factors include:

- **Fit**: how closely the account matches the ICP. Static or slow-changing.
- **Intent**: evidence that the account is researching the problem you solve, from third-party intent data, search behavior or industry events.
- **Engagement**: first-party interactions such as website visits by known or identified companies, content consumption, event attendance, email replies and meetings.
- **Relationship**: existing connections, such as former customers who now work at the account, partner relationships or prior opportunities.

A useful mental model is that fit tells you whether an account should buy, intent and engagement tell you whether it might be buying now, and relationship tells you whether you can get in. Timing matters as much as fit: a perfectly fitting account with no active initiative may not buy for years.

### Calibrating weights

Initial weights are judgement calls. Over time, calibrate them against outcomes: compare the scores of accounts that became opportunities against those that did not. If accounts with high engagement but low fit rarely convert, reduce the engagement weight or add a fit threshold below which an account cannot rise above Tier 3. Many teams use a gate structure, where an account must pass a minimum fit score before intent or engagement counts at all. This prevents a burst of activity from an unsuitable company, such as a competitor or a student researching a topic, from pushing it to the top of the list.

### Score decay

Engagement and intent signals lose value over time. A visit last week means more than a visit six months ago. A simple approach is to count only signals within a rolling window, such as ninety days, or to apply a decay factor so that each signal's contribution halves after a defined period. Without decay, accounts accumulate stale points and the ranking stops reflecting reality.

## Buying Groups and Stakeholder Mapping

B2B purchases are made by groups. The people involved commonly include:

- **Economic buyer**: the person who controls the budget and gives final approval.
- **Champion**: an internal advocate who wants the change and will sell it inside the organization.
- **Technical evaluator**: the person who assesses whether the solution works with existing systems and meets requirements.
- **End users**: the people whose daily work will change.
- **Procurement and legal**: the people who manage vendor risk, terms and price.
- **Influencers**: advisors, consultants or peers whose opinion carries weight.
- **Blockers**: people who prefer the status quo or a competing option, sometimes for good reasons.

SiriusDecisions (now part of Forrester) introduced the idea of the **demand unit**: the buying group associated with a particular need inside an account, rather than the account as a whole. A large enterprise may contain several demand units for the same product, such as different divisions with separate budgets, each progressing through its own buying stages. Thinking in demand units prevents the error of assuming that one engaged person means the whole account is engaged.

### Building a stakeholder map

For each Tier 1 and many Tier 2 accounts, the team builds a stakeholder map: a list of the people in the buying group with their role, their likely priorities, their current relationship with you (unknown, aware, engaged, supportive, opposed), and the next action for each. Sources include the CRM, professional networking sites, the account's own publications, conference speaker lists and conversations with existing contacts.

The map exposes **coverage gaps**. If the team has three engaged contacts, all in IT, and no relationship with finance or operations, the deal is exposed. The map turns that gap into specific plays: a finance-oriented business case brief, an operations peer roundtable, or an introduction requested from the champion.

### Messages by role

Each role cares about different things. The economic buyer cares about business results, risk and strategic fit. The technical evaluator cares about integration, security and effort. End users care about whether their work becomes easier. Procurement cares about terms, vendor stability and comparable pricing. An ABM content plan therefore produces a small set of role-specific assets around a single account narrative, so that every stakeholder hears a consistent story told in terms that matter to them.

## Account Insight and Account Planning

### What counts as insight

An insight is not a fact about the account; it is a conclusion that connects facts to a problem you can help solve. "The account opened two new warehouses this year" is a fact. "The account's expansion is outpacing its current inventory system, which was designed for a single site, so it will likely face stock accuracy problems during peak season" is an insight. Insight-led content earns attention because it tells the stakeholder something useful about their own situation.

Sources of insight include annual reports and investor presentations for public companies, press releases, job postings (which reveal priorities and technology choices), regulatory filings, executive interviews and articles, industry analyst commentary, conversations with existing customers in the same industry, and your own sellers' notes. For private companies with little public information, job postings and conversations are often the richest sources.

### The account plan

A Tier 1 account plan is a working document, typically a few pages, that the account team maintains. A sound plan contains:

1. **Account summary**: what the organization does, its size, structure and current relationship with you.
2. **Business priorities**: the account's stated goals and the pressures it faces.
3. **Hypothesis**: the problem you believe you can solve for them, and why now.
4. **Buying group map**: stakeholders, roles, relationships and gaps.
5. **Competitive situation**: incumbents and alternatives, including doing nothing.
6. **Objectives**: what the program aims to achieve in this account in the next period, expressed in account-level terms such as "secure a discovery meeting with the operations director" or "create a qualified opportunity in the logistics division."
7. **Plays**: the sequence of activities, owners and dates.
8. **Measures**: the engagement and pipeline indicators the team will review.
9. **Risks and dependencies**.

Account plans should be reviewed jointly by sales and marketing on a regular cadence, often every two to four weeks for Tier 1. A plan that is written once and never revisited has no value.

## Methods And Frameworks

Account-based marketing draws on several methods and models to target and engage high-value accounts. Each is useful within limits, and each has a typical failure mode.

**Fit, intent and timing prioritization.** Many teams prioritize accounts by combining three questions: does the account fit the product or service, is there evidence of intent to buy, and where is the account in its buying cycle? This lens is the logic behind the scoring model described earlier. Failure mode: over-reliance on intent data of uncertain quality, which can make random noise look like buying activity.

**The demand unit waterfall.** The SiriusDecisions Demand Unit Waterfall model frames the buyer's journey around the buying group for a specific need and tracks how demand units progress from targeted to engaged, qualified, opportunity and closed. It is applied to understand the buying journey and identify key decision-makers. Failure mode: neglecting to update the definitions and stage criteria as the business changes, so that the model reports progress that does not exist.

**Funnel stage content planning.** The top-of-funnel, middle-of-funnel and bottom-of-funnel (TOFU, MOFU, BOFU) framework categorizes content and engagement by stage: awareness and problem framing at the top, evaluation and solution comparison in the middle, and purchase justification and risk reduction at the bottom. In ABM it is applied per buying group rather than per individual. Failure mode: assuming a neat linear progression, when buying groups move back and forth between stages.

**Customer journey mapping.** Journey mapping visualizes the account's experience across all touchpoints, from first awareness through onboarding and renewal. It reveals gaps, such as a strong pre-sale experience followed by a weak handoff to implementation. Failure mode: building the map once from internal assumptions and never validating it with customers.

**A structured implementation sequence.** Practitioners commonly implement ABM in an ordered sequence: secure executive alignment, define the ICP and target accounts, tier the accounts, research and map buying groups, design plays and content, align sales and marketing on roles and service levels, execute, and measure and refine. The specific number of steps varies by author and consultancy; the principle that matters is that later steps depend on earlier ones. Failure mode: skipping steps, especially alignment and account selection, or failing to revisit the target account list regularly.

Across all of these methods, the most frequent failures are inadequate data quality, insufficient personalization and weak alignment between sales and marketing. Understanding each framework's purpose and pitfalls matters more than which name a team gives it.

## Orchestrating Plays Across Channels

A **play** is a coordinated sequence of activities aimed at a specific account objective, such as opening a new stakeholder, accelerating a stalled opportunity or expanding into a new division. Orchestration is the discipline of sequencing those activities so that each one reinforces the others rather than competing for attention.

### Anatomy of a play

Every play should specify:

- **Objective**: the account-level outcome, stated in observable terms.
- **Audience**: the accounts and roles involved.
- **Trigger**: what starts the play, such as a funding event, a new executive hire, an intent spike or a contract renewal date.
- **Sequence**: the activities, in order, with timing and owners.
- **Exit criteria**: what ends the play, whether success, failure or a handoff to another play.
- **Measures**: what the team will look at to judge it.

### A sample play: opening a new buying group

Objective: a first meeting with the operations leader at each of fifteen Tier 2 accounts in the cold-chain logistics cluster within eight weeks.

1. Week 1: targeted advertising to operations roles at the fifteen accounts, featuring a short benchmark report on temperature-excursion losses in cold chain logistics.
2. Week 2: personalized email from the account's assigned seller referencing a specific public development at the account and offering the benchmark report.
3. Week 3: direct mail of a printed version of the report with a handwritten note to the operations leader, for accounts where the address is confirmed and consent rules allow.
4. Week 4: a connection request and short message from the seller on a professional network, referencing the report.
5. Week 5: invitation to a small virtual roundtable of operations leaders from non-competing companies, hosted by your head of customer success and a current customer.
6. Weeks 6 to 8: follow-up calls by the seller to those who engaged; executive-to-executive email from your chief operating officer to the most engaged accounts.

Each touch has a reason to exist and refers to the previous one, so the experience feels like a coherent conversation rather than a series of unrelated messages.

### Orchestration principles

**Sequence matters.** Advertising before outreach builds familiarity so that the seller's email is not the first time the stakeholder has seen your name. Outreach after a stakeholder engages with content is more welcome than outreach out of the blue.

**One voice per account.** Without coordination, an account can receive a nurture email from marketing, a cold call from a new seller, and an event invitation from a partner in the same week, each with different messages. Assign a single account owner who approves contact plans.

**Respect attention.** More touches are not always better. Each touch should add value. Frequency caps on advertising and agreed limits on outreach per stakeholder protect the relationship.

**Use signals.** Programmatic tools can trigger plays when an account crosses an engagement threshold. Those triggers should route to a human who decides what to do, not simply launch more automated emails.

## Sales and Marketing Alignment and Governance

ABM changes how sales and marketing work together. In a lead-based model, the handoff point is the qualified lead; marketing's job ends where sales' job begins. In ABM, both teams work the same accounts at the same time, so they must agree on far more.

### The service level agreement

A written ABM service level agreement (SLA) records the commitments each team makes. Typical elements include:

- The target account list and tier assignments, and the process for changing them.
- Account ownership: which seller and which marketer own each account.
- Marketing commitments: research deliverables, plays per tier, response times for content requests, and reporting.
- Sales commitments: follow-up time when an account signal fires, participation in account planning, logging of activities and notes in the CRM.
- Shared definitions: what counts as an engaged account, a qualified account and an opportunity.
- Review cadence: weekly or biweekly account stand-ups, monthly program reviews, quarterly list and tier reviews.

### Shared goals

Alignment is easiest when both teams are measured on the same outcomes, such as pipeline created in target accounts and revenue won from them. If marketing is measured on leads and sales on revenue, each will optimize its own number and the program will suffer. Leadership must set shared targets and enforce them.

### The roles in an ABM team

A full ABM team commonly includes an ABM program lead who owns strategy and reporting; account-based marketers assigned to tiers or clusters; sellers and sales development representatives (SDRs) who own outreach; a marketing operations specialist who manages data, tools and integrations; content and creative specialists; and an executive sponsor. In smaller companies, one person may hold several roles, but every function must be covered.

## Measuring ABM: Metrics and Formulas

ABM is measured at the account level, and the metrics follow the account through stages. Lead counts and cost per lead are not suitable primary measures, because an ABM program can succeed while generating few leads in the traditional sense.

### Coverage

**Contact coverage** measures whether you know the right people.

Contact coverage = number of target accounts with at least N identified contacts in key buying roles divided by number of target accounts

N is set by tier; for example, at least three roles in Tier 1. A related measure, **buying group coverage**, counts the share of defined roles that have an identified, reachable contact.

### Awareness and engagement

**Account reach** is the share of target accounts in which at least one person was exposed to the program, for example by seeing an advertisement or receiving an email.

**Engaged account rate** = number of target accounts meeting the engagement threshold divided by number of target accounts

The engagement threshold should be defined in advance, for instance at least two distinct people from the account engaging meaningfully within thirty days. Defining engagement by breadth (number of people) as well as depth (intensity of interaction) prevents one curious individual from inflating the measure.

### Pipeline

**Opportunity rate** = number of target accounts with a qualified opportunity divided by number of target accounts

**Pipeline created** is the total value of new qualified opportunities in target accounts during the period.

**Win rate** = opportunities won divided by opportunities closed (won plus lost) in the period. Count only closed opportunities in the denominator, otherwise open deals distort the rate.

**Average deal size** = total value of won deals divided by number of won deals.

**Sales cycle length** = average days from opportunity creation to close for won deals.

### Pipeline velocity

Pipeline velocity summarizes how quickly a set of accounts generates revenue:

Pipeline velocity = (number of qualified opportunities multiplied by win rate multiplied by average deal size) divided by sales cycle length in days

The result is revenue per day. ABM programs often aim to improve several terms at once: more opportunities from a fixed account list, higher win rates through better buying group coverage, larger deals through broader solutions, and shorter cycles through earlier stakeholder alignment.

### Return

**Program return on investment (ROI)** = (attributable gross profit minus program cost) divided by program cost

Use gross profit rather than revenue when the program is judged as a business investment, and include all program costs: media, content, tools, events and the share of people's time devoted to the program. When only revenue is available, report **revenue return** (attributable revenue divided by cost) and label it clearly.

### Expansion and retention

For customers, ABM metrics include **net revenue retention** (revenue from a cohort of customers this period, including expansion, minus contraction and churn, divided by that cohort's revenue a year earlier), **expansion pipeline** and **number of active buying groups per customer**. Programs aimed at existing customers often deliver better returns than acquisition programs, because trust and access already exist.

### Attribution at the account level

Attributing revenue to ABM is hard because many touches across many people contribute to a single deal. Three approaches are common. **Influence reporting** counts revenue from accounts that received program touches before or during the opportunity; it shows reach but overstates causation. **Multi-touch attribution** divides credit among touches by a rule; it is better than single-touch but still assumes the touches caused the outcome. **Controlled comparison** compares target accounts with a matched set of similar accounts that did not receive the program; it is the most credible estimate of causal impact. A well-run program reports influence for operational management and uses controlled comparison to judge whether the program works at all.

## Technology and Data Stack

ABM does not require a particular vendor, but it does require certain capabilities. Understanding the categories lets a practitioner choose tools to fit the program rather than letting tools define it.

- **Customer relationship management (CRM)**: the system of record for accounts, contacts, opportunities and activities. ABM depends on clean account hierarchies and consistent ownership in the CRM.
- **Marketing automation platform**: manages email, landing pages, forms and contact-level engagement scoring.
- **Company and contact data providers**: supply firmographic and contact information to build and enrich account lists.
- **Intent data sources**: estimate which companies are researching particular topics, based on content consumption across publisher networks or on your own website. Intent data is probabilistic; treat it as a hint, not proof.
- **Account identification**: tools that infer which organization a website visitor belongs to, often from network information. Accuracy varies by company size and remote-work patterns.
- **Account-based advertising**: platforms that target ads to people at named companies. Professional networks offer company-list targeting, and specialist platforms extend this across websites.
- **Web personalization**: changes website content based on the visitor's identified account, industry or stage.
- **Sales engagement tools**: manage seller outreach sequences and log activity.
- **Analytics and reporting**: aggregate account-level engagement and pipeline data into dashboards.
- **AI assistants**: increasingly used to summarize account research, draft first versions of personalized messages and flag changes in engagement. They speed up work but require human review for accuracy and tone, and they must not be fed confidential information in ways that breach agreements or privacy rules.

A practical rule: start with the CRM and the data you already own, prove the program with manual effort on a small account list, and add tools only when a specific bottleneck appears.

## Budgeting and Resourcing ABM

### Budget by tier

Because personalization costs scale with depth, budget is allocated by tier rather than by channel. A common pattern is that Tier 1 receives a large share of budget spread across few accounts, Tier 2 a moderate share across more accounts, and Tier 3 a smaller share spread thinly. The right split depends on deal sizes and win rates in each tier, which is why Worked example 5 later in this chapter calculates expected return by tier.

### Cost per account

**Cost per target account** = tier budget divided by number of accounts in the tier

This number should be compared with the expected gross profit from an account in that tier. If a Tier 1 account costs a great deal to pursue but the expected value of winning it, multiplied by the probability of winning, is lower, the tier is overfunded or the account is mis-tiered.

### People time

The largest cost of ABM is often people, not media. A Tier 1 account may require many hours per month from a marketer, a seller, a solutions specialist and an executive. Include these hours when calculating program cost, and plan capacity before committing to account counts.

## Worked Examples

The following examples use illustrative numbers chosen for teaching. Real programs should substitute their own data. Each calculation has been checked.

### Worked example 1: Targeted content for a finance cluster

A B2B software company identifies 50 key accounts in the finance industry and creates personalized content for each: a short brief on how a regulation affects that account's operations, adapted from a cluster template. Content creation costs $500 per account.

Content cost = 50 multiplied by $500 = $25,000.

Over two quarters, 20% of the accounts engage with the content, meaning 10 accounts meet the engagement threshold. Five accounts, which is 10% of the original 50, become customers, with an average first-year contract value of $100,000.

Revenue = 5 multiplied by $100,000 = $500,000.

At a gross margin of 70%, gross profit is $350,000. Judged against content cost alone, the return looks extraordinary: ($350,000 minus $25,000) divided by $25,000 = 13.0, or 1,300%. But content was not the only cost. Suppose sales time, advertising and events attributable to these accounts cost a further $60,000, for a total program cost of $85,000.

ROI = ($350,000 minus $85,000) divided by $85,000 = 3.12, or about 312%.

Cost per customer acquired = $25,000 divided by 5 = $5,000 for content alone, or $85,000 divided by 5 = $17,000 on a full-cost basis.

The lesson: always state which costs are included. A return calculated on a single line item flatters the program and misleads decision-makers. Also note that half of the engaged accounts became customers, which suggests that engagement threshold and fit were well defined.

### Worked example 2: Account-based advertising to a technology segment

A company targets 200 accounts in the technology sector with account-based advertising on a professional network, spending $10,000. The campaign produces 1,000 clicks from people at 60 of the 200 accounts.

Cost per click = $10,000 divided by 1,000 = $10.

Cost per engaged account = $10,000 divided by 60 = about $167.

Two percent of clicks lead to a qualified opportunity after sales follow-up: 1,000 multiplied by 0.02 = 20 opportunities. Cost per opportunity = $10,000 divided by 20 = $500. With a 20% win rate, 4 deals close at an average of $50,000.

Influenced revenue = 4 multiplied by $50,000 = $200,000.

Return on ad spend (ROAS) = $200,000 divided by $10,000 = 20 to 1.

Two cautions apply. First, the advertising did not close those deals alone; sellers did significant work, so ROAS overstates the advertising's contribution. Second, in ABM the number of distinct engaged accounts (60 of 200, or 30%) is more meaningful than clicks, because 1,000 clicks from three accounts would be a very different result from 1,000 clicks spread across sixty.

### Worked example 3: Personalized email to healthcare decision-makers

A company targets 20 key accounts in healthcare. A researcher and a seller prepare personalized emails to decision-makers at each account: 5 emails per account, 100 in total. Because each email requires research, the fully loaded cost is $100 per email.

Cost = 100 multiplied by $100 = $10,000.

Results: 30% of emails are opened, which is 30 opens. Of those opens, 20% lead to a reply or a click to book time, which is 6 responses. Half of the responses become meetings: 3 meetings. One in three meetings converts to a customer, so 1 customer is won, with an average deal size of $50,000.

Revenue = 1 multiplied by $50,000 = $50,000.

Revenue ROI = ($50,000 minus $10,000) divided by $10,000 = 4.0, or 400%.

The lesson concerns small numbers. With 3 meetings and 1 customer, the result could easily have been 0 or 2 customers by chance. The team should not conclude that the approach yields exactly 400%; it should conclude that the approach is promising and repeat it on more accounts before scaling.

### Worked example 4: Scoring and ranking three accounts

An ABM team scores accounts on four factors, each from 0 to 100, with weights: fit 0.4, intent 0.3, engagement 0.2, relationship 0.1.

| Account | Fit | Intent | Engagement | Relationship |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| A | 90 | 40 | 30 | 20 |
| B | 70 | 85 | 60 | 50 |
| C | 50 | 90 | 80 | 10 |

Account A = 0.4 times 90 + 0.3 times 40 + 0.2 times 30 + 0.1 times 20 = 36 + 12 + 6 + 2 = 56.0.

Account B = 0.4 times 70 + 0.3 times 85 + 0.2 times 60 + 0.1 times 50 = 28 + 25.5 + 12 + 5 = 70.5.

Account C = 0.4 times 50 + 0.3 times 90 + 0.2 times 80 + 0.1 times 10 = 20 + 27 + 16 + 1 = 64.0.

Ranking: B (70.5), C (64.0), A (56.0). Account B combines good fit with strong current activity, so it is the best immediate target. Account A is an excellent fit but quiet; it belongs in a lower-intensity awareness play until signals appear. Account C is active but a weaker fit. If the team uses a fit gate of 60, Account C would be held in Tier 3 regardless of its activity, because high intent from a poor-fit account often reflects research that will not lead to a successful purchase.

### Worked example 5: Allocating budget across tiers

A company has an annual ABM budget of $600,000 and three tiers.

| Tier | Accounts | Budget share | Expected win rate | Average first-year deal |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 10 | 50% | 30% | $400,000 |
| 2 | 40 | 30% | 15% | $120,000 |
| 3 | 300 | 20% | 5% | $40,000 |

Tier 1 budget = $300,000, or $30,000 per account. Expected wins = 10 times 0.30 = 3. Expected revenue = 3 times $400,000 = $1,200,000. Revenue per budget dollar = 4.0.

Tier 2 budget = $180,000, or $4,500 per account. Expected wins = 40 times 0.15 = 6. Expected revenue = 6 times $120,000 = $720,000. Revenue per budget dollar = 4.0.

Tier 3 budget = $120,000, or $400 per account. Expected wins = 300 times 0.05 = 15. Expected revenue = 15 times $40,000 = $600,000. Revenue per budget dollar = 5.0.

Total expected revenue = $2,520,000, or 4.2 times the budget.

Interpretation: Tier 3 shows the highest revenue per dollar, which might tempt a planner to move money there. But the win rates are assumptions that depend on the spending; Tier 3's 5% may not hold if its budget is doubled and personalization becomes thinner relative to more accounts, and Tier 1 deals often bring larger lifetime value, strategic references and expansion that first-year revenue ignores. The right move is to test reallocations at the margin and measure, rather than assume returns are constant as spending changes.

### Worked example 6: Measuring a pilot against a matched control

A company runs an ABM pilot on 60 target accounts and selects 60 similar accounts, matched on industry, size and prior engagement, as a control group that receives only the usual marketing. After twelve months:

| Measure | Pilot | Control |
|---|---|---|
| Opportunity rate | 25% | 15% |
| Win rate | 30% | 25% |
| Average deal | $90,000 | $70,000 |
| Sales cycle | 150 days | 180 days |

Pilot opportunities = 60 times 0.25 = 15. Expected wins = 15 times 0.30 = 4.5. Revenue = 4.5 times $90,000 = $405,000, or $6,750 per account.

Control opportunities = 60 times 0.15 = 9. Expected wins = 9 times 0.25 = 2.25. Revenue = 2.25 times $70,000 = $157,500, or $2,625 per account.

Incremental revenue from the pilot = $405,000 minus $157,500 = $247,500. If the pilot cost $90,000, incremental revenue per dollar = $247,500 divided by $90,000 = 2.75.

Pipeline velocity, pilot = (15 times 0.30 times $90,000) divided by 150 = $2,700 per day.

Pipeline velocity, control = (9 times 0.25 times $70,000) divided by 180 = $875 per day.

The pilot's velocity is about 3.1 times the control's. Because the two groups were matched before the program began, the difference is a more credible estimate of program impact than an influence report. The fractional wins (4.5 and 2.25) are expected values; actual counts are whole numbers, and with groups this small the team should treat the estimate as indicative and continue measuring.

## Applications

Account-based marketing is used in practice to target high-value accounts and decision-makers with personalized content, messaging and engagement. The key principle across industries is to align marketing and sales with the specific needs of the target accounts, using data to inform and refine the approach, including intent signals that show which accounts are actively researching related solutions and account-based advertising to reach decision-makers and influencers.

**Technology.** Software and IT services firms use ABM to pursue large enterprises, for example by targeting a major bank's IT and risk functions with content on a specific modernization challenge, or by running cluster programs for mid-sized companies adopting a particular platform.

**Healthcare and life sciences.** Suppliers target hospital systems, medical groups and pharmaceutical companies with programs that respect strict regulatory requirements, long procurement cycles and clinical as well as administrative stakeholders.

**Financial services.** Firms selling to investment banks, private equity firms and asset managers build account-specific programs around regulatory change, operational risk and client reporting.

**Manufacturing and industrial.** Suppliers target procurement managers, plant leaders and product designers at specific manufacturers, often combining technical content with site visits and trade show meetings.

**Professional services.** Consulting, legal and accounting firms have practiced account-based relationship development for a long time and use ABM to coordinate partners, marketing and thought leadership around named clients.

**Expansion in existing customers.** One of the strongest applications is growing existing accounts: mapping divisions that do not yet buy, identifying new buying groups and using customer success stories from one division to open another. This is sometimes called land-and-expand.

**Partner and channel ABM.** Companies that sell through partners run joint account programs with selected resellers or alliance partners, sharing account lists and coordinating plays.

Across these applications, focusing on a smaller set of high-value accounts allows greater efficiency and depth, stronger relationships and better conversion, provided account selection and alignment are sound.

### Case study: Halvorsen Fleet Systems (a fictional company)

Halvorsen Fleet Systems is a fictional company created for this chapter. It sells fleet maintenance software to transport and logistics firms with between 200 and 5,000 vehicles. Annual contract values for new customers ranged widely, with most falling between $50,000 and $150,000.

**Problem.** Halvorsen's marketing team generated thousands of leads a year from webinars and gated reports, but sellers complained that most came from tiny operators or consultants, and the company's largest wins came from accounts sellers had pursued on their own. In the prior year, the company's 120 best-fit prospects produced 11 qualified opportunities and 2 wins, at an average first-year value of $85,000. Marketing and sales disagreed about lead quality in every weekly meeting.

**What was done.** Leadership commissioned a twelve-month ABM program with the following steps.

1. The team analyzed three years of closed deals and found that customers with mixed fleets of refrigerated and dry vehicles, operating across several depots, renewed at much higher rates and expanded more often. This became the core of the ICP.
2. From a total addressable list of about 900 companies, the team selected 120 target accounts: 12 in Tier 1, 48 in Tier 2 (split into three clusters by sub-industry) and 60 in Tier 3.
3. Each Tier 1 account received a written account plan and a monthly joint review. A marketer and a seller built stakeholder maps and found that in most accounts they knew only the maintenance manager; finance and operations leaders were unknown.
4. The content team produced a benchmark study on maintenance cost per vehicle by fleet type, using anonymized, aggregated customer data with customer permission, plus role-specific briefs for finance and operations.
5. Plays combined targeted advertising, seller outreach, a small invitation-only roundtable for operations directors and executive-to-executive letters for Tier 1.
6. Sales and marketing signed an SLA with shared definitions and a 48-hour follow-up commitment for engaged-account alerts.
7. To judge the program, the team compared the 120 accounts against their own performance in the prior year and against a matched set of 60 accounts outside the program.

**Measured results.** Over twelve months, the 120 target accounts produced 23 qualified opportunities and 6 wins, at an average first-year value of $112,000. First-year revenue from the target accounts rose from $170,000 (2 wins at $85,000) to $672,000 (6 wins at $112,000). The control group's results were similar to its prior year, which suggested the improvement was not simply a market upturn. Buying group coverage in Tier 1 rose from an average of 1.4 known roles to 4.2. Total program cost, including allocated staff time, was $210,000, giving first-year revenue of about 3.2 times cost, before counting renewals and expansion.

**Lessons.** First, the ICP change mattered more than any channel: better accounts made every later step more effective. Second, buying group coverage was the clearest leading indicator; deals with finance engaged early closed faster. Third, the shared SLA ended the lead-quality argument because both teams now measured the same accounts. Fourth, Tier 3 underperformed expectations; light-touch advertising alone produced awareness but few opportunities, and the team decided to shrink Tier 3 and promote engaged accounts to Tier 2 more aggressively.

## Legal, Privacy and Ethical Considerations

ABM depends on researching organizations and the people in them, which raises legal and ethical questions that practitioners must handle carefully.

**Personal data in B2B.** Business contact details, such as a named person's work email address and job title, are generally personal data under privacy laws such as the European Union's General Data Protection Regulation. Processing them requires a lawful basis, transparency about how the data is used and respect for individuals' rights, such as access and objection. Many organizations rely on legitimate interests for B2B prospecting, which requires a documented balancing test. Rules on electronic marketing to business addresses differ between jurisdictions, so practitioners should follow their legal team's guidance rather than assume one country's rules apply everywhere.

**Commercial email rules.** Many jurisdictions require commercial emails, including B2B emails, to identify the sender accurately, avoid deceptive subject lines and offer a working way to opt out. In the United States, the CAN-SPAM Act sets such requirements for commercial email.

**Tracking and identification.** Website tracking, cookies and account identification tools may require consent in some jurisdictions. Even where they are lawful, personalization that reveals more than the visitor expects, such as greeting a named individual by name on an anonymous visit, can feel intrusive and damage trust.

**Data sourcing.** Purchased contact and intent data should come from providers who can explain how they collected it and on what legal basis. Scraping data in breach of a website's terms or collecting data without notice creates legal and reputational risk.

**Honesty in personalization.** Personalization should reflect real understanding, not pretend familiarity. Claiming a relationship that does not exist, implying endorsement by a customer without permission or using aggregated customer data without consent are ethical breaches and may be legal ones.

**Confidential information.** Account research must never use confidential information obtained improperly, such as documents shared by a former employee of the account in breach of their obligations.

**Respect for attention.** Ethical ABM avoids overwhelming stakeholders. A coordinated program can feel like surveillance if it follows a person across every channel without offering value. The test is whether the stakeholder, seeing the full set of touches, would consider them reasonable and useful.

## Common Errors

Practitioners of account-based marketing often make mistakes that weaken their programs. The following are the most common, with their consequences.

**Misaligned sales and marketing.** Failing to align the two teams leads to disjointed communication, inconsistent messaging and conflicting outreach to the same stakeholders. Without shared goals, each team optimizes its own metrics and the account experience suffers.

**Poor target account selection.** Choosing accounts that do not match the ICP or the company's business goals, often because they are famous names, wastes the program's most expensive resources. Account selection should be evidence based and reviewed regularly.

**Insufficient personalization.** Using generic content across accounts, with only the company name changed, defeats the purpose of ABM. Personalization should reflect the account's actual situation, at a depth appropriate to the tier.

**Volume over quality.** Prioritizing lead volume rather than engagement from key decision-makers at chosen accounts reverts the program to lead-based marketing with a new label.

**Single-threading.** Focusing on one contact rather than the buying group neglects the complex decision-making in B2B purchasing and leaves deals exposed when that contact leaves or loses influence.

**Over-reliance on technology.** Buying an ABM platform before defining strategy, or letting automation replace human relationships, produces activity without understanding. Technology should support a defined process.

**Poor data management.** Duplicate account records, missing hierarchies and inaccurate contacts cause wrong targeting, inefficient execution and misleading reports.

**Inadequate measurement.** Failing to define and track account-level metrics such as engagement, opportunity rate and customer lifetime value makes it impossible to judge success, learn and hold the program accountable. Equally, claiming causal credit from influence reports alone overstates results.

**Siloed execution.** Running ABM separately from other marketing channels, customer success and sales creates a fragmented experience. The account should feel one coordinated company.

**Too many accounts per tier.** Assigning more Tier 1 accounts than the team can serve with real depth produces a program that is one-to-one in name only.

**Impatience.** ABM targets long sales cycles. Judging a program after one quarter on revenue alone often leads to abandoning it before it can work. Use leading indicators early and revenue measures later.

## Advanced

Account-based marketing continues to evolve with new data sources and technology, and several areas are of particular interest at advanced levels of practice.

**Account-based experience (ABX).** The field is moving toward emphasizing the full customer experience across the entire journey, from awareness through onboarding, adoption, renewal and advocacy. ABX treats marketing, sales and customer success as one account team, and measures success by long-term account value rather than by acquisition alone.

**Predictive account selection.** Data science methods, including classification models trained on historical wins and losses, can estimate which accounts resemble past successful customers and which are entering a buying window. The open question is how to balance model output with human judgement, especially when the model's training data reflects the company's past biases, such as having only ever sold to one region.

**Integrated data platforms.** Customer data platforms and data warehouses can unify account-level data from CRM, marketing automation, product usage and support systems. A unified view enables more precise targeting and better measurement, but also raises the stakes of data governance and privacy compliance.

**Product usage signals.** For software companies with free trials or freemium products, product usage by employees of a target account is a powerful buying signal. Combining product-led growth with ABM, sometimes called product-led sales, allows sellers to approach accounts where users already see value.

**AI-assisted personalization.** Large language models can draft account summaries, personalized messages and content variants at a scale that was previously impossible. This shifts the bottleneck from production to judgement: deciding what is true, relevant and appropriate. It also creates a risk that all vendors' personalized messages start to sound alike, which may lower the value of personalization itself.

**Attribution and incrementality.** Measuring ABM's causal impact on revenue and customer lifetime value remains an open problem. Matched controls, holdout tests on advertising and statistical models of account progression are improving practice, but small account counts limit statistical confidence.

**Conversational and event-based engagement.** Live chat, virtual events and small peer communities offer ways to engage buying groups that respond poorly to traditional outreach.

**Privacy and data governance.** Tighter privacy rules, browser restrictions on tracking and greater public sensitivity to surveillance are pushing ABM toward first-party data, consented relationships and value exchange rather than covert identification.

Open questions in the field include the optimal balance between human relationship-building and automation, the role of ABM in driving retention and expansion, and how to measure account-level return credibly when samples are small and sales cycles long.

## Summary

Account-based marketing focuses marketing and sales effort on a defined set of high-value organizations and the buying groups within them. It reverses the lead-based funnel: the team selects accounts first, then builds coverage, engagement and opportunities inside them. ABM programs are tiered, with one-to-one programs for a few strategic accounts, one-to-few programs for clusters with shared problems, and one-to-many programs that use technology to reach larger lists with light personalization. A sound program begins with an evidence-based ideal customer profile, a scored and tiered account list, and a clear map of each account's buying group. It runs coordinated plays that combine insight-led content, advertising, seller outreach and events, governed by a written agreement between sales and marketing with shared definitions and goals. It measures progress at the account level through coverage, engagement, opportunity rate, win rate, deal size, cycle time, pipeline velocity and return, and it uses matched comparisons to judge causal impact. Practitioners must respect privacy law and ethical limits on tracking and personalization, and must avoid the common errors of poor account selection, single-threading, technology-first thinking and premature judgement.

## Key terms

**Account-based marketing (ABM)**: a B2B strategy that concentrates marketing and sales resources on a defined set of target accounts and treats each account or cluster as a market.

**Target account list**: the set of accounts a program actively pursues during a defined period.

**Ideal customer profile (ICP)**: a description of the organizations most likely to buy, succeed with and remain loyal to an offer.

**Buyer persona**: a semi-fictional profile of a type of person within a buying group.

**Buying group**: the set of people who participate in a purchase decision inside an account.

**Demand unit**: the buying group associated with a specific need within an account, which may progress independently of other demand units in the same organization.

**Tier**: a level of ABM investment and personalization, commonly one-to-one, one-to-few or one-to-many.

**Account scoring**: a formula that combines fit, intent, engagement and relationship signals into a single number for ranking accounts.

**Intent data**: signals suggesting that an organization is researching a topic or solution, collected from first-party or third-party sources.

**Stakeholder map**: a structured view of the people in an account's buying group, their roles, relationships and next actions.

**Play**: a coordinated sequence of activities designed to achieve a specific account objective.

**Orchestration**: the sequencing and coordination of plays and touches across channels and teams so that they reinforce one another.

**Service level agreement (SLA)**: a written agreement between sales and marketing defining responsibilities, response times and shared definitions.

**Engaged account rate**: the share of target accounts that meet a predefined engagement threshold.

**Pipeline velocity**: the number of qualified opportunities multiplied by win rate and average deal size, divided by sales cycle length, expressing revenue generated per day.

**Account-based experience (ABX)**: an extension of ABM that coordinates the full customer experience across marketing, sales and customer success.

**Matched control group**: a set of accounts similar to the targets that does not receive the program, used to estimate incremental impact.

**Net revenue retention**: revenue from an existing customer cohort, including expansion and net of contraction and churn, divided by that cohort's revenue from a prior period.

## Review questions

1. How does the unit of measure in account-based marketing differ from the unit of measure in lead-based demand generation?
2. What conditions make a company a good candidate for an ABM program, and what conditions make it a poor one?
3. How do one-to-one, one-to-few and one-to-many ABM differ in account count, personalization depth and resource requirements?
4. Why should an ideal customer profile be built from closed-won, closed-lost and retention data rather than from a list of desirable brand names?
5. What is the purpose of a fit gate in an account scoring model?
6. Why do engagement and intent signals need to decay over time in a scoring model?
7. What is a demand unit, and why does thinking in demand units prevent errors in large accounts?
8. What is the difference between a fact about an account and an insight about it?
9. What elements should a play specify before it is launched?
10. Which commitments typically appear in an ABM service level agreement between sales and marketing?
11. How is pipeline velocity calculated, and which of its terms can an ABM program influence?
12. Why is a matched control group a more credible way to judge ABM impact than an influence report?
13. In Worked example 1, why did the ROI fall from 1,300% to about 312%?
14. What privacy obligations apply when an ABM team processes the work email addresses and job titles of individuals?
15. What are three signs that an ABM program has drifted back into lead-based marketing?

## Answer key

1. ABM measures progress by account (coverage, engagement, opportunities and revenue within named accounts), while lead-based demand generation measures individual leads and their conversion through a funnel.
2. Good candidates have high contract values, a nameable finite market, multi-stakeholder purchases, long cycles and leaders willing to share goals; poor candidates sell low-priced or impulse products to very large numbers of small buyers, or cannot yet say which customers succeed.
3. One-to-one covers a handful of strategic accounts with fully custom research, content and dedicated teams; one-to-few covers dozens to a couple of hundred accounts grouped by shared problem with cluster-level content; one-to-many covers hundreds or thousands with technology-driven light personalization and the lowest cost per account.
4. Evidence from actual outcomes shows which organizations buy, succeed and stay, while aspirational lists reflect prestige rather than fit and lead to wasted spending on accounts that rarely convert or churn quickly.
5. A fit gate prevents accounts that are a poor match for the product from rising to high tiers on the strength of activity alone, since high intent from poor-fit accounts often reflects research that will not lead to a successful purchase.
6. Signals lose relevance with time, so without decay accounts accumulate stale points and the ranking stops reflecting current buying activity.
7. A demand unit is the buying group tied to a specific need within an account; recognizing it prevents the team from assuming that engagement from one division or person means the whole account is engaged or in market.
8. A fact is an observation, such as a new warehouse opening, while an insight connects facts to a problem you can help solve, such as the likely strain on an inventory system designed for one site.
9. A play should specify its objective, audience, trigger, sequence of activities with timing and owners, exit criteria and measures.
10. Typical commitments include the account list and tiers, account ownership, marketing deliverables and response times, sales follow-up times and logging duties, shared definitions of engaged and qualified accounts and opportunities, and the review cadence.
11. Pipeline velocity equals the number of qualified opportunities times win rate times average deal size, divided by sales cycle length in days; ABM can influence all four terms by creating more opportunities, improving buying group coverage, broadening deals and aligning stakeholders earlier.
12. Influence reports count revenue from any touched account and so confuse correlation with causation, while a matched control shows what similar accounts achieved without the program, isolating its incremental effect.
13. The 1,300% figure counted only content cost; adding $60,000 of sales, advertising and event costs raised total cost to $85,000, so the ROI on full cost was ($350,000 minus $85,000) divided by $85,000, about 312%.
14. Such data is generally personal data under laws like the GDPR, so the team needs a lawful basis such as documented legitimate interests, must be transparent about its use, must honor rights such as objection and access, and must follow the electronic marketing rules of each relevant jurisdiction.
15. Signs include reporting lead counts or cost per lead as the main success measure, measuring clicks instead of engaged accounts, a target list too large for real personalization, and plays that are generic nurture emails sent to the whole list.

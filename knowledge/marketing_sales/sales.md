# Sales

Selling is the work of helping a buyer reach a decision that improves their situation, and of making sure the seller is paid fairly for the value delivered. This chapter treats sales as a system with measurable stages, not as a talent some people are born with. You will learn how revenue is produced stage by stage, how to choose whom to sell to, how to open conversations, how to qualify and run discovery, how to present value in the buyer's terms, how to handle objections and risk, how to negotiate and close, how to grow accounts after the first sale, and how to forecast honestly. The chapter ends with a development path from first-year seller to revenue leader, a full case study, the key terms, review questions and an answer key.

All companies, people and figures in the examples and case studies are hypothetical and were built for teaching. Where the chapter refers to laws, it describes them in general terms; a seller should confirm the rules that apply in their own market with qualified counsel.

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter you should be able to:

1. Describe a sale as a sequence of buyer decisions and map each stage of a revenue system to its inputs, outputs, conversion rate and owner.
2. Build pipeline math backwards from a revenue target and calculate sales velocity.
3. Write an ideal customer profile that excludes poor-fit buyers as clearly as it includes good ones.
4. Plan outreach that earns a first conversation and complies with common consent and anti-spam rules.
5. Qualify an opportunity with a structured framework and decide when to disqualify.
6. Run a discovery conversation that moves from a surface complaint to a quantified business impact.
7. Build a cost-of-inaction and return-on-investment case the buyer can defend internally.
8. Classify objections, find the risk underneath each one and respond without pressure tactics.
9. Use mutual action plans and clear commitments to close, and avoid manipulative closing techniques.
10. Prepare for a negotiation with walk-away points, tradeable concessions and margin awareness.
11. Manage multi-stakeholder deals by mapping roles, building a champion and handling blockers.
12. Measure retention and expansion with gross and net revenue retention.
13. Produce a weighted forecast and explain the difference between pipeline, commit and best case.
14. Apply ethical and legal limits, including the safe use of AI in selling.

## 1. What Selling Is

A sale happens when a buyer decides that an offer is worth more to them than what they give up for it: money, time, attention, the effort of change and the risk of being wrong. The seller's job is to help the buyer see their situation clearly, judge whether the offer improves it, and move through the decision without avoidable friction. When the offer does not improve the buyer's situation, the seller's job is to say so and walk away. That last part separates professional selling from persuasion for its own sake.

Two ideas sit underneath every effective sales conversation. First, people act to move away from a problem or toward a gain, and the problem usually has to feel more costly than the change before anything happens. A buyer who is mildly annoyed rarely buys; a buyer who can see that the annoyance costs them a measurable amount each month often does. Second, decisions are made with both feeling and reasoning. Feeling creates urgency and trust; reasoning lets the buyer justify the decision to themselves and to others. A seller who only appeals to feeling produces regret and cancellations. A seller who only presents logic produces polite interest and no decision.

Modern selling is diagnostic. Buyers can read product information on their own, compare prices and see reviews before they ever speak to a seller. What they cannot easily do alone is apply that information to their own situation, see the costs they have stopped noticing, and organize the people inside their company who must agree. The seller adds value by diagnosing before prescribing. A doctor who prescribes before examining a patient would be reckless; a seller who presents before understanding the buyer's situation is making the same mistake.

Selling also differs by motion. In a transactional sale the price is low, one person decides, and the cycle takes minutes to days; the seller's work is speed, clarity and removing friction. In a consultative sale the problem is specific to the buyer, several people are involved and the cycle takes weeks to months; the seller's work is discovery, value building and coordinating stakeholders. In an enterprise sale the purchase changes how a large organization operates, procurement and legal teams participate, and the cycle can run longer than a year; the seller's work includes project management of the buyer's own decision process. The principles in this chapter apply to all three, with more weight on structure as the deal grows.

## 2. The Revenue System

A company's revenue is the output of a chain of stages. A typical business-to-business chain runs from target accounts to contacted accounts, to first meetings, to qualified opportunities, to proposals, to won deals, to onboarded customers, to renewals and expansions. Each stage has an input, an output, a conversion rate between them, a time spent in the stage, a cost and an owner. When the stages are written down this way, problems become visible. A team that books plenty of meetings but converts few of them into qualified opportunities has a targeting or discovery problem, not an effort problem. A team that wins deals but loses customers within a year has a fit or delivery problem that more prospecting will only make worse.

The most useful single number for the speed of a revenue system is sales velocity:

Sales velocity = (number of qualified opportunities × win rate × average deal size) ÷ sales cycle length in days

Velocity is revenue produced per day by the current pipeline. It shows that four levers matter: more qualified opportunities, a higher win rate, a larger deal size and a shorter cycle. Teams often pull only the first lever, because more activity feels like progress. The other three are usually cheaper to move.

### Worked Example 1: Building Pipeline Math Backwards from a Target

A software company sells a scheduling product to multi-location service businesses. Leadership wants $600,000 of new annual contract value in the next quarter. Historical data shows an average deal size of $20,000, a win rate of 25% from qualified opportunity to closed deal, a 50% conversion from first meeting to qualified opportunity, and a 2% conversion from contacted prospect to first meeting. The team has four sellers and the average cycle is 90 days.

Step 1, deals needed: $600,000 ÷ $20,000 = 30 won deals.

Step 2, qualified opportunities needed: 30 ÷ 0.25 = 120 qualified opportunities.

Step 3, first meetings needed: 120 ÷ 0.50 = 240 first meetings.

Step 4, prospects to contact: 240 ÷ 0.02 = 12,000 contacted prospects.

Step 5, per seller: 240 meetings ÷ 4 sellers = 60 meetings each in a 13-week quarter, or about 4.6 per week.

Step 6, pipeline coverage: 120 opportunities × $20,000 = $2,400,000 of pipeline, which is 4 times the target. Coverage ratios are only meaningful if the win rate behind them is real; a team that claims 4× coverage with stale opportunities is fooling itself.

Step 7, velocity: (120 × 0.25 × $20,000) ÷ 90 = $600,000 ÷ 90 ≈ $6,667 per day.

Now compare two improvements. If coaching raises the win rate to 30%, velocity becomes (120 × 0.30 × $20,000) ÷ 90 = $720,000 ÷ 90 = $8,000 per day. If better qualification instead cuts the cycle to 75 days at the original 25% win rate, velocity becomes $600,000 ÷ 75 = $8,000 per day. Either change is worth 20% more revenue per day without a single extra cold contact. The lesson is to diagnose which lever is weakest before asking people to work harder.

### Stage Definitions and Exit Criteria

Pipeline math only works if stages mean the same thing to everyone. Each stage should have exit criteria written as facts about the buyer, not activities of the seller. "Sent proposal" is a seller activity. "Economic buyer has reviewed the proposal and agreed to a decision date" is a buyer fact. When stages are defined by buyer facts, a manager can inspect any opportunity and see whether it truly belongs where it sits. A simple set might be:

- Qualified: a named problem, a person who owns it, a reason to act this quarter, and a fit with the ideal customer profile.
- Discovery complete: the business impact is quantified, decision criteria and the decision process are known, and the economic buyer is identified.
- Solution validated: the buyer has confirmed that the proposed approach solves the problem, usually after a demonstration or trial with agreed success criteria.
- Proposal: commercial terms are presented to the economic buyer and a decision date is agreed.
- Commit: the buyer has stated the intention to sign and the remaining steps (legal review, signature) are scheduled.

## 3. The Ideal Customer Profile

The ideal customer profile, usually shortened to ICP, describes the accounts most likely to buy, succeed and stay. It is built from evidence: look at the customers who bought quickly, reached value quickly, renewed and expanded, and find what they share. Common attributes include industry, company size, geography, the systems they already use, the presence of a particular role, a triggering event such as a new location or a regulatory change, and a level of operational maturity.

A good ICP is as useful for exclusion as for inclusion. If customers with fewer than three locations churn at twice the rate of larger ones, the profile should say so, and sellers should stop pursuing them even when they are easy to sign. Easy-to-sign poor-fit customers are expensive: they consume onboarding time, generate support tickets, churn, and leave reviews that discourage good-fit buyers.

Inside each target account sit buyer personas: the roles involved in a decision. A persona describes what the person is measured on, what frustrates them, which words they use for their problem, what they fear about change and what proof they trust. A finance leader may care about payback period and audit risk; an operations manager about daily workload; an end user about whether the new tool will make their day harder before it makes it easier. Messaging and discovery questions should change by persona.

Write the ICP down and review it each quarter against won, lost and churned deals. Profiles drift as products and markets change, and a profile nobody revisits becomes a guess.

## 4. Prospecting and Outreach

Prospecting is the work of creating first conversations with people who match the ICP. Inbound prospecting responds to people who have raised their hand by downloading something, requesting a demo or asking a question. Outbound prospecting starts the conversation by phone, email, social messages, events or referrals. Most healthy teams use both, because inbound volume depends on marketing reach and outbound fills the gaps with accounts chosen on purpose.

Effective outreach is relevant, brief and specific. Relevance comes from a reason the message is going to this person now: a trigger event, a known problem in their role or industry, or a mutual connection. Brevity respects the reader. Specificity shows research. A strong first message usually contains one observation about the buyer's situation, one sentence about a result similar companies achieved, and one low-effort request such as a short call or a question that can be answered in a line. Generic messages that open with the seller's company history are deleted.

A sequence is a planned series of touches over several weeks, mixing channels. A common structure is six to ten touches over three to four weeks, each adding something new: a useful observation, a short case, a relevant question. Each touch should stand on its own, because the buyer may not have read the earlier ones. A sequence should end with a clear, polite close-out message; people often reply to the last message precisely because it removes pressure.

Referrals are the highest-converting form of outbound. A satisfied customer who introduces a peer transfers some of their trust to the seller. Ask for referrals when value has been demonstrated, be specific about the kind of person you want to meet, and make the introduction easy by drafting it for the customer.

### Consent and Anti-Spam Rules

Outreach is regulated in most markets, and the rules differ by channel and country. In the United States, commercial email falls under the CAN-SPAM Act, which prohibits misleading header information and deceptive subject lines, requires the message to identify itself as an advertisement where applicable, requires a valid physical postal address, and requires a working opt-out that is honored promptly. Calls and texts to mobile phones using automated dialing systems or prerecorded voices are restricted by the Telephone Consumer Protection Act and generally require prior consent, and telemarketing calls must respect the National Do Not Call Registry. In the European Union and the United Kingdom, data protection law requires a lawful basis for processing personal data, gives people the right to object to direct marketing, and is supplemented by electronic marketing rules that often require consent. Canada's anti-spam law generally requires consent before commercial electronic messages are sent. Rules change and contain exceptions, so a company should maintain written outreach policies reviewed by counsel, record consent and opt-outs in its CRM, and suppress opted-out contacts across every tool.

## 5. Qualification

Qualification decides whether an opportunity deserves the seller's time. Time is the seller's scarcest resource, and every hour spent on a deal that cannot close is an hour taken from one that can. Qualification is therefore not a gate the buyer must pass to earn attention; it is a shared check on whether working together makes sense.

Several frameworks give structure. BANT asks about Budget, Authority, Need and Timeline. It is simple and suits smaller deals, though it can feel like an interrogation if asked as a checklist, and budget often does not exist until value is proven. MEDDICC, used in complex business-to-business sales, covers Metrics (the measurable outcome the buyer expects), Economic buyer (the person who can approve the spending), Decision criteria (how options will be judged), Decision process (the steps and people involved in deciding), Identify pain (the problem and its cost), Champion (an insider who wants the change and will sell it internally), and Competition (the alternatives, including doing nothing). Other frameworks rename or regroup these ideas, but the core questions are stable: Is there a real problem? Does it cost enough to act on? Is there someone with authority who cares? Is there a reason to act now? Can we solve it better than the alternatives?

Disqualification is a skill. A seller who closes out a poor opportunity early, politely and with a clear reason, keeps the pipeline honest and earns respect. A useful habit is to write, for every open opportunity, the single biggest reason it might not close, and the next step that will test that risk. Opportunities with no answer to "why now?" should be moved to a nurture list rather than kept in the forecast.

## 6. Discovery

Discovery is the conversation, or series of conversations, in which the seller learns the buyer's situation well enough to know whether and how to help. It is the stage where most deals are won or lost, even though the outcome only becomes visible later.

Good discovery starts with preparation: research the company, the person and the likely problems before the call, so that time is spent on what cannot be found in public. Open by agreeing on the purpose and length of the meeting and on what will happen at the end, for example, "If it looks like a fit, we'll schedule a session with your operations lead; if not, I'll tell you so." This simple agreement, sometimes called an upfront contract, reduces the buyer's guard because it removes the fear of being pushed.

A widely taught structure for questions is SPIN, which stands for Situation, Problem, Implication and Need-payoff. Situation questions collect facts ("How are orders entered today?"). They are necessary but tiring, so ask only the ones research cannot answer. Problem questions surface difficulties ("Where does that process break down?"). Implication questions explore consequences ("When an order is entered wrong, what happens next, and who has to fix it?"). Need-payoff questions let the buyer state the value of solving it ("If those errors stopped, what would that free your team to do?"). The order matters: buyers rarely feel urgency from a problem until they have described its consequences in their own words.

A related technique is the pain funnel: start with the surface complaint, ask for an example, ask how long it has been happening, ask what it has cost, ask what they have already tried, and ask how it affects them personally. Each layer moves from a vague annoyance to a specific, owned, quantified problem. The personal layer matters because organizations do not buy; people inside them do, and each has a stake in the outcome.

Listening is the core skill. Summarize what you heard in the buyer's words and ask whether you have it right. Label emotions you observe ("It sounds like this has been frustrating for your team") without exaggerating them. Ask follow-up questions instead of jumping to the product the moment a problem appears. A useful rule is that the buyer should speak for most of a discovery call.

### Worked Example 2: Quantifying the Cost of Inaction

During discovery, a wholesale distributor explains that three staff members re-key orders from email into its order system. Each spends about 15 hours a week on this work. The fully loaded cost of each staff member is $38 per hour. The company processes 9,000 orders a year, about 2% of them contain entry errors, and each error costs roughly $140 to correct through returns, reshipping and credits. The seller's product automates order capture for $3,000 per month ($36,000 per year), plus a one-time implementation fee of $12,000. Based on similar customers, the seller expects to remove about 80% of the manual labor and 75% of the errors, and asks the buyer to confirm those assumptions.

Labor cost today: 3 people × 15 hours = 45 hours per week. 45 × $38 = $1,710 per week. Over 50 working weeks: $1,710 × 50 = $85,500 per year.

Error cost today: 9,000 × 0.02 = 180 errors. 180 × $140 = $25,200 per year.

Total annual cost of the current process: $85,500 + $25,200 = $110,700.

Expected annual savings: labor 0.80 × $85,500 = $68,400; errors 0.75 × $25,200 = $18,900; total $87,300.

First-year net benefit: $87,300 − $36,000 subscription − $12,000 implementation = $39,300.

First-year return on investment: $39,300 ÷ $48,000 total first-year cost ≈ 81.9%.

Payback period on the implementation fee: monthly savings are $87,300 ÷ 12 = $7,275; after the $3,000 monthly subscription, net monthly benefit is $4,275; $12,000 ÷ $4,275 ≈ 2.8 months.

In later years, without the implementation fee, the annual net benefit rises to $87,300 − $36,000 = $51,300. The seller presents these figures with the buyer's own inputs, shows the assumptions openly, and invites the buyer to change any number they disagree with. A business case the buyer helped build is one they can defend to their finance team; a case the seller produced alone is easy to dismiss.

## 7. Presenting Value

A presentation or demonstration should answer one question: does this solve the problem we discussed, in a way that fits how this company works? Everything that does not serve that question is noise. The most common mistake is the feature tour, in which the seller shows everything the product can do. Feature tours bury the relevant parts under irrelevant ones and imply the seller did not listen.

A strong structure is to restate the buyer's situation and goals as heard in discovery, confirm they are still accurate, then show how the solution addresses each priority in order of importance, using the buyer's data or scenarios wherever possible. For each point, connect a capability to a benefit to a measurable outcome: "The order inbox reads incoming emails and drafts the order (capability), so your team reviews instead of typing (benefit), which in similar companies removed about four-fifths of entry time (outcome)." Stop after each priority to check understanding and invite questions.

Proof matters more than claims. Useful forms of proof include customer stories from similar companies, references the buyer can call, trial periods with agreed success criteria, third-party reviews, and guarantees. Proof should match the buyer's specific doubts: a buyer worried about adoption wants to hear from users, while a buyer worried about security wants documentation and an audit report.

Differentiate against the real alternative. The competitor in most deals is not another vendor but the status quo: doing nothing, doing it manually, or asking an internal team to build something. A seller who only compares their product to a rival's misses the larger risk that the buyer decides not to change at all. The cost-of-inaction work from discovery is the main defense against that outcome.

## 8. Objections and Buyer Risk

An objection is a signal that the buyer sees a risk or a gap. Treating objections as obstacles to overcome makes buyers defensive. Treating them as information to understand makes the conversation more honest and often reveals the real issue.

Most objections fall into a few classes. Price objections ("It's too expensive") usually mean the value has not been made clear or the budget sits with someone else. Timing objections ("Not right now") usually mean the problem does not yet feel urgent or another priority is ahead of it. Trust objections ("I'm not sure this will work for us") point to a lack of proof or a past bad experience. Competitor objections ("We're also looking at another vendor") ask for differentiation. Status-quo objections ("What we have is fine") mean the cost of the problem has not been felt. Authority objections ("I need to check with my manager") reveal that the right person is not in the conversation.

A dependable response pattern has four parts. Acknowledge the concern without arguing ("That's a fair concern"). Clarify it with a question, because the first statement of an objection is rarely the whole story ("When you say expensive, is that compared with another option, or with the budget you have set aside?"). Respond to the clarified concern with evidence, a reframing or a change to the proposal. Confirm that the response addressed it ("Does that answer the concern, or is there something else behind it?").

Some objections are correct. If the buyer's budget truly cannot cover the minimum viable scope, or the product lacks a capability they need, the honest response is to say so. Sellers who argue past real limits create unhappy customers, and unhappy customers cost more than lost deals.

Anticipate predictable objections. If every buyer in a segment asks about data migration, address it during the presentation rather than waiting for it. Objections raised before the seller mentions them feel like resistance; risks the seller raises first feel like candor.

## 9. Proposals, Commitment and Closing

Closing is not a trick performed at the end of a sale. It is the natural result of a sequence of smaller commitments: agreeing to a discovery call, sharing data, bringing a colleague to a demonstration, agreeing on success criteria, reviewing a proposal. If those commitments have been made, the final decision is a short step. If they have not, no closing technique will save the deal.

A mutual action plan is a shared document listing every step between today and the go-live date, with an owner and a date for each: security review, legal review, reference call, budget approval, signature, kickoff. It turns the buyer's internal process into a visible project and exposes steps the seller did not know about. It also gives the champion a tool to drive the decision internally. When the plan slips, it shows exactly where, which makes the conversation about the slip factual rather than awkward.

A proposal should restate the problem, the agreed success criteria and the quantified value before it mentions price. Offer a small number of clear options, usually two or three, that differ in scope rather than in arbitrary discounts. Name the terms that matter, including start date, duration, payment schedule and what is included. Review the proposal live with the economic buyer when possible; a proposal sent by email to a distracted inbox is the weakest form of closing.

Simple, honest closing questions work. A summary close restates the agreed value and asks for the decision ("We've confirmed the problem costs about $110,000 a year, the plan addresses it, and your team is ready to start in March; shall we move to signature?"). An alternative-choice close lets the buyer choose between two acceptable options ("Would you prefer to start with the two pilot locations or all six?"). An assumptive close proceeds to the next logistical step when the buyer has already signaled agreement. Avoid false scarcity, invented deadlines, or pressure that exploits fear; these may close one deal but damage trust, increase cancellations and in some contexts break consumer protection law.

## 10. Negotiation

Negotiation is the part of the sale where terms are settled. It goes best when both sides understand their alternatives and when concessions are traded rather than given away.

Preparation is the decisive step. Know your walk-away point: the least favorable terms you would accept before declining. Know your best alternative to a negotiated agreement, often called the BATNA: what you will do if this deal does not happen. Estimate the buyer's alternatives too. The zone of possible agreement is the overlap between what the buyer is willing to accept and what you are willing to accept; if there is no overlap, no amount of skill will produce a good deal.

List tradeable variables in advance: price, payment terms, contract length, scope, start date, service level, training, case-study rights and referrals. Each concession should be traded for something of value ("If we extend to a two-year term, we can hold this price"). Unconditional discounts teach the buyer that pushing works, and they invite further pushing.

### Worked Example 3: The Real Cost of a Discount

A seller is negotiating a $50,000 annual contract. The product has a 60% gross margin, so delivery costs $20,000 and the gross profit is $30,000. The buyer asks for 10% off.

After a 10% discount the price is $45,000. Delivery still costs $20,000, so gross profit falls to $25,000. A 10% cut in price has removed $5,000 ÷ $30,000 ≈ 16.7% of the gross profit.

To earn the same total gross profit at the discounted price, the company would need $30,000 ÷ $25,000 = 1.2 times as many deals, or 20% more volume, just to stand still.

Now consider a traded concession instead. The seller offers 5% off in exchange for a two-year commitment. The annual price becomes $47,500, the committed contract value becomes $95,000, and gross profit per year is $27,500, or $55,000 over the term. The buyer receives a lower price; the seller receives certainty of revenue and avoids a renewal risk in year two. The discount is now an exchange, not a loss.

Several behaviors improve outcomes. Let the other side state numbers first when you lack information about their range, but anchor first when you know the market well, because the first credible number often shapes the final one. Make concessions smaller as the negotiation goes on, which signals that you are approaching your limit. Summarize agreements in writing as you go. Separate the people from the problem: firm on terms, warm toward the person. Know who on the buyer's side is authorized to agree, so that a late "my boss needs one more discount" does not reopen settled terms.

Procurement teams are measured on savings, so expect a request for a discount even when the business sponsor is satisfied with the price. Plan for it in your opening position, and give the procurement team a documented concession they can report, ideally one that costs you little, such as payment terms.

## 11. Complex Deals and Buying Committees

As deal size grows, the number of people involved grows with it. A mid-size business-to-business purchase commonly involves several people across functions. Each role has different concerns. The economic buyer controls the budget and cares about return and risk. Technical evaluators care about integration, security and reliability. End users care about daily workload. Legal and procurement care about terms and liability. A champion wants the change and will advocate for it internally. A blocker, for reasons of politics, preference or genuine concern, resists it.

Map the committee early. For each person record their role in the decision, what they care about, their current attitude toward the purchase, their influence, and who on the seller's side owns the relationship. Gaps in the map are risks: a deal where the seller has never spoken to the economic buyer is a deal that may be stopped by someone the seller has never met.

Multithreading means building relationships with several people in the account rather than relying on one contact. Deals that depend on a single person stall when that person is busy, leaves, or loses an internal argument. Ask the champion to introduce you to others, and offer each person something useful to them specifically, such as a technical session for evaluators or a business case review for finance.

Test a champion rather than assume one. A real champion has influence, shares information about the decision process, gives access to other stakeholders and personally benefits from the change. A friendly contact who does none of these things is a coach, not a champion. Coaches are valuable, but they cannot carry a deal.

Handle blockers by understanding them. Many blockers have a legitimate concern: a security lead worried about data exposure, a manager who fears a failed rollout. Meet them directly, ask what would have to be true for them to support the decision, and address that concern with evidence. Going around a blocker sometimes works in the short term but often turns them into a determined opponent during implementation.

## 12. Account Management, Retention and Expansion

The first sale begins the customer relationship. For subscription businesses, most of a customer's lifetime value arrives after the first contract, so retention and expansion matter as much as new acquisition.

Retention starts at the handoff from sales to onboarding. Everything the seller learned in discovery, including the problem, the success criteria, the stakeholders and the promises made, should be passed to the team responsible for delivery. A customer who has to explain their goals a second time loses confidence. Agree on a short-term milestone that proves value early, often within the first 30 to 90 days, and measure it.

Regular business reviews keep the relationship tied to outcomes. A good review compares results with the original success criteria, shows usage trends, raises risks honestly and proposes next steps. Reviews that only show product updates waste the customer's time.

Expansion happens when the customer buys more: more seats, more locations, higher tiers or additional products. Expansion is earned by delivering the first outcome and then identifying the next problem. It should never be pushed on an account that has not yet reached value; that accelerates churn.

### Worked Example 4: Gross and Net Revenue Retention

A company starts the year with a cohort of customers paying $1,000,000 in annual recurring revenue. During the year, those same customers add $180,000 through expansion, reduce spending by $40,000 through downgrades (contraction), and $90,000 of revenue is lost when some customers cancel (churn). New customers acquired during the year are excluded, because retention measures what happened to the starting cohort.

Gross revenue retention counts only losses: ($1,000,000 − $40,000 − $90,000) ÷ $1,000,000 = $870,000 ÷ $1,000,000 = 87%. It can never exceed 100% and shows how well the company keeps what it already has.

Net revenue retention adds expansion: ($1,000,000 + $180,000 − $40,000 − $90,000) ÷ $1,000,000 = $1,050,000 ÷ $1,000,000 = 105%. Above 100% means the existing customer base grows on its own, even before any new customer is signed.

The two numbers tell different stories. A company with 105% net retention but 87% gross retention is growing its happiest customers while losing a meaningful share of the rest; the expansion is hiding a fit or delivery problem. The right response is to study the churned accounts against the ideal customer profile and the onboarding record.

## 13. Pipeline Management and Forecasting

A forecast is a prediction of the revenue that will close in a period. Leaders use it to plan hiring, spending and cash, so an inaccurate forecast damages the whole company, not only the sales team. Accuracy comes from honest stage definitions, regular inspection and a clear vocabulary.

Pipeline is every open opportunity expected to close in the period. A weighted pipeline multiplies each opportunity by a probability tied to its stage. Commit is the set of deals the seller is confident will close, usually because the buyer has confirmed the decision and the remaining steps are scheduled. Best case adds deals that could close if things go well. Sellers should forecast commit conservatively and be held to it; leaders should not punish honest downgrades, or sellers will learn to hide bad news.

### Worked Example 5: A Weighted Forecast

A seller has five opportunities expected to close this quarter. The company's stage probabilities are based on its own historical close rates.

| Deal | Value | Stage | Probability | Weighted value |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| A | $40,000 | Proposal | 50% | $20,000 |
| B | $25,000 | Negotiation | 75% | $18,750 |
| C | $60,000 | Discovery | 20% | $12,000 |
| D | $15,000 | Commit | 90% | $13,500 |
| E | $30,000 | Qualified | 10% | $3,000 |

Total pipeline: $40,000 + $25,000 + $60,000 + $15,000 + $30,000 = $170,000.

Weighted pipeline: $20,000 + $18,750 + $12,000 + $13,500 + $3,000 = $67,250.

Commit (deals at 75% or above with confirmed next steps): B + D = $40,000.

Best case (commit plus the proposal-stage deal): $40,000 + $40,000 = $80,000.

The weighted figure is useful across a large team, because errors in individual deals tend to average out. For one seller with five deals, it is less useful: deal C will close for $60,000 or for nothing, never for $12,000. The seller should forecast $40,000 as commit, name $80,000 as best case, and explain the specific evidence behind each deal. The manager's question for deal C is not "will it close?" but "what has to happen this month for it to move to proposal, and has the economic buyer agreed to that step?"

Pipeline hygiene keeps forecasts honest. Every opportunity should have a next step with a date, a close date that reflects the buyer's process rather than the end of the quarter, and a current stage that meets the written exit criteria. Opportunities that have not moved in a set period, often 30 or 60 days, should be reviewed and either advanced with a new commitment from the buyer or closed out.

## 14. Ethics, Law and AI in Selling

Trust is the asset that makes every future sale cheaper. A seller who misrepresents a product may close the current deal, but they increase cancellations, refunds, complaints and legal exposure, and they damage referrals. Ethical selling is therefore also effective selling over any horizon longer than one quarter.

Several practices are basic. Make only claims you can support. Disclose material limitations the buyer would want to know. Do not invent deadlines, competing offers or scarcity. Respect a clear "no." Keep the buyer's confidential information confidential. Follow your company's rules on gifts and entertainment, and be especially careful with public-sector buyers, where gifts can violate anti-bribery laws. In consumer sales, many jurisdictions give buyers cooling-off rights for certain purchases made outside a store, and they prohibit unfair or deceptive practices; sellers should know the rules in their market.

AI tools now support many sales tasks: researching accounts, drafting outreach, summarizing calls, suggesting next steps, scoring leads, and rehearsing objection handling. They save time, but they add their own risks. AI can state plausible but false facts about a prospect, so every fact used in outreach should be verified. It can draft messages that sound generic at scale, which damages the sender's reputation. It must be fed only data the company is permitted to use: customer conversations and personal data are subject to privacy law and to the promises made to customers, and confidential customer data should never be pasted into tools that are not approved for it. Recording or transcribing calls requires the consent that local law demands, and in some jurisdictions every party must agree. Finally, disclose automated contact where the law or the buyer's reasonable expectation requires it. The rule of thumb is that AI may prepare and assist, but a responsible person checks what goes out under their name.

## 15. The Development Path: From First Sale to Revenue Leadership

Sales skill develops in recognizable stages. The labels below follow the Sales King Academy mastery levels; the descriptions explain what changes at each stage.

At the beginning levels, a seller learns the process: researching accounts, booking meetings, running a structured discovery call, handling the common objections with the acknowledge-clarify-respond-confirm pattern, keeping the CRM accurate, and setting a dated next step at the end of every conversation. The measures that matter are activity quality, meeting conversion and the accuracy of their notes.

At the intermediate levels, a seller manages a pipeline of their own. They qualify rigorously, run discovery that reaches quantified impact, deliver tailored demonstrations, and close with summary and alternative-choice questions. They track their own win rate, cycle length and average deal size, and use them to decide where to improve.

At the advanced levels, a seller applies full methodologies in multi-stakeholder deals. They map buying committees, test champions, build mutual action plans, and reframe the buyer's thinking with insight the buyer did not have. Challenger-style teaching, the Sandler approach of equal footing and upfront agreements, and MEDDICC qualification are tools at this level, chosen to fit the deal rather than applied mechanically.

At the expert levels, a seller leads enterprise cycles that include procurement, legal and security reviews. They build business cases the champion can present without them, quantify the cost of inaction, negotiate with executives, and plan deal strategy across months.

At the grandmaster level, the focus shifts from deals to systems: designing sales processes, stage definitions, compensation plans, enablement programs and capacity models that let many sellers perform. They coach, and they are measured by the results of others.

At the highest level, a sales leader shapes markets. They define how buyers think about a problem before any request for proposal exists, build a culture others study, and run a revenue organization in which customers become advocates without being asked.

Two parallel skill tracks run through every level. The cognitive track includes first-principles reasoning about a deal, thinking about second-order consequences, asking what would guarantee losing (inversion), and making probability estimates instead of hopeful guesses. The emotional track includes listening, labeling emotions accurately, staying calm under pressure, and building trust. One useful way to think about trust is that it rises with credibility, reliability and closeness, and falls as the seller's self-interest becomes visible.

## Case Study: Restoring a Collapsing Win Rate

This case is hypothetical and was written for teaching.

Brightline Scheduling sells appointment software to multi-location clinics. Over four quarters its pipeline grew by 60%, but its win rate on qualified opportunities fell from 28% to 14%, and the average cycle stretched from 75 to 110 days. Leadership's first reaction was to hire two more sellers and raise activity targets.

A new sales director reviewed 40 recent lost and stalled opportunities before approving the hires. She found three patterns. First, 26 of the 40 had been advanced to the proposal stage without anyone speaking to the economic buyer, usually the clinic group's operations director or chief financial officer. Proposals were sent to office managers, who liked the product but could not approve spending. Second, 15 of the 40 were single-location clinics, outside the original ideal customer profile; marketing had widened its targeting to raise lead volume, and sellers had accepted the meetings. Single-location clinics churned at nearly twice the rate of larger groups within a year. Third, demonstrations were standardized feature tours, and in call recordings buyers asked about integration with their billing system in 70% of meetings, yet the demonstration covered it in the final two minutes.

The director made four changes. She rewrote stage exit criteria so that an opportunity could only enter proposal after a meeting with the economic buyer and an agreed decision date. She narrowed the ideal customer profile back to groups with three or more locations and moved single-location leads to a lower-cost self-service offer. She rebuilt the demonstration around the three most common discovery findings, opening with billing integration. And she introduced a mutual action plan for every opportunity above $15,000.

Over the next two quarters, the pipeline shrank by about 30% because poor-fit opportunities were closed out or moved to self-service. The win rate rose to 24%, the average cycle fell to 85 days and the forecast error at the start of each month fell sharply. Revenue in the second quarter after the change exceeded the level before the decline, with the same headcount. The two planned hires were redirected to account management, where expansion opportunities in multi-location groups had been neglected.

Questions for analysis:

1. Which lever of sales velocity did leadership's first reaction try to pull, and why would it have made the situation worse?
2. Why did advancing opportunities without the economic buyer lengthen the cycle as well as lower the win rate?
3. What are the risks of moving single-location clinics to self-service, and how should the company monitor them?
4. How would you verify that the demonstration change, rather than the other changes, contributed to the improvement?

## Key Terms

**Average deal size:** total value of won deals divided by the number of won deals in a period.

**BATNA:** best alternative to a negotiated agreement; what a party will do if no deal is reached.

**Blocker:** a stakeholder who resists a purchase; often has a legitimate concern that can be addressed.

**Buying committee:** the set of people who influence or approve a purchase.

**Champion:** a person inside the buying organization who has influence, wants the change, and actively advocates for it.

**Churn:** revenue or customers lost when customers cancel.

**Commit:** forecast category for deals the seller is confident will close, with confirmed buyer steps.

**Contraction:** revenue lost when existing customers reduce spending without cancelling.

**Cost of inaction:** the measurable cost to the buyer of leaving a problem unsolved.

**Coverage ratio:** pipeline value divided by the revenue target for the period.

**Discovery:** the stage in which the seller learns the buyer's situation, problem, impact and decision process.

**Economic buyer:** the person with authority to approve the spending.

**Exit criteria:** buyer facts that must be true before an opportunity moves to the next stage.

**Expansion:** additional revenue from existing customers.

**Gross revenue retention:** starting recurring revenue minus contraction and churn, divided by starting recurring revenue.

**Ideal customer profile (ICP):** a description of the accounts most likely to buy, succeed and stay.

**MEDDICC:** qualification framework covering metrics, economic buyer, decision criteria, decision process, identified pain, champion and competition.

**Multithreading:** building relationships with several stakeholders in an account.

**Mutual action plan:** a shared, dated list of steps both sides must complete to reach a decision and go live.

**Net revenue retention:** starting recurring revenue plus expansion minus contraction and churn, divided by starting recurring revenue.

**Sales velocity:** qualified opportunities times win rate times average deal size, divided by cycle length; revenue produced per day.

**SPIN:** questioning sequence of situation, problem, implication and need-payoff questions.

**Upfront contract:** an agreement at the start of a meeting about its purpose, length and possible outcomes.

**Weighted pipeline:** the sum of each opportunity's value multiplied by its stage probability.

**Win rate:** won opportunities divided by all opportunities that reached a decision, from a defined stage.

**Zone of possible agreement:** the range of terms acceptable to both parties.

## Answer Key

1. It describes the seller's activity, not the buyer's state. A stronger criterion is a buyer fact such as "the economic buyer has reviewed the proposal and agreed to a decision date."
2. Deals needed: $900,000 ÷ $30,000 = 30. Opportunities needed: 30 ÷ 0.20 = 150 qualified opportunities.

4. It tells sellers which accounts to avoid, which protects time and prevents poor-fit customers who churn, consume support and lower satisfaction.
5. Implication questions lead the buyer to describe the consequences of the problem in their own words, which makes its cost concrete and personal.
6. Savings: labor 0.50 × $85,500 = $42,750; errors 0.50 × $25,200 = $12,600; total $55,350. Net first-year benefit: $55,350 − $36,000 − $12,000 = $7,350.

8. Ask what the price is being compared with, such as another option, a budget, or the expected value. The answer separates a value gap, a budget-ownership issue and a competitive comparison, each of which needs a different response.

12. Across many deals, over- and under-estimates average out; a single seller's deals close for their full value or nothing, so the weighted sum rarely matches the actual result.
13. It can state false facts about a prospect, produce generic messages at scale that harm sender reputation, and expose personal or confidential data to tools not approved for it.

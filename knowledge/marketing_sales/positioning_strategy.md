# Positioning Strategy

> The course assumes foundational knowledge of marketing concepts and builds upon them with applied practice and methods.

## Overview

Marketing discipline: positioning strategy. Demand creation and conversion for revenue teams.

## Foundations

Positioning strategy refers to the process of creating and maintaining a unique identity for a product, service, or brand in the minds of target customers, distinct from competitors. A practitioner must understand core definitions, including **market positioning**, which involves identifying a specific market niche and tailoring the product or service to meet the needs of that niche. **Unique selling proposition (USP)** is a key concept, defined as a statement that clearly communicates the benefits and value that differentiate a product or service from its competitors. **Target market** refers to a specific group of customers that a business aims to serve, characterized by demographics, needs, and preferences. **Competitive advantage** is a condition or circumstance that puts a business in a favorable position relative to its competitors, often achieved through effective positioning strategy. **Brand identity** encompasses the visual, tonal, and philosophical elements that define a brand, including its **value proposition**, which is the promise of value that a brand makes to its customers. Understanding these foundational concepts is crucial for developing a successful positioning strategy. **Competitive advantage** is the benefit that a business gains over its competitors, often through differentiation, cost leadership, or focus.

## Mechanisms

Positioning strategy operates through a series of mechanisms that influence consumer perception and behavior. The process begins with market research, where a company identifies its target audience, competitors, and market trends. This information is used to create a unique value proposition (UVP), which is a statement that defines the company's unique benefits and differentiators. The UVP is then communicated to the target audience through various marketing channels, such as advertising, social media, and content marketing. As consumers are exposed to the UVP, they begin to perceive the company and its products in a certain way, which can influence their attitudes, preferences, and purchasing decisions. The causal chain can be broken down into the following steps: (1) market research informs UVP creation, (2) UVP creation informs marketing messaging, (3) marketing messaging influences consumer perception, and (4) consumer perception influences behavior. By understanding and leveraging these mechanisms, companies can effectively position themselves in the market and achieve a competitive advantage. The key to successful positioning is to create a UVP that resonates with the target audience and differentiates the company from its competitors, ultimately driving consumer preference and loyalty.

Next, a unique value proposition is developed, which is a statement that clearly defines how the product or service meets the needs of the target audience in a way that competitors do not. This value proposition is then used to create a positioning statement, which is a concise statement that defines how the product or service is perceived by the target audience. The positioning statement is used to guide all marketing efforts, including advertising, packaging, and sales messaging. As the marketing efforts are executed, they influence the target audience's perceptions, creating an association between the product or service and the unique value proposition. This association, in turn, drives purchasing decisions, ultimately leading to sales and revenue. The causal chain is as follows: market research → unique value proposition → positioning statement → marketing efforts → target audience perception → purchasing decision → sales and revenue.

## Methods And Frameworks

The Ansoff Matrix is used to evaluate growth strategies, considering market penetration, product development, market development, and diversification. Use when assessing expansion opportunities. Failure mode: overemphasis on growth without considering resource constraints. 
The Boston Consulting Group (BCG) Matrix categorizes products based on market growth and relative market share, guiding investment decisions. Use when prioritizing products in a portfolio. Failure mode: oversimplification of complex market dynamics. 
The Porter's Five Forces framework analyzes industry competitiveness, considering threat of new entrants, bargaining power of buyers and suppliers, threat of substitutes, and rivalry among existing competitors. Use when assessing market attractiveness. Failure mode: neglecting external factors like government policies or technological changes. 
The SWOT analysis (Strengths, Weaknesses, Opportunities, and Threats) is used to identify internal and external factors influencing positioning strategy. Use when developing a comprehensive strategy. Failure mode: failure to prioritize and address identified factors. 
The Unique Selling Proposition (USP) formula helps create a distinctive value proposition, differentiating a product or service from competitors. Use when developing marketing campaigns. Failure mode: inability to deliver on the promised unique value. 
The perceptual mapping technique visualizes customer perceptions of products or services, facilitating competitive analysis. Use when identifying market gaps or opportunities. Failure mode: reliance on incomplete or inaccurate customer data.

## Worked Examples

To illustrate the application of positioning strategy, consider the following examples. 
1. A company introducing a new energy drink wants to position it as a healthy alternative to existing products. Market research indicates that the target audience values natural ingredients and low sugar content. If the new drink contains 80% natural ingredients and 20 grams of sugar per serving, while the leading competitor contains 40% natural ingredients and 30 grams of sugar per serving, the company can position its product as a healthier option, potentially commanding a premium price. 
2. A retailer seeking to position itself as a discount store might compare its prices to those of a high-end retailer. If the high-end retailer sells a particular brand of jeans for $80, and the discount retailer sells the same brand for $60, the discount retailer can position itself as offering significant savings, attracting price-sensitive customers. 
3. A technology firm developing a new smartphone wants to position it as a high-performance device. Benchmark tests show that the new phone's processor is 25% faster than that of the market leader, and its battery life is 15% longer. By highlighting these performance advantages, the company can position its phone as a superior choice for demanding users, potentially justifying a higher price point. In each of these examples, the positioning strategy is based on specific, measurable differences between the company's product and those of its competitors.

To illustrate the application of positioning strategy, consider the following examples:

The hotel offers exclusive services such as private beach dinners and couples' spa treatments. With a limited capacity of 20 rooms, the hotel can charge a premium rate of $500 per night, which is $200 more than the average hotel rate in the area. By targeting a specific niche, the hotel can maximize its revenue and occupancy rate.

3. **Perceived Value**: A company launching a new smartphone wants to position its product as a high-end device with advanced features such as artificial intelligence and augmented reality. Although the production cost is $300 per unit, the company prices the product at $800, which is comparable to other high-end smartphones in the market. By creating a perception of high value through marketing and branding efforts, the company aims to justify the premium price and attract customers who are willing to pay for the latest technology.

## Applications

Positioning strategy is used in practice across various domains, including marketing, branding, and product management. In marketing, positioning strategy involves identifying a unique niche or space in the market where a product or service can be differentiated from competitors. This is achieved by analyzing customer needs, preferences, and behaviors, as well as competitor offerings and market trends. For instance, a company like Apple positions its products as innovative, design-driven, and user-friendly, appealing to customers who value sleek design and ease of use. In branding, positioning strategy is used to create a unique brand identity and message that resonates with the target audience. This involves developing a brand positioning statement that outlines the brand's unique value proposition, target audience, and key differentiators. In product management, positioning strategy is used to inform product development and launch decisions, ensuring that products meet customer needs and are competitively differentiated. By applying positioning strategy, businesses can create a unique market presence, build brand loyalty, and drive long-term growth and profitability. Effective positioning strategy requires ongoing market research, customer feedback, and competitive analysis to ensure that the positioning remains relevant and effective over time.

## Common Errors

A common error in positioning strategy is confusing it with branding, where the focus is solely on creating a unique image rather than differentiating the product or service from competitors. Another mistake is failing to conduct thorough market research, leading to a positioning strategy that is not grounded in consumer needs and preferences. Some practitioners also overlook the importance of trade-offs, attempting to position a product or service as everything to everyone, which can result in a muddled and ineffective positioning strategy. Furthermore, not considering the competitive landscape and how it may change over time can lead to a positioning strategy that is quickly rendered obsolete. Additionally, positioning a product or service based on features rather than benefits can fail to resonate with consumers, who are more interested in what a product or service can do for them rather than its technical specifications. Lastly, not regularly reassessing and adjusting the positioning strategy can lead to stagnation and a loss of relevance in the market. Additionally, practitioners often overlook the importance of consistency in messaging and visual identity, resulting in a diluted or confusing brand position. Some also mistakenly believe that a positioning strategy can be static, when in fact it requires continuous monitoring and adaptation to changes in the market and consumer behavior. Furthermore, attempting to position a product or service based on features rather than benefits can lead to a strategy that fails to resonate with the target audience, as consumers are more interested in how a product or service can solve their problems or improve their lives.

A common error in positioning strategy is failing to differentiate between unique selling points (USPs) and unique value propositions (UVPs). USPs focus on the product's features, whereas UVPs emphasize the benefits and value that the product offers to customers. Another mistake is attempting to position a product or service based on multiple, conflicting attributes, resulting in a muddled and ineffective message. Additionally, some practitioners neglect to consider the competitive landscape and customer perceptions, leading to a positioning strategy that is not grounded in reality. Overreliance on demographic data, rather than psychographic and behavioral insights, can also lead to inaccurate positioning. Furthermore, failing to regularly reassess and adjust the positioning strategy can result in a product or service becoming outdated and less relevant in the market. These errors can be avoided by conducting thorough market research, understanding customer needs and preferences, and developing a clear and concise value proposition that resonates with the target audience.

## Advanced

The field of positioning strategy is continually evolving, with advancements in technology, shifts in consumer behavior, and the rise of new marketing channels. At the graduate level, students delve into the intricacies of positioning strategy, exploring topics such as cognitive mapping, which involves creating visual representations of consumers' mental perceptions of brands and products. Another key area of study is the application of machine learning algorithms to analyze large datasets and identify patterns in consumer behavior, enabling more precise positioning strategies. Furthermore, the concept of dynamic positioning is being explored, where brands continuously adapt and adjust their positioning in response to changing market conditions. Open questions in the field include how to effectively measure the impact of positioning strategies on consumer behavior and brand performance, as well as the role of positioning in shaping consumer perceptions of sustainability and social responsibility. As the field continues to evolve, researchers are investigating the potential of emerging technologies, such as virtual and augmented reality, to create immersive brand experiences that influence consumer perceptions and positioning. Additionally, the increasing importance of global marketing and the need for positioning strategies to be adapted for diverse cultural contexts are driving new areas of research and inquiry. Furthermore, the increasing importance of sustainability and social responsibility in marketing raises questions about how companies can effectively position themselves as environmentally and socially conscious, while also maintaining a competitive edge. As the field continues to evolve, researchers are exploring new areas such as the role of artificial intelligence in positioning strategy and the impact of virtual and augmented reality on consumer perceptions of brands.

Positioning strategy is evolving with advancements in data analytics, artificial intelligence, and digital marketing.

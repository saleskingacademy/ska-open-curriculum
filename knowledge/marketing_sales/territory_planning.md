# Territory Planning

> Dividing accounts and markets among sellers so that each has a fair, workable opportunity and the company covers its best prospects.

## Overview

Territory planning is how a sales organisation decides which accounts each seller owns. A territory can be a region, an industry, a company-size band, a named list of accounts or a combination. Good territory design gives every rep enough real opportunity to reach quota, avoids two reps chasing the same account, and makes sure the best prospects are actually being worked. Poor design creates uneven results that reflect the map more than the skill of the people on it.

## Foundations

A **territory** is the set of accounts or the market segment assigned to one seller or team. **Territory potential** is the estimated revenue available in it. **Coverage** is how much of the target market is assigned and actively worked. **Capacity** is how many accounts a seller can realistically manage. **Quota** is the revenue target assigned to the territory. **Balance** means territories have comparable potential relative to their quotas.

Territories are usually defined by one or more dimensions: **geography**, **industry or vertical**, **company size or segment**, or **named accounts**, where specific strategic accounts are assigned directly.

## Mechanisms

Territories work by giving each seller clear ownership. Ownership reduces conflict between reps, makes accountability clear and encourages long-term relationship building, because a rep who owns an account benefits from investing in it. Balanced territories also make quota fair: when potential is similar, results reflect execution, which makes coaching and compensation decisions more accurate.

## Methods And Frameworks

Start with the ideal customer profile and count the accounts that match it in each potential territory. Estimate potential for each account using size, fit and likely spend. Sum potential by territory and compare it with seller capacity: a territory with more good accounts than one rep can work needs to be split, and one with too few needs to be combined or expanded.

Assign quotas in proportion to potential, not as identical numbers for every rep. Keep strategic accounts as named assignments for experienced sellers. Document the rules for edge cases, such as a company with offices in several regions or an inbound lead from an account another rep owns, so that disputes are settled by rule rather than argument.

Review territories on a schedule, typically once or twice a year, using pipeline, win rates and actual revenue by territory. Avoid frequent changes, because moving accounts breaks relationships and resets progress.

## Worked Examples

A software company has three reps and divides the country into three equal-sized regions. After two quarters, one rep is far ahead and two are behind. Analysis shows the leading rep's region contains most of the target industry's companies. The team rebuilds the territories by counting ideal-profile accounts instead of land area, giving each rep a similar number of good-fit accounts. The next quarter's results are closer together, and total revenue rises because accounts in the formerly overloaded region are finally being worked.

In another case, a large customer with offices in two regions is contacted by two reps at once, confusing the buyer. The team adds a rule that the account is owned by the rep covering the headquarters location, with credit sharing for regional expansion deals.

## Applications

Territory planning drives hiring plans, quota setting, compensation design, marketing support and forecasting. It also guides where to focus account-based marketing, because each territory's top accounts become target lists. Revenue operations teams typically own the analysis, while sales leaders own the final assignments.

## Common Errors

Common errors include dividing territories by area alone, assigning equal quotas to unequal territories, overloading top performers with more accounts than they can work, and redrawing territories too often. Another error is ignoring existing relationships when reassigning accounts, which can lose deals already in progress.

## Advanced

Advanced teams build territories from account-level data, combining fit scores, intent signals and historical spend, and model several designs before choosing one. They plan for growth by keeping capacity in reserve for new hires, and they align territories with marketing and customer success so that each account has one coordinated team. At the highest level, territory design becomes a strategic tool that shapes where the company competes and how efficiently it grows.

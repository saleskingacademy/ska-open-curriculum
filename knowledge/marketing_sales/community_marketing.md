# Community Marketing

> The course assumes foundational knowledge of marketing and teaches applied practice of community marketing.

## Overview

Professional discipline for the amplify specialist: community marketing. Applied practice used to generate revenue.

## Foundations

Community marketing refers to a marketing strategy that focuses on building and engaging with a community of customers, prospects, and partners to achieve business objectives. A **community** is a group of individuals who share common interests, needs, or characteristics, and interact with one another to create a sense of belonging and shared identity. **Marketing** is the process of creating, communicating, and delivering value to customers and stakeholders. **Core definitions** in community marketing include **target audience**, the specific group of people a business aims to engage with, and **unique value proposition**, the unique benefits and value a business offers to its customers. **First principles** of community marketing involve understanding **social identity theory**, which states that individuals derive a sense of self and identity from the groups they belong to, and **social capital**, the networks, relationships, and norms of trust and cooperation that exist within a community. Key vocabulary includes **engagement**, the process of interacting with and involving the community in business activities, **influencer**, an individual who has the power to influence the purchasing decisions of others, and **advocacy**, the act of promoting or supporting a business or cause. A **practitioner** must understand these concepts to develop effective community marketing strategies. Effective community marketing requires a deep understanding of these concepts and the ability to apply them in a way that resonates with the target community.

## Mechanisms

Community marketing operates through a series of interconnected steps that foster engagement, build trust, and ultimately drive brand loyalty. The process begins with **identification of the target community**, where marketers pinpoint specific groups based on shared interests, demographics, or behaviors. Next, **community insight generation** occurs, involving the collection of data to understand the community's needs, preferences, and pain points. This insight is then used to **develop a community value proposition**, which outlines how the brand intends to provide value to the community, such as through content, events, or support. 
The **creation of community touchpoints** follows, including social media groups, forums, or physical meeting spaces where community members can interact with the brand and each other. **Content strategy and creation** are crucial, as relevant, high-quality content is developed to engage the community, address their interests, and reinforce the brand's value proposition. 
**Community engagement and activation** involve encouraging community members to participate in discussions, share content, and invite others to join, thereby fostering a sense of belonging and ownership. As the community grows and matures, **community management** becomes essential, ensuring that the community remains aligned with the brand's objectives and that any issues are addressed promptly. 
Throughout these steps, **measurement and evaluation** are ongoing, using metrics such as engagement rates, community growth, and customer retention to assess the effectiveness of the community marketing efforts and inform future strategies. Ultimately, **brand loyalty and retention** are strengthened, leading to long-term customer relationships and sustained business growth. Throughout this process, **continuous feedback and evaluation** are crucial, allowing marketers to refine their strategies, address community concerns, and optimize their community marketing efforts.

## Methods And Frameworks

Community marketing employs various methods and frameworks to engage and mobilize communities. The Social Identity Model of Deindividuation Effects (SIDE) is used to understand how community members derive a sense of identity and belonging, informing strategies to foster inclusivity and shared values. The Community-Based Social Marketing (CBSM) framework is applied when targeting specific behaviors, leveraging social norms, and promoting community-led initiatives. The Diffusion of Innovations model is utilized to track the adoption of new ideas or practices within a community, identifying opinion leaders and early adopters to accelerate the diffusion process. The Failure Mode and Effects Analysis (FMEA) is used to anticipate potential pitfalls, such as community resistance or lack of engagement, allowing for proactive mitigation strategies. The Transtheoretical Model (TTM) of behavior change is employed to understand the stages of community engagement, from pre-contemplation to maintenance, and develop targeted interventions to support progress. Each method and framework has its limitations, and failure modes include overreliance on a single approach, neglecting community diversity, and inadequate assessment of community needs and capacities. The Community Development Model focuses on empowering community members to take ownership of initiatives, suitable for long-term, locally-driven projects. The Diffusion of Innovations theory, which describes how new ideas spread within communities, is applied when introducing new products or services. The Rogers' Adoption Curve formula (Innovators 2.5%, Early Adopters 13.5%, Early Majority 34%, Late Majority 34%, Laggards 16%) predicts the rate of adoption, helping marketers tailor strategies to each group. The failure mode of these methods often lies in inadequate understanding of the community's needs, values, and social dynamics, leading to misplaced or ineffective marketing efforts.

## Worked Examples

To illustrate the application of community marketing principles, consider the following examples. 
1. A local fitness studio wants to increase its membership by 20% within 6 months. It partners with a nearby health food store to offer joint promotions, such as discounts on studio memberships for store customers and vice versa. If the studio currently has 150 members and the monthly membership fee is $50, the potential increase in revenue can be calculated as follows: 20% of 150 = 30 new members, resulting in an additional $1,500 per month (30 * $50) or $18,000 per year. 
2. A community garden seeks to increase its volunteer base by 50% within 3 months. It collaborates with a local school to develop an educational program, where students can earn credits by volunteering at the garden. Assuming the garden currently has 20 volunteers, each contributing 5 hours per week, the potential increase in volunteer hours can be calculated as: 50% of 20 = 10 new volunteers, resulting in an additional 50 hours per week (10 * 5 hours) or 2,600 hours per year. 
3. A small business wants to increase its social media following by 30% within 2 months. It launches a referral program, where existing followers can earn rewards by inviting friends to follow the business's social media accounts. If the business currently has 1,000 followers and the average customer acquisition cost is $10, the potential reduction in customer acquisition costs can be calculated as follows: 30% of 1,000 = 300 new followers, resulting in a potential cost savings of $3,000 (300 * $10). It partners with a nearby health food store to offer joint promotions, resulting in a 15% increase in membership within 3 months. The studio's marketing budget is $1,000 per month. By allocating 30% of this budget to community outreach and partnerships, the studio is able to reach a wider audience and achieve its goal. 
2. A bookstore aims to increase sales by 15% within a year by hosting book clubs and author events. It allocates 20% of its $5,000 monthly marketing budget to community events, resulting in a 10% increase in sales within 6 months. The bookstore also collects feedback from attendees, which helps to refine its marketing strategy and improve customer engagement. 
3. A non-profit organization wants to raise $50,000 for a local charity event. It partners with 5 local businesses to offer sponsored content on social media, resulting in a 25% increase in donations within 2 months. The organization allocates 40% of its $2,000 monthly marketing budget to community outreach and partnerships, and uses the remaining budget to promote the event through targeted advertising. By leveraging the reach and influence of its partners, the organization is able to exceed its fundraising goal.

## Applications

Community marketing is applied in various domains, including non-profit organizations, businesses, and government agencies. In non-profit organizations, community marketing is used to promote social causes, such as fundraising campaigns, volunteer recruitment, and awareness programs. For instance, the American Red Cross uses community marketing to promote blood donation, disaster relief, and first aid training. 
In businesses, community marketing is used to build brand loyalty, engage with customers, and promote products or services. Companies like Patagonia and REI use community marketing to promote environmental conservation and outdoor recreation, aligning their brand values with those of their target audience. 
Government agencies also use community marketing to promote public health, education, and community development programs. The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) uses community marketing to promote vaccination programs, disease prevention, and healthy lifestyles. 
The key principle in community marketing is to understand the needs, values, and interests of the target community and develop marketing strategies that resonate with them, fostering a sense of belonging and social connection. By doing so, organizations can build trust, credibility, and loyalty among community members, ultimately achieving their marketing objectives. In government agencies, community marketing is used to promote public health initiatives, such as vaccination programs, disease prevention, and healthy lifestyle campaigns. Effective community marketing involves understanding the target audience, building relationships, and fostering a sense of community, which can lead to increased engagement, loyalty, and social impact.

## Common Errors

A primary error in community marketing is neglecting to establish clear, measurable objectives, leading to ineffective allocation of resources. Another mistake is failing to understand the target community's needs, preferences, and behaviors, resulting in marketing efforts that lack relevance and resonance. Additionally, practitioners often overlook the importance of building trust and credibility with the community, instead relying on short-term promotional tactics that can come across as insincere or exploitative. Furthermore, inadequate engagement with community stakeholders, such as local leaders and influencers, can hinder the success of community marketing initiatives. A lack of cultural sensitivity and understanding of community nuances can also lead to marketing efforts that are perceived as insensitive or tone-deaf. Moreover, insufficient monitoring and evaluation of community marketing efforts can make it difficult to assess their impact and make data-driven decisions. By recognizing and avoiding these common errors, practitioners can develop more effective community marketing strategies that foster meaningful connections with their target audiences.

## Advanced

Community marketing's advanced concepts involve nuanced understandings of social identity theory, where individuals derive a sense of belonging and self-concept from group memberships. This theory is crucial in designing effective community marketing strategies, as it highlights the importance of creating inclusive environments that foster a sense of community among customers. Furthermore, the application of social network analysis (SNA) is becoming increasingly significant, as it allows marketers to map and analyze the complex relationships within and between communities, identifying key influencers and information brokers. Open questions in the field include the impact of social media on community dynamics, the role of community marketing in shaping consumer attitudes towards social and environmental issues, and the development of metrics to measure the effectiveness of community marketing initiatives. The field is moving towards a more integrated approach, incorporating insights from sociology, anthropology, and psychology to better understand the complexities of community formation and engagement. Additionally, the rise of online communities and social media platforms has created new opportunities for community marketing, but also raises concerns about the authenticity and sustainability of these communities, highlighting the need for ongoing research and critical evaluation. Graduate-level research explores the tensions between community-driven initiatives and corporate interests, as well as the role of power dynamics in shaping community outcomes. Open questions in the field include the measurement and valuation of social capital, the impact of social media on community cohesion, and the ethics of community manipulation for marketing purposes. The field is moving towards a deeper understanding of community as a complex system, incorporating insights from sociology, anthropology, and network science to develop more effective and sustainable community marketing strategies. Key areas of inquiry include the application of community-based participatory research methods, the development of community-centric metrics and evaluation frameworks, and the exploration of emerging technologies such as blockchain and virtual reality in community marketing contexts.

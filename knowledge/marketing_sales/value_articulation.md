# Value Articulation

> The course teaches practical field techniques for professional selling, assuming foundational knowledge of sales and marketing principles.

## Overview

Sales discipline: value articulation. Practical field technique for professional selling.

## Foundations

Value articulation refers to the process of explicitly defining and communicating the value proposition of a product, service, or idea, which is a statement that explains how it meets the needs and wants of its target audience. A value proposition is a promise of value to be delivered, and it is based on the unique benefits, features, and attributes that differentiate it from alternatives. The core definitions involved in value articulation include: 
1. **Value**: the perceived benefits, utility, or satisfaction that a customer derives from a product, service, or idea, often measured in terms of its ability to solve a problem, meet a need, or create an experience. 
2. **Benefit**: a specific advantage or positive outcome that a customer receives from a product, service, or idea, such as cost savings, increased efficiency, or enhanced enjoyment. 
3. **Feature**: a characteristic or attribute of a product, service, or idea that provides a benefit, such as a specific technology, design element, or functionality. 
4. **Attribute**: a quality or property of a product, service, or idea that influences customer perception or behavior, such as brand reputation, quality, or aesthetics. 
First principles of value articulation involve understanding customer needs, wants, and preferences, as well as the competitive landscape and market trends. Key vocabulary includes: 
* **Customer insights**: knowledge and understanding of customer needs, wants, and behaviors, often gathered through research, feedback, and data analysis. 
* **Unique selling proposition (USP)**: a statement that clearly and concisely communicates the unique benefits and value of a product, service, or idea. 
* **Positioning**: the process of creating a unique and meaningful identity for a product, service, or idea in the minds of its target audience, often through branding, marketing, and messaging. 
* **Value chain**: a series of activities or processes that create value for customers, from production and delivery to marketing and support. 
* **Value stream**: a visual representation of the value-creating processes and activities, often used to identify areas for improvement and optimization. 
Understanding these core definitions, first principles, and vocabulary is essential for effective value articulation, as it enables practitioners to develop a clear and compelling value proposition that resonates with their target audience. A value proposition is a core concept in value articulation, defined as a promise of value to be delivered to customers, comprising a bundle of goods, services, or experiences that satisfy their needs. The target audience, also known as stakeholders or customers, are the individuals or groups that will benefit from the value proposition. Needs and wants are fundamental concepts in value articulation, referring to the requirements or desires that drive human behavior, with needs being essential and wants being discretionary. Utility, a key principle in value articulation, refers to the usefulness or satisfaction derived from a product, service, or idea, often measured in terms of its ability to solve problems, improve efficiency, or enhance well-being. Effective value articulation requires a deep understanding of these core definitions, as well as the ability to identify, analyze, and prioritize the needs and wants of the target audience, in order to craft a compelling value proposition that resonates with them.

## Mechanisms

Value articulation operates through a series of interconnected steps that facilitate the translation of user needs into actionable design and development directives. The process begins with user research, where data is collected to understand user behaviors, preferences, and pain points. This data is then analyzed to identify patterns and trends, which inform the development of user personas. These personas serve as archetypes, representing the needs and goals of distinct user groups. The next step involves defining the value proposition, which is a statement that clearly articulates the benefits and unique value that a product or service offers to its users. This value proposition is then used to guide the development of product features and functionalities, ensuring that they align with the identified user needs. The causal chain is as follows: user research informs persona development, which in turn informs value proposition definition, and ultimately, this definition drives product development. Throughout this process, feedback loops are essential, allowing for iterative refinement and ensuring that the final product meets the articulated user needs and delivers the intended value. Effective value articulation relies on continuous communication among stakeholders, including designers, developers, and users, to ensure that the value proposition remains consistent and aligned with user expectations. Throughout this process, feedback loops are essential, allowing for the continuous reassessment and refinement of the value articulation process. This iterative approach ensures that the values remain relevant and effective in guiding decision-making over time. By making the causal chain explicit, value articulation provides a systematic approach to ensuring that decisions are consistent with an individual's or organization's core values.

## Methods And Frameworks

Value articulation employs various methods and frameworks to elicit, analyze, and prioritize stakeholder values. The Kano Model categorizes attributes into must-haves, more-is-better, and delighters, helping to identify which features to prioritize. Use the Kano Model when understanding customer satisfaction drivers is key, but beware of its failure mode: oversimplification of complex value structures. 
The Six Thinking Hats method, developed by Edward de Bono, separates thinking into parallel, distinct modes, facilitating a structured approach to value articulation. Apply this method when diverse stakeholder perspectives need to be integrated, but be cautious of its failure mode: stifling of creative, free-form thinking. 
The Value Stream Mapping (VSM) framework, originating from the Toyota Production System, visualizes and analyzes value streams to identify waste and optimize processes. Utilize VSM when process efficiency and waste reduction are primary objectives, but recognize its failure mode: neglect of non-process-related value drivers. 
The MECE (Mutually Exclusive, Collectively Exhaustive) principle ensures that value attributes are categorized in a way that avoids overlap and ensures comprehensive coverage. Apply MECE when categorizing and prioritizing value attributes, but be aware of its failure mode: forced categorization of inherently ambiguous or overlapping values. 
The Analytic Hierarchy Process (AHP) is a decision-making framework that derives relative weights for value attributes through pairwise comparisons. Use AHP when quantifying and prioritizing value attributes is necessary, but beware of its failure mode: inconsistency in pairwise comparisons, leading to unreliable results.

The Value Proposition Canvas is a strategic management tool used to design, innovate, and iterate value propositions, ideal for startups and new product development. The Six Thinking Hats method, developed by Edward de Bono, enables parallel thinking and consideration of different perspectives, useful for value articulation in group settings. The McKinsey 7S Framework assesses an organization's effectiveness in implementing its value proposition, focusing on strategy, structure, systems, skills, style, staff, and shared values. The failure mode of these methods often lies in inadequate understanding of customer needs, insufficient stakeholder involvement, or poor communication of the value proposition. The Jobs-To-Be-Done (JTBD) framework focuses on the underlying tasks customers are trying to accomplish, guiding the development of solutions that meet those needs. It is effective when used to identify opportunities for innovation. Failure mode: narrow focus on existing solutions rather than the underlying job. 
The Service Blueprint method visualizes the service process, highlighting touchpoints and interactions, to design and improve customer experiences. It is useful for service-oriented businesses. Failure mode: overlooking critical backstage processes. 
Each method and framework has its strengths and limitations, and the choice of which to use depends on the specific context and goals of the value articulation effort.

## Worked Examples

To illustrate the concept of value articulation, consider the following examples. 
1. A company offers a premium subscription service for $20/month, providing access to exclusive content and priority customer support. The cost of providing this service is $5/month. If the company has 1000 premium subscribers, the total revenue is $20,000/month, and the total cost is $5,000/month. The value articulation in this case is the difference between the revenue and the cost, which is $15,000/month. This represents the value that the company is able to capture from its customers.
2. A manufacturer produces a product with a production cost of $10/unit and sells it for $15/unit. If the manufacturer produces 1000 units/month, the total revenue is $15,000/month, and the total cost is $10,000/month. The value articulation in this case is $5,000/month, which is the difference between the revenue and the cost. This value can be used to invest in research and development, marketing, or other activities that create more value for customers.
3. A software company offers a free version of its product with limited features and a paid version with additional features for $10/month. If 10% of the 10,000 monthly users upgrade to the paid version, the total revenue is $10,000/month. The cost of providing the paid features is $1/month per user, so the total cost is $1,000/month. The value articulation in this case is $9,000/month, which is the difference between the revenue and the cost. This value can be used to improve the product and provide more value to customers, increasing the likelihood of them upgrading to the paid version. The cost of maintaining the free version is $10/year per user, and the cost of maintaining the paid version is $20/year per user. By effectively articulating value, companies can increase demand, drive revenue, and ultimately achieve profitability.

## Applications

Value articulation is used in various domains to facilitate decision-making, prioritize resources, and optimize outcomes. In product development, it helps teams to identify and prioritize features based on customer needs and business goals. For instance, in agile software development, value articulation informs the creation of user stories and acceptance criteria, ensuring that development efforts align with customer value. In healthcare, value articulation is used to prioritize treatments and interventions based on patient needs, outcomes, and cost-effectiveness. In marketing, it helps to craft messaging and positioning that resonates with target audiences, by articulating the unique value proposition of a product or service. Additionally, value articulation is applied in policy-making to evaluate and prioritize initiatives based on their potential impact on societal well-being, economic growth, and environmental sustainability. By applying value articulation principles, organizations can make more informed decisions, allocate resources more effectively, and ultimately create more value for their stakeholders. ## Common Errors

A common error in value articulation is conflating features with benefits, where practitioners focus on listing product attributes rather than explaining how these attributes create value for the customer. Another mistake is using generic value statements that lack specificity and fail to differentiate the product or service from competitors. Additionally, some practitioners make the error of articulating value solely in terms of cost savings, neglecting other important aspects such as increased efficiency, improved quality, or enhanced customer experience. Furthermore, failing to consider the customer's perspective and prioritize their needs can lead to value articulation that resonates poorly with the target audience. Lastly, using overly technical or complex language can obscure the value proposition, making it difficult for customers to understand and appreciate the value being offered. These errors can undermine the effectiveness of value articulation, making it essential to carefully craft and communicate the value proposition in a clear, customer-centric, and compelling manner.

Lastly, not regularly revisiting and refining the value articulation to reflect changing customer needs, market conditions, or competitor activity can render the value proposition stale and less competitive.

## Advanced

These approaches enable the consideration of multiple, conflicting objectives and the integration of qualitative and quantitative factors. Researchers are also exploring the application of machine learning and artificial intelligence to value articulation, particularly in the context of preference learning and decision support systems. Open questions in the field include the development of more effective methods for eliciting and representing human values, the integration of value articulation with other decision-making approaches, and the investigation of cultural and individual differences in value structures. Furthermore, the field is moving towards a greater emphasis on dynamic and adaptive value articulation, recognizing that values and preferences can change over time and in response to new information. This requires the development of more flexible and responsive value articulation frameworks, capable of accommodating uncertainty and ambiguity.

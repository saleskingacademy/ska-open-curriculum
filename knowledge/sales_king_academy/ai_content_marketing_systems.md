# AI Content Marketing Systems

This chapter teaches you how to build a content marketing system: a repeatable process that takes an idea from strategy to research, brief, draft, edit, fact-check, approval, publication, distribution and measurement, with AI speeding up the work at each stage while people keep control of strategy, accuracy and brand voice. You will learn to write briefs that produce useful drafts, to edit and fact-check AI-assisted material, to turn one strong piece into many, to measure content by the business results it drives rather than by page views, and to stay on the right side of the law and of your readers' trust. The chapter closes with a field case from Sales King Academy's own content quality work and a lab on the live platform.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this chapter you will be able to:

1. Describe a content marketing system as a pipeline of stages, and explain which stages AI can accelerate and which must stay under human control.
2. Define a content strategy in terms of audience, problems, positioning, content pillars and goals at each stage of the buying journey.
3. Use AI for research while avoiding invented sources, and capture original insight from customers, data and experts.
4. Write a content brief and a brand voice guide that let AI produce drafts close to publishable quality.
5. Run structured editing passes, including readability scoring, and a fact-check that traces every claim to a source.
6. Repurpose a long piece into channel-specific assets and plan their distribution.
7. Measure content with a hierarchy of metrics, compare assets by cost per qualified lead, and judge an A/B test with a significance check.
8. Identify the legal and ethical duties that apply to AI-assisted marketing content, including truthful claims, disclosure, copyright and email consent.

## 1. What a content marketing system is

**Content marketing** is the practice of attracting and keeping customers by publishing material they find useful: articles, guides, videos, podcasts, emails, social posts, case studies, tools and courses. Instead of interrupting people with an advertisement, the business earns their attention by helping them, and in doing so it builds the trust that makes a later sale easier.

Most businesses produce content as a series of one-off efforts. Someone has an idea, writes something when they find time, posts it, and moves on. Nobody measures what happened, the piece is never updated, and the next piece starts from nothing. A **content marketing system** replaces that with a defined, repeatable pipeline in which every piece moves through the same stages, every stage has an owner, and results feed back into what is produced next.

### The stages of the pipeline

A complete system has eleven stages:

1. **Strategy**: who the content is for, what problems it addresses and what business goal it serves.
2. **Research**: what the audience is asking, what already exists, and what the business knows that others do not.
3. **Brief**: a written specification for one piece.
4. **Draft**: the first full version.
5. **Edit**: structural, line and brand edits.
6. **Fact-check**: every claim traced to a source.
7. **Approve**: sign-off by the content owner and, where needed, legal or subject experts.
8. **Publish**: release on the primary channel with correct formatting and metadata.
9. **Distribute**: adapted versions sent to other channels, such as email, social platforms and sales teams.
10. **Measure**: results against the goal set in the brief.
11. **Refresh or retire**: update pieces that still matter and remove ones that no longer serve.

### Where AI helps and where it does not

AI changes the economics of several stages dramatically. It can summarize research, propose outlines, produce a first draft from a good brief, check a draft against a style guide, rewrite one piece into ten formats, and summarize performance data. These are the stages where speed improves most.

Three things do not move to AI. **Strategy**, because deciding who to serve and what to stand for is a business judgment that depends on knowledge of customers, competitors and the company's own goals. **Original insight**, because the facts, stories, data and opinions that make content worth reading come from the business's own experience, and a model trained on public text can only rearrange what is already public. **Accountability**, because every claim published under a company's name is the company's claim, and responsibility for its truth cannot be delegated to software.

The result is a division of labour that runs through the whole chapter: **people decide what to say and whether it is true; AI helps say it faster, in more forms, for more channels.**

### Why systems beat volume

Because AI makes generic text nearly free, the amount of content in the world is rising quickly. That makes generic content worth less and distinctive content worth more. A business that uses AI simply to publish more of the same material that everyone else publishes will find its work ignored by readers and by search engines alike. A business that uses AI to publish its own knowledge, its customers' results and its experts' views more quickly and in more useful forms gains an advantage that competitors cannot copy by buying the same tool.

## 2. Strategy: the part that stays human

Every content system starts from a written strategy. Without one, AI simply produces faster versions of whatever someone happened to think of, and the output never adds up to anything.

### Audience and problems

Start with the people the business wants to reach. A **persona** is a short description of a typical buyer: their role, the problems they face, the questions they ask, what they already know, what worries them about buying and where they look for information. Good personas are built from evidence: interviews with customers, notes from sales calls, support tickets and search data. Personas invented in a meeting tend to describe the buyer the team wishes it had.

A useful way to capture a persona's needs is to list the **jobs** they are trying to get done and the obstacles in the way. A bookkeeper at a small firm is not looking for "accounting software content"; they are trying to close the month faster, avoid errors that annoy the owner and stop chasing receipts. Content that helps with those jobs earns attention.

### The buying journey

Buyers need different content at different stages:

- **Awareness**: they have a problem but may not have named it. Content explains the problem, its causes and its cost. Educational articles, short videos and checklists fit here.
- **Consideration**: they are comparing approaches. Content compares methods honestly, explains trade-offs and shows how to decide. Guides, comparison pages, webinars and calculators fit here.
- **Decision**: they are choosing a supplier. Content provides proof and removes risk: case studies, demonstrations, pricing explanations, implementation plans and answers to objections.
- **Retention and expansion**: they are customers. Content helps them succeed with what they bought and shows what else is possible. Onboarding guides, tutorials, community posts and advanced courses fit here.

A strategy that produces only awareness content generates traffic that never converts; one that produces only decision content has no audience to convert. A balanced plan covers all stages, weighted towards where the business's pipeline is weakest.

### Positioning and content pillars

**Positioning** is the place the business wants to occupy in the buyer's mind: what it is best at, for whom, and why that matters. Content should reinforce positioning consistently, which is why a few **content pillars**, broad themes the business will be known for, are chosen in advance. A company selling scheduling software to clinics might choose three pillars: reducing missed appointments, running an efficient front desk, and complying with patient-communication rules. Every piece should belong to a pillar. Pillars stop a content programme from drifting into topics that attract attention but not buyers.

### Goals and the editorial calendar

Each piece should have one primary goal tied to the business: to rank for a question buyers ask, to generate sign-ups for a webinar, to equip sales with an answer to a common objection, or to reduce support tickets on a confusing feature. The **editorial calendar** turns strategy into a schedule: what will be produced, by whom, for which pillar and stage, with which goal, and when. A calendar that shows the balance of pillars and stages at a glance prevents the common drift towards whatever is easiest to write.

### Original insight: the ingredient AI cannot supply

The most important strategic decision is where the content's original insight will come from. Five sources are available to almost any business:

- **Customer results**: what happened when real customers used the product or service, with their permission.
- **First-party data**: numbers from the business's own operations, anonymized and aggregated, such as the most common causes of a problem across hundreds of jobs.
- **Expert opinion**: the views of the business's own specialists, captured in interviews.
- **Process knowledge**: how the business actually does something, step by step, including the mistakes it learned from.
- **Honest comparison**: clear statements of when the business's approach is not the right choice, which competitors rarely publish and readers trust.

A content system should have a routine for collecting these, such as a monthly interview with a subject-matter expert, a quarterly data summary and a standing request to the sales team for customer stories. AI is extremely useful for turning that raw material into publishable form. It cannot replace it.

## 3. Research with AI, safely

Research is where AI saves the most time and where it can cause the most embarrassing errors.

### What AI does well in research

- **Question mining**: given transcripts of sales calls, support tickets or community posts, AI can list the distinct questions customers ask, group them by theme and count how often each appears. This is one of the highest-value uses in the whole system, because it grounds the calendar in what real buyers want to know.
- **Summarizing sources you supply**: a long report, a set of interview notes or a competitor's published guide can be reduced to its main points quickly.
- **Gap analysis**: comparing the business's existing content against the list of customer questions shows which questions have no good answer yet.
- **Outlining**: proposing a structure for a piece, which the writer then reshapes.
- **Interview preparation and processing**: drafting questions for an expert interview, then turning the transcript into a summary of quotable points.

### What AI does badly in research

Asked a factual question with no source material, a language model produces a likely-sounding answer, not a verified one. It may give a statistic that does not exist, attribute a quotation to the wrong person, or cite a study, book or web page that was never written. These invented details are fluent and specific, which makes them dangerous: they look like research.

Three rules prevent the problem:

1. **Bring the sources to the model, not the other way round.** Give it the documents, data and transcripts, and ask it to work only from them.
2. **Never publish a citation you have not opened.** Every statistic, study and quotation must be checked against the original by a person before it appears under your name.
3. **Use live sources for live facts.** Prices, laws, market figures and recent events change. A model's training data has a cut-off date, so answers about recent matters need a current source, clearly labelled as such.

Some AI tools, including the Sales King Academy chat, show where an answer came from with a source label. A label saying an answer came from a web search, from stored verified knowledge or from AI generation tells the researcher how much checking it needs, but it does not remove the need to open the original source before publishing.

### Capturing original insight efficiently

The bottleneck in most content systems is not writing but getting experts to share what they know. AI helps here more than anywhere. A 30-minute recorded conversation with a specialist, transcribed and summarized, can supply the substance for several articles, and the expert only needs to review the final drafts rather than write them. The key discipline is to keep the expert's actual words and claims intact and to send the finished piece back for their approval, because an AI summary can subtly change what someone meant.

## 4. The content brief and the brand voice guide

The single biggest improvement most teams can make to AI-assisted writing is to stop asking for drafts from one-line requests. "Write a blog post about invoice automation" produces generic text. A **content brief** produces a draft that needs editing, not rewriting.

### What a brief contains

A good brief fits on one page and answers these questions:

- **Audience**: which persona, at which stage of the buying journey, and what they already know.
- **Goal**: the one business result this piece should produce, and how it will be measured.
- **Key message**: the single idea the reader should remember, in one sentence.
- **Pillar and search intent**: which content pillar it belongs to and, for search-driven pieces, the question the reader is trying to answer.
- **Original insight**: the customer result, data, expert view or process knowledge that makes this piece different from what already exists. If this line is empty, the piece probably should not be written.
- **Sources**: the documents, transcripts and data the writer, human or AI, must work from.
- **Outline**: the main sections, in order.
- **Constraints**: length, reading level, claims that must not be made, terms that must be used or avoided.
- **Call to action**: what the reader should do next.

### Brand voice

**Brand voice** is the consistent personality of a company's writing: how formal it is, how it addresses the reader, which words it prefers, and what it never says. Without a written guide, AI writes in a bland, generic style, and different writers drift in different directions.

A **brand voice guide** that works with AI has four parts:

1. **Traits with explanations.** Not just "friendly and expert" but what that means in practice: "We write as an experienced colleague. We use 'you' and 'we'. We explain jargon the first time we use it. We do not use exclamation marks."
2. **Do and don't pairs.** Short examples showing the same idea written in voice and out of voice. Models learn much more from a contrast than from adjectives.
3. **Vocabulary.** Preferred terms, banned terms, product names spelled correctly, and phrases the company avoids because competitors use them or because they overpromise.
4. **Approved examples.** Three to five published pieces that represent the voice well, supplied to the model with the brief.

The guide also lists **claim rules**: statements the business must never make without approval, such as guarantees of results, comparisons naming competitors, or health, financial or legal claims. These rules matter more than style, because a breach can cause legal trouble.

### Worked example 1: The economics of AI-assisted production

A marketing agency's writers cost $45 an hour, including overheads. Before adopting AI, a 1,500-word article took 3 hours of research, 4 hours of drafting, 1.5 hours of editing and 0.5 hours of approvals: 9 hours in all.

With a brief-driven AI process, the writer spends 1 hour preparing the brief and gathering sources, 2.5 hours reviewing and rewriting the AI draft, 1 hour fact-checking, 1 hour on the final edit and 0.5 hours on approvals: 6 hours in all. Model usage for the drafts and editing passes costs about $0.50 per article in this example.

- Cost before: 9 × $45 = **$405** per article.
- Cost after: 6 × $45 + $0.50 = $270 + $0.50 = **$270.50** per article.
- Saving: ($405 − $270.50) ÷ $405 = 0.332, or about **33.2 percent**.

For a client receiving 8 articles a month, monthly cost falls from 8 × $405 = $3,240 to 8 × $270.50 = $2,164, a saving of $1,076.

Two lessons sit inside the numbers. First, the saving is about a third, not the ninety percent that people sometimes imagine, because the human stages that protect quality, briefing, rewriting, fact-checking and editing, still take real time. Second, the cost of the model is trivial compared with the cost of people. The economic question is never "how cheap is the AI?" but "how much skilled time does the process still need to produce work worth publishing?" A team that cuts the fact-check to save another hour is not saving money; it is buying risk.

## 5. Drafting and editing

### Patterns for drafting with AI

Teams use AI in drafting in three main patterns, and the right one depends on the piece.

- **Brief to draft.** AI writes a complete first draft from the brief and sources; a writer reshapes it. This suits standard formats such as how-to guides, product explanations and summaries of expert interviews.
- **Outline and sections.** The writer and AI agree an outline, then AI drafts one section at a time with the writer steering each. This suits long or technical pieces, where a single long generation tends to repeat itself or drift from the outline.
- **Human draft, AI edit.** The writer drafts, and AI tightens, restructures or adapts it. This suits opinion pieces, thought leadership and anything where the writer's own voice is the point.

Whatever the pattern, the instructions to the model should include the brief, the voice guide, the sources and an explicit instruction to work only from the sources for any factual claim and to mark places where a fact is needed but not supplied, rather than filling the gap.

### The editing passes

Editing works best as several focused passes rather than one general read. Each pass has a single question:

1. **Structure**: does the piece deliver the key message, in a logical order, for the stated audience? Are there sections that repeat each other?
2. **Substance**: is the original insight from the brief actually present and prominent, or has it been buried under generic material?
3. **Line**: are sentences clear and direct? Is there filler, such as empty openings, stacked adjectives or sentences that restate the previous one?
4. **Voice**: does it match the brand voice guide, including vocabulary and claim rules?
5. **Readability**: is it at the right reading level for the audience?
6. **Format**: headings, lists, links, images with descriptive alternative text, and a clear call to action.

AI can perform a first version of passes 3 to 6 well, flagging problems for the editor. Passes 1 and 2 need a person who understands the audience and the business. One problem deserves special attention in AI-assisted text: **repetition**. Models tend to restate points in slightly different words, especially across long pieces or across many pieces generated from similar instructions. An editor should look for the same idea appearing twice and keep only the better version; the Sales King Academy field case later in this chapter shows how much duplication can accumulate when this check is skipped at scale.

### Readability scores

A **readability formula** estimates how hard a text is to read from simple counts. The two best known are the Flesch Reading Ease score and the Flesch-Kincaid Grade Level, both based on average sentence length and average syllables per word:

- **Flesch Reading Ease** = 206.835 − 1.015 × (words ÷ sentences) − 84.6 × (syllables ÷ words). Higher scores are easier; scores around 60 to 70 are usually considered plain English for a general adult audience.
- **Flesch-Kincaid Grade Level** = 0.39 × (words ÷ sentences) + 11.8 × (syllables ÷ words) − 15.59. The result approximates the United States school grade needed to understand the text.

These formulas are rough guides. They cannot tell whether a text is clear, accurate or well organized, and a short, simple sentence can still be confusing. They are useful for catching drafts that have drifted far from the intended level, which AI drafts sometimes do when they adopt a formal, abstract style.

### Worked example 2: Bringing a draft to the right reading level

A home-insurance broker writes for homeowners and sets a target of Flesch Reading Ease above 60. An AI draft of one section has 120 words in 5 sentences, with 204 syllables.

- Words per sentence: 120 ÷ 5 = 24.
- Syllables per word: 204 ÷ 120 = 1.7.
- Reading Ease = 206.835 − 1.015 × 24 − 84.6 × 1.7 = 206.835 − 24.36 − 143.82 = **38.66** (38.655 before rounding).
- Grade Level = 0.39 × 24 + 11.8 × 1.7 − 15.59 = 9.36 + 20.06 − 15.59 = **13.83**.

The section reads at roughly university level, far above the target. The editor splits long sentences and replaces abstract words ("subsequent remediation of water ingress") with plain ones ("fixing the leak afterwards"). The revised section still has 120 words, now in 8 sentences, with 180 syllables.

- Words per sentence: 120 ÷ 8 = 15.
- Syllables per word: 180 ÷ 120 = 1.5.
- Reading Ease = 206.835 − 1.015 × 15 − 84.6 × 1.5 = 206.835 − 15.225 − 126.9 = **64.71**.
- Grade Level = 0.39 × 15 + 11.8 × 1.5 − 15.59 = 5.85 + 17.7 − 15.59 = **7.96**.

The section now meets the target. The editor then reads it aloud, because the formula would reward choppy sentences just as well as clear ones, and only a person can judge which the revision has produced.

## 6. Fact-checking and quality control

Fact-checking is the stage most often skipped and the one whose absence does the most damage. An error published under a company's name is the company's error, whoever or whatever wrote the sentence.

### The claims register

A practical method is the **claims register**: before approval, every factual claim in the piece is listed with its source and the name of the person who checked it.

| Claim in the draft | Type | Source | Checked by | Result |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| "Missed appointments fell from 12 percent to 7 percent at the client's clinics" | Customer result | Client's report, with written permission to publish | Content lead | Confirmed |
| "Most clinics lose a significant share of revenue to no-shows" | General claim | None supplied | Content lead | Softened to an attributed expert view |
| "Required by law in all states" | Legal claim | None supplied | Legal reviewer | Removed; not true as stated |
| Product supports text reminders in 14 languages | Product fact | Product documentation | Product manager | Corrected to the documented number |

The register makes three kinds of problem visible. Unsupported statistics, which AI drafts often contain, are found and either sourced or removed. Legal and regulatory claims are routed to someone qualified. Product facts are checked against the current documentation, because products change faster than content.

### Types of claim and how to check them

- **Numbers and statistics**: trace to the original publication, not a secondary article that quotes it, and check the date, the population and the exact wording.
- **Quotations**: confirm with the person quoted, or with the original recording or document.
- **Customer results**: confirm with the customer and record their written permission to publish.
- **Product facts**: check against current documentation or with the product owner.
- **Legal, financial and health statements**: route to a qualified reviewer, and prefer wording the business has already approved.
- **Comparisons with competitors**: check that each statement is accurate, current and fair, and route to legal review if competitors are named.

### Originality and duplication

Two further checks protect the business. The first is **originality**: the published text must not copy other people's work. AI models can occasionally reproduce passages close to text they were trained on, and writers sometimes paste in material from sources. A plagiarism check against the web and a rule that sources are summarized, not copied, prevent this. The second is **internal duplication**: the same paragraphs appearing across many of the business's own pages. Duplication makes content tedious for readers, makes it harder for search engines to decide which page to show, and is a common symptom of AI content produced at volume from similar instructions.

A note on **AI detection tools**, which claim to tell whether text was written by AI: they are unreliable, producing both false accusations and missed cases, and should not be used to judge the work of writers or students. The useful question is not "was AI involved?" but "is this accurate, original, useful and in our voice?", and the claims register and editing passes answer it directly.

## 7. Repurposing and distribution

### Repurposing

**Repurposing** turns one substantial piece of content into many smaller pieces adapted to different channels and formats. It is one of the highest-value uses of AI in marketing, because the expensive work, finding and developing an original idea, is done once and the adaptation is fast.

A recorded 45-minute webinar with a customer and an internal expert might become:

- A long article built from the transcript, organized around the questions the audience asked.
- A short summary email to the webinar's registrants, including those who did not attend.
- Five to ten social posts, each built on one specific point or quotation.
- Two or three short video clips of the strongest moments, with captions.
- A one-page checklist that sales can send to prospects.
- A set of answers added to the help centre or FAQ.
- A case study, if the customer agrees.

Each adaptation needs its own short brief, because channels differ in what works. A social post must make sense on its own, with no context. An email must earn a click with a specific promise. A sales one-pager must answer an objection. AI is good at adapting length and format; an editor must still check that each piece keeps the original meaning, because shortening is where nuance is lost and claims become exaggerated.

### Distribution

A piece that nobody sees has no value, however good it is. **Distribution** is the plan for getting each piece in front of its audience:

- **Owned channels**: the business's website, email list, blog, community and product. These are the most valuable, because the business controls them and they do not depend on a platform's rules.
- **Earned channels**: coverage, mentions, links and shares by others. Original data and strong opinions earn far more of these than generic content.
- **Social and community platforms**: useful for reach, but subject to changing rules about what they show to whom.
- **Paid channels**: advertising to promote the best pieces to specific audiences.
- **Sales and support**: often forgotten, but frequently the highest-converting channel. A case study sent by a salesperson at the right moment in a deal may be worth more than thousands of anonymous views.

A **distribution checklist** attached to every brief ensures each piece is sent through the channels its goal requires, with the right adaptation, at the right time.

## 8. Search, AI answers and the changing route to readers

Many readers find content through search engines, and increasingly through AI systems that answer questions directly, sometimes citing and linking to the sources they used. A content system must work for both.

### Search intent and structure

People search to answer a question or complete a task. **Search intent** is that underlying purpose: to learn something, to compare options, to find a particular site or to buy. Content that matches intent, answering the question directly and fully near the top, then giving the detail, serves readers and search engines alike. Clear headings that state what each section answers, short paragraphs, lists and tables for comparisons, and descriptive page titles all help.

### Quality signals

Search engines aim to show helpful, reliable content. Google, the most widely used search engine, describes the qualities it looks for under the heading of experience, expertise, authoritativeness and trustworthiness, often shortened to E-E-A-T, and its public guidance says that content made mainly to manipulate search rankings, including content produced at scale with little value to readers, is treated as spam whether people or machines produced it. The practical implications are consistent with the rest of this chapter:

- Show who wrote or reviewed a piece and why they are qualified.
- Include first-hand experience: what the business actually did and saw.
- Cite sources for factual claims.
- Keep pieces accurate and updated, with dates.
- Publish fewer, better pieces rather than many thin ones.

### Writing for AI answer systems

AI systems that summarize the web tend to draw on content that is clear, well structured, factually specific and attributed. Writing with direct answers, defined terms, named sources and clear statements of who the information applies to makes content easier for such systems to use accurately. No one outside these systems can guarantee how they choose sources, and their behaviour changes, so the reliable strategy is the same as for human readers: be the clearest, most accurate and most original source on the questions your buyers ask.

## 9. Measuring what content achieves

### A hierarchy of metrics

Content metrics fall into four levels, and the higher levels matter most:

1. **Reach**: impressions, views, sessions, followers. These show whether content is being seen.
2. **Engagement**: time on page, scroll depth, clicks, replies, shares. These show whether it is being read.
3. **Conversion**: sign-ups, downloads, demo requests, trial starts. These show whether it moves people to act.
4. **Business outcomes**: qualified leads, sales conversations, pipeline value, deals won, customers retained, support tickets avoided. These show whether it pays.

Teams often report the first two levels because they are easy to collect. Decisions should be made on the last two. A useful working definition is the **qualified lead**: a contact who meets the business's criteria for fit and interest and is accepted by sales for follow-up. Measuring content by qualified leads rather than raw leads stops the system from rewarding pieces that attract many people who will never buy.

### Attribution

Buyers usually touch several pieces of content before they buy. **Attribution** is the method of assigning credit for a result to the touches that preceded it. Common models include:

- **First touch**: all credit to the first piece the buyer met. Favours awareness content.
- **Last touch**: all credit to the final piece before conversion. Favours decision content.
- **Linear**: equal credit to every touch.
- **Position-based**: more credit to the first and last touches, the rest shared among those in between.

No model is correct; each answers a different question. Pick one, apply it consistently, and support it with a simple direct question on forms and in sales calls: "How did you hear about us?" Self-reported answers often reveal influence, such as a podcast or a colleague's recommendation, that tracking cannot see.

### Worked example 3: Comparing two assets by cost per qualified lead

A software company compares two pieces of content promoted over the same quarter.

| | Asset A: broad industry trends article | Asset B: implementation checklist |
|---|---|---|
| Views | 8,000 | 2,000 |
| Conversion to lead | 1.0% | 4.0% |
| Share of leads qualified by sales | 25% | 45% |
| Total cost (production and promotion) | $1,200 | $900 |

- Asset A leads: 8,000 × 1.0% = 80. Qualified: 80 × 25% = 20. Cost per lead: $1,200 ÷ 80 = $15.00. Cost per qualified lead: $1,200 ÷ 20 = **$60.00**.
- Asset B leads: 2,000 × 4.0% = 80. Qualified: 80 × 45% = 36. Cost per lead: $900 ÷ 80 = $11.25. Cost per qualified lead: $900 ÷ 36 = **$25.00**.

Asset A had four times the views, and a report based on reach would call it the winner. Both produced the same number of leads. But Asset B produced 36 qualified leads against 20, at less than half the cost per qualified lead.

The company can take the analysis one step further. If 20 percent of qualified leads become customers and the average first-year deal is worth $3,000, Asset B's 36 qualified leads are expected to produce 36 × 20% = 7.2 deals, worth 7.2 × $3,000 = $21,600, while Asset A's 20 are expected to produce 4 deals worth $12,000. These are expectations, not guarantees, and they credit each asset with the whole sale, which no single piece of content truly earns. Even so, the direction of the evidence is clear: the next quarter's budget should favour practical, decision-stage material like Asset B.

### Testing

When two versions of something could work, such as two subject lines, two headlines or two calls to action, the business can test them. In an **A/B test**, the audience is split at random, each half sees one version, and the results are compared. The comparison must account for chance: with small numbers, one version can look better simply by luck. A standard check for comparing two rates is the **two-proportion z-test**, which measures how many standard errors apart the two rates are. A z value beyond about 1.96 in either direction corresponds to a difference that would arise by chance less than 5 percent of the time if the versions truly performed the same, a common threshold for calling a result **statistically significant**.

One practical note on email: opens have become an unreliable measure, because some email applications load images automatically for privacy reasons, which registers opens that did not happen. Clicks and replies are better measures for testing email content.

### Worked example 4: Is the new subject line really better?

A training company sends its monthly newsletter to 10,000 subscribers, split at random into two groups of 5,000. Group A receives the usual subject line; Group B receives a new one that names a specific result. Group A produces 150 clicks and Group B produces 190.

- Click rate A: 150 ÷ 5,000 = 3.0 percent. Click rate B: 190 ÷ 5,000 = 3.8 percent.
- Relative improvement: (3.8 − 3.0) ÷ 3.0 = 26.7 percent.
- Pooled rate: (150 + 190) ÷ 10,000 = 0.034.
- Standard error of the difference: √(0.034 × 0.966 × (1 ÷ 5,000 + 1 ÷ 5,000)) = about 0.003625.
- z = (0.038 − 0.030) ÷ 0.003625 = about **2.21**.

Because 2.21 is greater than 1.96, the difference is statistically significant at the 5 percent level; the two-sided probability of a difference this large arising by chance if the subject lines performed equally is about 0.027. The company adopts the new style of subject line. It also notes the limits of the result: it applies to this audience and this kind of email, and a single test does not prove that naming results always works. Repeating the test on later newsletters builds confidence.

## 10. Law, ethics and trust

Marketing content is regulated speech in most countries, and AI does not change the rules. It changes the speed at which a business can break them.

### Truthful, substantiated claims

In most jurisdictions, advertising and marketing claims must be truthful and not misleading, and a business must be able to support objective claims with evidence. In the United States, the Federal Trade Commission enforces this principle; other countries have their own regulators and codes. A claim such as "cuts admin time in half" needs evidence that it does, for typical customers, before it is published. AI drafts often contain confident, specific claims with no basis, which is exactly why the claims register exists.

### Endorsements, testimonials and reviews

Testimonials must reflect real customers' honest experiences, and material connections, such as payment, free products or a family relationship with the business, must be disclosed where the rules require it. AI must never be used to write fake reviews or invented testimonials, and an edited customer quote must still say what the customer meant and be approved by them. Regulators in several countries have taken action against fake and AI-generated reviews, and the reputational damage when they are discovered is often worse than any penalty.

### Disclosure of AI use

Whether and how to disclose AI involvement depends on context and on the rules in each market. A reasonable practice is: content reviewed, edited and approved by named people can carry their names, because they are responsible for it; content that presents itself as a personal experience or opinion must actually be one; and automated channels, such as an AI chat on the website, should say they are automated. Images or videos that depict real people or events in ways that did not happen should not be presented as real.

### Copyright and ownership

Two questions matter. First, **using others' material**: copying text, images or other works without permission can infringe copyright, and asking AI to imitate a particular living author or reproduce a specific work is a risk to avoid. Summarize sources in your own words, cite them, and use images you have the rights to. Second, **owning your output**: in the United States, the Copyright Office has taken the position that copyright protects human authorship, so material generated by AI with little human creative contribution may not be protected, while human selection, arrangement and substantive editing can be. Rules differ by country and are still developing. A business that wants to protect its content should ensure meaningful human authorship and keep records of how important pieces were made, and should read its AI provider's terms on output ownership and use.

### Email and personal data

Email marketing is governed by laws that differ by country. In the United States, the CAN-SPAM Act requires, among other things, that commercial emails not use misleading headers or subject lines, identify the sender, include a valid postal address and offer a working way to opt out. In the European Union and the United Kingdom, data-protection and electronic-communications rules generally require consent or another lawful basis before sending marketing emails to individuals. When content forms collect personal data, the privacy notice should explain what is collected and why, and data sent to AI tools for personalization should be limited to what the task needs.

## 11. Running the content system

### Roles

Even a one-person content operation benefits from naming roles, because each role carries a distinct responsibility:

- **Content owner**: sets strategy, owns the calendar and is accountable for results.
- **Subject-matter experts**: supply original insight and approve technical accuracy.
- **Writer and editor**: produce and refine each piece, using AI as a tool.
- **Fact-checker or approver**: signs the claims register; for regulated topics, a legal or compliance reviewer.
- **Distribution lead**: adapts and sends each piece through its channels.
- **Analyst**: reports results against goals and feeds findings back into the calendar.

### Governance of AI use

Write down how AI is used in the system: which tools are approved, what data may be sent to them, which stages require human review, how the voice guide and brief templates are maintained, and who may change them. Keep the instructions used for recurring tasks, such as the repurposing instructions for webinars, as versioned templates, so improvements are shared and changes are deliberate. Review a sample of published pieces each month against the voice guide and claims rules.

### The content inventory and refresh cycle

A **content inventory** lists every published piece with its pillar, stage, goal, publication date, last update, owner and recent performance. Reviewed quarterly, it supports four decisions for each piece: keep as is, refresh (update facts, examples and dates), merge (combine overlapping pieces into one stronger one) or retire (remove or redirect). Refreshing a strong existing piece is often a better use of time than writing a new one. The inventory is also where problems that no single editor would notice become visible: duplicated sections across many pages, broken links, outdated product facts, and pieces that are published but cannot be found because a link, menu or index points to the wrong place.

### Case study: Cedar & Pine Home Goods rebuilds its content engine

*This case uses a fictional company to illustrate the methods in this chapter. Cedar & Pine Home Goods is not a real business, and its figures are invented for teaching.*

Cedar & Pine is an online retailer of solid-wood furniture with a small marketing team of three. After adopting an AI writing tool, the team increased its output from 4 blog posts a month to 30, mostly short articles on decorating trends generated from one-line requests. Six months later, traffic had risen slightly, but sales attributed to content had not moved, customer service was receiving complaints about an article that recommended a cleaning product that damages oiled wood, and the team was spending more time correcting errors than planning.

**Diagnosis.** The content owner built an inventory of all 180 posts. It showed that most posts belonged to no clear pillar, that dozens covered nearly the same trends with overlapping paragraphs, that almost none contained anything Cedar & Pine knew that competitors did not, and that no post had been fact-checked against the company's own care instructions.

**Strategy reset.** The team chose three pillars tied to its positioning as a maker of furniture that lasts: choosing solid wood, caring for wood furniture, and designing rooms around pieces you will keep for decades. It interviewed its workshop lead and two long-standing customers, and mined a year of customer-service emails for the questions buyers actually asked. The most common questions were about care, finishes and delivery, not trends.

**System.** Every piece now started from a brief with an original-insight line, usually something from the workshop. The voice guide gained a claim rule: no care advice unless it matches the company's published care instructions. A claims register became a required step before publishing. Output fell to 8 pieces a month, each repurposed into an email, social posts and a short workshop video.

**Clean-up.** Using the inventory, the team merged overlapping trend articles into a handful of strong guides, retired the rest with redirects to the guides, corrected every care recommendation, and removed the damaging advice at once with a correction notice.

**Results.** Within two quarters, the care guides had become the site's most visited content from search, customer-service emails about care fell noticeably, and the share of buyers who mentioned the guides when asked "How did you hear about us?" rose. The team published far less and achieved far more.

**Questions to consider.** Why did tripling output with AI fail to raise sales? What role did customer-service emails play in the new strategy? Which single control would have prevented the damaging cleaning advice?

## SKA Field Case Study: Removing duplicate text across a curriculum

This case comes from Sales King Academy's own content operation: the curriculum you are reading now. It concerns two problems that any business publishing a large body of AI-assisted content can face: material that repeats itself, and good material that readers cannot reach.

### The situation

Sales King Academy publishes an education catalogue of 1,119 subjects organized into 107 textbooks, at eight levels anchored to real education levels, with every unit labelled by its own 16-digit DNA-16 identifier. The material is written with AI assistance under human direction and is also published in a public curriculum repository on GitHub. The platform's agents answer students' questions from this material, and Deterministic mode answers only from verified knowledge, so the quality of the stored text directly shapes the quality of every answer built on it.

### The problems it caused

Two problems surfaced in audits in early October 2026.

**Duplicated text.** An audit found repeated sentences across a large part of the catalogue. On 7 and 8 October, 466 subjects had repeated text removed, a total of 1,006 duplicate sentences. The problem was concentrated in exactly the subjects this program is built around: in sales and marketing, about half of the text was duplicated. Repetition of this kind wastes the reader's time, makes material look padded, and weakens the grounding that agents draw on, because the same point restated several times adds bulk without adding knowledge.

**Unreachable content.** A separate audit on 7 October found that the subject index pointed 55 core sales subjects at the wrong folder. The material existed, but students who opened those subjects saw nothing. Good content that cannot be found has, for the reader, the same value as content that was never written.

### What was done

The duplicates were removed while every distinct worked example was kept. That rule matters: the aim was to remove repeated sentences, not to cut material indiscriminately, and worked examples carry much of a textbook's teaching value. For the unreachable subjects, a single fix to the index restored all 55.

### What it shows

The case illustrates five points from this chapter:

1. **Repetition is the characteristic flaw of AI-assisted content at scale.** Section 5 named repetition as a problem to check for in every edit. When many pieces are generated from similar instructions and the check is skipped, duplication accumulates until it makes up a large share of the text, as it did in the sales and marketing subjects here.
2. **Quality control must distinguish repetition from substance.** Removing duplicates while keeping every distinct worked example is the content equivalent of the merge decision in Section 11: keep the strongest version of each idea, remove the copies, and protect the material that does the teaching.
3. **Distribution failures can hide good work.** The index problem is a distribution failure in the sense of Section 7. A single broken lookup made 55 subjects invisible, and a single fix restored them. Content inventories and periodic audits catch such failures; page-by-page editing never will.
4. **Grounded systems inherit the quality of their sources.** Because the platform's agents answer from stored material, content quality is product quality. A duplicated or unreachable passage weakens every answer that depends on it.
5. **Audits are a routine, not an event.** Both problems were found by deliberate audits, not by chance. A content system that audits its own inventory finds its problems before its readers do.

### What remains open

The case leaves questions that are good practice for any content operation. How can duplication be caught at the moment of writing, rather than in a later clean-up, for example by checking each new piece against existing material before it is published? What automated check would confirm that every subject in the index points to material that exists and is non-empty? How should the quality of AI-assisted chapters be measured beyond word counts, for instance by the share of review questions students answer correctly? ## Summary

A content marketing system is a defined pipeline from strategy through research, brief, draft, edit, fact-check, approval, publication, distribution, measurement and refresh. AI accelerates research, drafting, editing, repurposing and analysis; strategy, original insight and accountability for truth stay with people. As generic text becomes cheap, distinctive content built on a business's own knowledge becomes more valuable.

Strategy defines personas from evidence, maps content to the stages of the buying journey, sets positioning and a few content pillars, and gives every piece a business goal in an editorial calendar. Research with AI works when sources are brought to the model and every citation is opened before publishing. A one-page brief and a written brand voice guide, with traits, contrasts, vocabulary, examples and claim rules, turn AI drafts from generic to nearly publishable. Production savings are real but moderate, because the human stages that protect quality still take time.

Editing runs as focused passes for structure, substance, line, voice, readability and format, with special attention to repetition. Readability formulas such as Flesch Reading Ease and Flesch-Kincaid Grade Level are rough but useful guides. A claims register traces every factual claim to a source and a checker. Repurposing turns one strong piece into many channel-specific assets, and a distribution plan uses owned, earned, social, paid and sales channels. Content for search and AI answer systems should match intent, show experience and expertise, cite sources and stay current.

Measurement should move from reach and engagement to conversions and business outcomes, using consistent attribution and comparisons such as cost per qualified lead, with A/B tests judged by a significance check. Marketing content must be truthful and substantiated, testimonials must be genuine, AI involvement must not mislead, copyright must be respected, and email and data-protection rules must be followed. The Sales King Academy field case showed duplication accumulating in AI-assisted material, removed without losing worked examples, and a single broken index entry hiding 55 subjects until one fix restored them.

## Key terms

- **Content marketing**: attracting and keeping customers by publishing material they find useful.
- **Content marketing system**: a defined, repeatable pipeline that moves every piece of content through the same owned stages.
- **Persona**: an evidence-based description of a typical buyer, their problems and their questions.
- **Buying journey**: the stages a buyer moves through, from awareness to consideration, decision and retention.
- **Positioning**: the place a business aims to occupy in buyers' minds.
- **Content pillar**: a broad theme a business chooses to be known for.
- **Editorial calendar**: the schedule of planned content with owners, pillars, stages and goals.
- **Original insight**: knowledge, data, results or opinion that only the business can supply.
- **Question mining**: extracting and grouping the questions customers ask from calls, tickets and posts.
- **Content brief**: a one-page specification for a single piece of content.
- **Brand voice**: the consistent personality of a company's writing.
- **Brand voice guide**: a written description of the voice, with contrasts, vocabulary, examples and claim rules.
- **Claim rules**: statements a business must not publish without approval.
- **Readability formula**: a calculation estimating reading difficulty from sentence length and word length.
- **Flesch Reading Ease**: a readability score where higher numbers indicate easier text.
- **Flesch-Kincaid Grade Level**: a readability score expressed as an approximate school grade.
- **Claims register**: a list of every factual claim in a piece with its source and checker.
- **Repurposing**: adapting one substantial piece into many channel-specific assets.
- **Owned channel**: a channel the business controls, such as its website or email list.
- **Earned channel**: coverage, links and mentions by others.
- **Search intent**: the purpose behind a search, such as learning, comparing or buying.
- **E-E-A-T**: experience, expertise, authoritativeness and trustworthiness, qualities Google says it looks for in content.
- **Qualified lead**: a contact who meets the business's criteria and is accepted by sales for follow-up.
- **Attribution**: assigning credit for a result to the content touches that preceded it.
- **A/B test**: a randomized comparison of two versions shown to separate halves of an audience.
- **Two-proportion z-test**: a check of whether two rates differ by more than chance would explain.
- **Statistical significance**: a difference unlikely to have arisen by chance under a stated threshold.
- **Content inventory**: a list of all published content with its purpose, owner, dates and performance.
- **Substantiation**: evidence that supports an objective marketing claim.

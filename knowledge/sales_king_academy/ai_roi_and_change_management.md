# AI ROI and Change Management

This chapter opens the second volume of the Sales King Academy program. The first volume taught what AI can do in sales and marketing and how to connect it to the systems a business runs on. This volume turns to the questions owners and managers face once the technology is understood: is it worth the money, and will people actually use it? The two questions cannot be separated. An AI tool that would save hundreds of hours a year returns nothing if the team ignores it, and a tool everyone loves can still lose money if its running costs grow faster than its benefits. You will learn to build the full cost and benefit picture of an AI project, calculate return on investment, payback and net present value, work out the unit economics of an AI feature, prove benefits with baselines and comparison groups, and lead people through the change using established frameworks. The chapter closes with a field case from the Sales King Academy platform about paying for AI only where it earns its keep, and a lab in which you measure the cost and value of real tasks on the live site.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this chapter you will be able to:

1. Explain why the return on an AI project depends on both its financial design and its adoption by people.
2. Build the total cost of ownership of an AI project, including the hidden costs most business cases leave out.
3. Identify the types of benefit an AI project can produce and convert each into a defensible money value against a measured baseline.
4. Calculate return on investment, payback period and net present value, and run a sensitivity analysis that finds the break-even assumption.
5. Work out the unit economics of an AI feature, including cost per task and the effect of routing work to cheaper models.
6. Design a pilot that proves benefits, using control groups and a difference-in-differences comparison.
7. Apply the main change management frameworks, including Lewin's three stages, Kotter's eight steps, ADKAR and the diffusion of innovations, to an AI rollout.
8. Plan an AI rollout with a sponsor, champions, workflow-based training, adoption metrics and reinforcement, and handle resistance honestly.
9. Govern a portfolio of AI projects with stage gates and continue, expand or stop decisions.
10. Measure the cost and time value of real tasks on the Sales King Academy platform and turn them into a simple business case.

## 1. Why AI projects need a business case

Many businesses buy AI tools the way people buy gym memberships: with good intentions, a vague sense that it will be good for them, and no plan for measuring whether it was. A few months later, nobody can say whether the subscription is paying for itself. Some tools quietly become essential; others are used by one enthusiast and forgotten by everyone else. Without a business case and a measurement plan, the business cannot tell which is which.

### Return on investment

**Return on investment (ROI)** compares what a project gains with what it costs:

ROI = (total benefit − total cost) ÷ total cost

An ROI of 100 percent means the project returned its cost plus the same amount again. An ROI of zero means it broke even. A negative ROI means it lost money. ROI is simple and widely understood, but it ignores timing: a project that returns its money in three months and one that takes three years can show the same ROI. That is why this chapter also uses payback period and net present value.

### Types of value

AI projects in sales and marketing produce value in five main ways.

- **Cost reduction**: doing the same work with less paid time or fewer outside services, such as drafting proposals faster or handling routine support questions automatically.
- **Revenue increase**: winning more deals, larger deals or keeping customers longer, for example through faster lead response, better personalization or more sales conversations per week.
- **Error and risk reduction**: fewer wrong prices, missed renewals, compliance breaches or lost leads.
- **Speed**: shorter time from enquiry to quote, or from sign-up to first value, which often feeds revenue indirectly.
- **Capability**: doing something that was not possible before, such as offering support outside office hours or researching every target account before a call.

The first three are the easiest to express in money. Speed and capability are real but harder to value, and business cases that rest mainly on them should say so openly rather than attach precise figures to vague gains.

### Adoption decides the return

The most important fact about AI ROI is that the benefit side depends on people. A drafting tool saves time only when salespeople use it for their drafts. A lead score improves results only when salespeople call in score order. A chatbot reduces support load only when customers use it and it answers well enough that they do not phone anyway. For this reason, change management is not an extra cost bolted onto an AI project. It is part of the investment, and the business case should include its cost and its effect on the timing of benefits.

## 2. The cost side: total cost of ownership

Business cases usually understate costs. The licence fee is visible on the invoice; the rest is spread across people's time and other budgets. The **total cost of ownership (TCO)** includes everything the business spends to get the project working and keep it working.

### One-time costs

- **Selection and evaluation**: the time spent comparing tools, running trials and testing output, as described in Chapter 1.
- **Integration**: connecting the tool to the CRM, email platform, website or billing system, whether by configuring connectors or paying a developer.
- **Data preparation**: cleaning, deduplicating and mapping the data the tool needs. Chapter 8 showed that this is often the largest part of an automation project.
- **Content and configuration**: writing the instructions, templates, approved claims and knowledge material the AI will draw on.
- **Initial training**: the hours staff spend learning the new way of working, valued at the cost of their time, plus any trainer's fees.
- **Change management**: the time of the sponsor, champions and managers spent communicating, coaching and following up.

### Recurring costs

- **Licences or subscriptions**: per user, per month or per year.
- **Usage**: many AI services charge per request, per token, per minute of audio or per second of compute. These costs scale with volume and can grow faster than expected when adoption succeeds.
- **Human review and oversight**: the time people spend checking AI output before it reaches customers, reviewing samples and handling the cases the AI hands over.
- **Maintenance**: updating prompts and knowledge as products and prices change, fixing broken integrations, re-testing after a vendor changes its model.
- **Ongoing training**: onboarding new staff and refreshing existing staff when the tool changes.
- **Administration**: managing accounts, permissions, security reviews and vendor relationships.

### Costs that are easy to forget

- **The productivity dip.** In the first weeks, people work more slowly as they learn. This is a real cost, often larger than the licence.
- **Exit costs.** If the tool is dropped later, data may need to be exported and workflows rebuilt. Lock-in has a price even if it is never paid.
- **Risk costs.** An AI error that reaches a customer, such as a wrong price or an inappropriate message, can cost refunds, staff time and reputation. Good design lowers this risk, but a prudent business case acknowledges it.
- **Opportunity cost.** The time and money spent on this project could have gone to another. That is why portfolio reviews (Section 10) compare projects against each other.

## 3. The benefit side: measuring against a baseline

Benefits must be measured against a **baseline**: the level of performance before the change. Without a baseline, any later number is just a number. Measure the baseline before the project starts, over a long enough period to smooth out normal variation (several weeks at least, and longer for seasonal businesses), using the same definitions you will use afterwards.

### Time saved and redeployment

The most common AI benefit claim is time saved. It is also the most commonly overstated. Saving a salesperson four hours a week has money value only if those hours are used for something valuable: more customer conversations, better preparation, or work that would otherwise have needed extra staff or overtime. If the saved time is absorbed into longer lunches and more email checking, the business has gained comfort, not money.

A defensible business case therefore applies a **redeployment factor**: the share of saved time that is actually turned into valuable work. The factor should be estimated from a pilot where possible, and set conservatively when not. Then:

Value of time saved = hours saved × redeployment factor × value per hour

The value per hour can be the cost of the employee's time (salary plus employment costs), which is appropriate when the saved time avoids hiring or overtime. For revenue-producing roles, a better measure is the revenue that extra selling time produces, but that requires evidence that more time really does produce more sales.

### Revenue gains

Revenue benefits should be measured as incremental: the revenue that would not have come in without the project. Chapter 8 showed how holdout groups measure this for automations. Section 6 of this chapter shows how pilots with comparison groups measure it for tools used by teams.

### Error reduction and cost avoidance

To value error reduction, count the errors in the baseline period, estimate the cost of each (refunds, rework hours, lost customers) and measure the reduction after the change. For example, if missed renewal reminders lost an average of three customers a quarter before an automated renewal workflow, and the workflow cuts that to one, the benefit is two customers a quarter times their lifetime value, as calculated in Chapter 9.

### Worked example 1: A business case for an AI drafting assistant

A sales team of 12 people is considering an AI assistant that drafts follow-up emails, call summaries and proposals. The figures below are illustrative; a real business would use its own measured numbers and actual vendor prices.

**Costs in year 1**

- Licences: $40 per user per month × 12 users × 12 months = $5,760.
- Integration with the CRM (one-time): $8,000.
- Training (one-time): 12 people × 6 hours × $45 per hour = $3,240.
- Ongoing review and administration: 2 hours per week × 52 weeks × $45 per hour = $4,680.

Total year-1 cost: 5,760 + 8,000 + 3,240 + 4,680 = $21,680.

**Benefits in year 1**

A two-week trial with three salespeople measured an average saving of 4 hours per person per week. The team works 46 weeks a year. Gross time saved: 12 × 4 × 46 = 2,208 hours.

The sales manager estimates, conservatively, that half of the saved time will become extra customer conversations and preparation. Redeployed hours: 2,208 × 0.5 = 1,104.

Valuing those hours at $45: 1,104 × $45 = $49,680.

**ROI**

ROI = (49,680 − 21,680) ÷ 21,680 ≈ 1.292, or about 129 percent.

**Payback**

One-time costs are $8,000 + $3,240 = $11,240. Recurring costs are ($5,760 + $4,680) ÷ 12 = $870 a month. Benefits are $49,680 ÷ 12 = $4,140 a month. Net monthly benefit: 4,140 − 870 = $3,270.

Payback period: 11,240 ÷ 3,270 ≈ 3.4 months.

The case looks strong, but every benefit figure depends on two assumptions: that all 12 people use the tool as the three trial users did, from day one, and that half the saved time is redeployed. Worked examples 2 and 5 test both.

## 4. Financial measures: ROI, payback and net present value

ROI and payback are useful first measures. For projects whose benefits and costs run over several years, a fuller picture needs to account for the time value of money.

### Payback period

The **payback period** is the time it takes for cumulative net benefits to repay the upfront investment. It is easy to explain and favours projects that return cash quickly, which matters to small businesses with limited cash. Its weakness is that it ignores everything after payback: a project that pays back in four months and then stops producing value looks better than one that pays back in six months and produces value for years.

### Net present value

Money received in the future is worth less than money today, because money today can be invested or used to reduce borrowing, and because the future is uncertain. **Discounting** converts future amounts to their value today using a **discount rate**, which reflects the business's cost of capital or the return it could earn elsewhere, plus an allowance for risk.

The present value of an amount received t years from now is:

PV = amount ÷ (1 + r)^t

where r is the annual discount rate.

**Net present value (NPV)** is the sum of the present values of all future net benefits, minus the upfront investment. A positive NPV means the project is expected to create more value than it costs, after allowing for the time value of money and the chosen rate of return. When comparing projects, the one with the higher NPV creates more value, though projects of very different sizes need judgment as well as arithmetic.

**Internal rate of return (IRR)** is the discount rate at which a project's NPV is exactly zero. It is often quoted alongside NPV; a project whose IRR is above the business's required rate of return is attractive. IRR is usually found with a spreadsheet function rather than by hand.

### Sensitivity analysis and break-even

Every business case rests on assumptions. **Sensitivity analysis** asks how the result changes when an assumption changes. The most useful form identifies the **break-even value** of the most uncertain assumption: the value at which NPV falls to zero. If the break-even value is far from the expected value, the project is robust. If it is close, the project is risky and the assumption should be tested before committing.

### Worked example 2: Three-year NPV and the break-even redeployment rate

Continue with the drafting assistant from Worked example 1. Assume the team keeps the tool for three years, with the same recurring costs and benefits each year, and the business uses a 10 percent discount rate.

Upfront investment (year 0): $11,240.
Annual net benefit (years 1 to 3): $49,680 − ($5,760 + $4,680) = $49,680 − $10,440 = $39,240.

Present values:

- Year 1: 39,240 ÷ 1.10 ≈ $35,672.73
- Year 2: 39,240 ÷ 1.21 ≈ $32,429.75
- Year 3: 39,240 ÷ 1.331 ≈ $29,481.59

NPV = −11,240 + 35,672.73 + 32,429.75 + 29,481.59 ≈ $86,344.07.

**Sensitivity.** Suppose only a quarter of saved time is redeployed instead of half. Annual benefit halves to $24,840, and annual net benefit becomes $24,840 − $10,440 = $14,400. The present values are about $13,090.91, $11,900.83 and $10,818.93, and NPV ≈ −11,240 + 35,810.67 ≈ $24,570.67. The project still creates value, though far less.

**Break-even.** At what redeployment factor f does NPV reach zero? Gross hours saved are 2,208 a year, worth 2,208 × $45 = $99,360 if fully redeployed. The sum of the discount factors for years 1 to 3 at 10 percent is about 2.4869. NPV is zero when:

2.4869 × (99,360 × f − 10,440) = 11,240

Solving, 99,360 × f − 10,440 ≈ 4,519.77, so f ≈ 14,959.77 ÷ 99,360 ≈ 0.151.

The project breaks even if about 15 percent of saved time is redeployed. The manager's estimate of 50 percent is far above that, so on this assumption the project is robust. The remaining risk is the other one: that the time is not saved at all because people do not use the tool. That is a change management problem, addressed from Section 7 onwards.

## 5. The unit economics of AI features

Some AI costs are fixed, like a subscription. Others vary with every use, like paying per request or per second of compute. For any AI feature used at volume, whether inside your own operations or offered to your customers, you need to understand its **unit economics**: the cost and value of a single unit of work, such as one drafted email, one answered question or one conversation.

### Cost per task

Cost per task is the variable cost of doing the task once: the model usage, any speech or search charges, and the human review time per task averaged across tasks. Chapter 1 showed how to estimate model usage from tokens. Multiply cost per task by expected volume to get the variable cost per month, and compare it with the value per task.

Two volume effects deserve attention. First, success raises cost: a feature that customers love will be used more, and if each use costs money, the bill grows with popularity. Second, a few heavy users can dominate cost, so look at the distribution of use, not just the average.

### Levers on unit cost

- **Model routing.** Send each task to the smallest model that does it well enough. Classification, short factual answers and simple drafts often need only a small model; complex reasoning and long, high-stakes documents may justify a large one. Routing can also follow the customer's plan: free users get a capable small model, and paying users get the larger ones.
- **Reuse.** If the same question gets the same answer, store it and serve the stored answer instead of generating it again. This cuts cost and improves consistency.
- **Trimming context.** Most of the cost of a request often comes from the material sent to the model. Send only what the task needs.
- **Deterministic paths.** Answer from exact systems where possible, using generation only where it adds value.
- **Pricing.** For features offered to customers, price usage so that each unit covers its cost and contributes margin, or limit free use to an amount the business can afford.

### Worked example 3: Routing by plan

A business runs a chat assistant inside its product. Each month it handles 50,000 messages from free-plan users and 10,000 from paying users. At first, every message goes to a large model. For illustration, suppose the large model costs $0.02 per message and a small model costs $0.001 per message; real costs depend on the provider, the model and the length of each message.

**Before routing:** 60,000 × $0.02 = $1,200 a month.

**After routing** free-plan messages to the small model and keeping paying users on the large model:

- Free plan: 50,000 × $0.001 = $50
- Paying: 10,000 × $0.02 = $200
- Total: $250 a month

Saving: 1,200 − 250 = $950 a month, or about 79.2 percent.

Before the change, five-sixths of the model bill was spent on users who paid nothing. After it, most of the bill is spent on paying users, whose fees can cover it. The business must check that the small model is good enough for free-plan users, since a poor free experience may stop them upgrading, and it should tell users plainly what each plan includes. But the economics change from a cost that grows with every free user to one that grows mainly with revenue.

## 6. Proving the benefit: pilots and comparisons

A business case is a forecast. A pilot turns the forecast into evidence. The aim is to measure what changed because of the AI project, separated from everything else that changed at the same time.

### Why before-and-after is not enough

Comparing a team's results before and after a tool was introduced is the simplest approach, but it is easily fooled. Results might have improved anyway because of the season, a new product, a price change, a strong economy or simply because the team knew it was being watched. A before-and-after comparison credits all of that to the tool.

### Comparison groups

A stronger design compares a **pilot group** that uses the tool with a **comparison group** that does not, over the same period. Both groups experience the same season, market and products, so differences between them are more likely to be caused by the tool. The best design assigns people or territories to the two groups at random; when that is not possible, choose groups that are as similar as possible and check their baseline performance.

### Difference-in-differences

When the two groups did not start at exactly the same level, **difference-in-differences** gives a fairer estimate. It measures the change in the pilot group, subtracts the change in the comparison group over the same period, and treats the remainder as the effect of the tool. The method assumes that, without the tool, both groups would have changed by about the same amount; checking that their trends were similar before the pilot makes that assumption more believable.

### Worked example 4: Did the AI research assistant add meetings?

A company pilots an AI tool that researches each prospect and drafts a tailored first email. One sales team of 8 people uses it for a quarter; a similar team does not.

| | Before (meetings per rep per week) | During pilot | Change |
|---|---|---|---|
| Pilot team | 6.0 | 7.5 | +1.5 |
| Comparison team | 6.2 | 6.6 | +0.4 |

A before-and-after view of the pilot team alone would credit the tool with 1.5 extra meetings per rep per week.

But the comparison team also improved, by 0.4, without the tool, perhaps because of a seasonal rise in demand. The difference-in-differences estimate is 1.5 − 0.4 = 1.1 extra meetings per rep per week.

To put a value on it, suppose the company's records show that 20 percent of meetings lead to a sale averaging $3,000, and that the team works 46 weeks a year. Extra meetings for the pilot team over a year: 1.1 × 8 × 46 = 404.8. Expected extra revenue: 404.8 × 0.20 × $3,000 = $242,880.

That figure rests on the conversion rate of the extra meetings being the same as for ordinary meetings, which may not hold: AI-researched outreach might win meetings with less interested prospects. The next step is to track the extra meetings through to closed deals before extending the tool to every team.

### Pilot design checklist

- Choose one primary metric close to business value, and define it before the pilot starts.
- Measure the baseline for both groups.
- Run the pilot long enough to get past the learning dip; a few weeks is rarely enough for tools that change how people work.
- Record usage, not just outcomes, so you can tell a tool that does not work from a tool that was not used.
- Collect qualitative feedback: what users liked, what they worked around, what they stopped doing.
- Decide in advance what result would lead you to expand, adjust or stop.

## 7. Why adoption decides ROI

Even a well-chosen, well-integrated tool can fail if people do not change how they work. This section explains why adoption is slow and uneven, and how to build it into the numbers.

### The adoption curve

New practices spread through a group unevenly. The sociologist Everett Rogers, in his work on the **diffusion of innovations**, described adopters in five groups: innovators (about 2.5 percent of a population), early adopters (about 13.5 percent), the early majority (about 34 percent), the late majority (about 34 percent) and laggards (about 16 percent). The percentages come from treating adoption times as a bell-shaped distribution; real teams vary, but the pattern is familiar to every manager. A few people try the new tool at once, a larger group waits to see whether it works for colleagues, and some resist until it becomes the only way to do the job.

Rogers also identified qualities that speed adoption: a clear **relative advantage** over the old way, **compatibility** with existing values and work, low **complexity**, the ability to **try it** on a small scale and **observability**, meaning others can see the results. These map directly onto practical rollout choices: show the advantage with the team's own numbers, fit the tool into existing workflows, keep the first use simple, offer a low-risk trial and make early wins visible.

### Usefulness and ease of use

The **technology acceptance model**, proposed by Fred Davis in 1989, holds that people's intention to use a technology depends mainly on two beliefs: that it is useful for their work (**perceived usefulness**) and that it is easy to use (**perceived ease of use**). For AI tools, usefulness is often undermined by early experiences of wrong or generic output, and ease of use by tools that sit outside the systems people already work in. Both are design problems as much as training problems: a tool that drafts inside the CRM, from the CRM's own data, scores better on both counts than one that requires copying and pasting into a separate window.

### The productivity dip

When people change how they work, performance usually falls before it rises. This pattern, often called the **J-curve**, happens because the old habits stop working before the new ones are fluent. A business case that assumes full benefits from the first day ignores the dip, and a manager who judges the tool in its first fortnight may cancel it at the bottom of the curve.

### Worked example 5: Adoption-adjusted payback

Return to the drafting assistant. Worked example 1 assumed full benefits of $4,140 a month from the first month. A more realistic plan assumes that 20 percent of the planned benefit is achieved in month 1, 40 percent in month 2, 60 percent in month 3 and 80 percent from month 4 onwards, because some people adopt slowly and a few never fully adopt.

Recurring costs stay at $870 a month, and the upfront cost is $11,240.

| Month | Adoption | Benefit | Net (benefit − $870) | Cumulative (after −$11,240 upfront) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 20% | $828 | −$42 | −$11,282 |
| 2 | 40% | $1,656 | $786 | −$10,496 |
| 3 | 60% | $2,484 | $1,614 | −$8,882 |
| 4 | 80% | $3,312 | $2,442 | −$6,440 |
| 5 | 80% | $3,312 | $2,442 | −$3,998 |
| 6 | 80% | $3,312 | $2,442 | −$1,556 |
| 7 | 80% | $3,312 | $2,442 | $886 |

Cumulative net benefit turns positive in month 7, against 3.4 months in the simple case. Over the full first year, benefits total $34,776 instead of $49,680, and first-year ROI falls from about 129 percent to (34,776 − 21,680) ÷ 21,680 ≈ 60.4 percent.

The project is still worthwhile. But the example shows that slower adoption roughly doubled the payback period, and that the gap between 80 percent and 100 percent adoption is worth $828 a month. Spending a few hundred dollars more on coaching the slowest adopters could pay for itself quickly. Change management has a measurable return of its own.

## 8. Change management frameworks

**Change management** is the structured approach to moving people from the current way of working to a new one, so that the change is adopted and lasts. Several frameworks are widely used. They differ in emphasis, but they agree on more than they disagree, and each offers a useful lens on an AI rollout.

### Lewin's three stages

The psychologist Kurt Lewin described change in three stages. **Unfreeze**: people must see why the current way is no longer good enough, which loosens attachment to existing habits. **Change**: people learn and practise the new way, with support. **Refreeze**: the new way is embedded in routines, systems, measures and rewards so that it becomes normal and people do not slide back. The model is simple, and its main lesson for AI rollouts is the third stage: a tool introduced without changing the processes, targets and reports around it will be dropped once the launch excitement fades.

### Kotter's eight steps

John Kotter, writing about why large organisational changes fail, set out eight steps: establish a sense of urgency; create a guiding coalition of people with enough influence to lead the change; develop a vision and strategy; communicate the change vision; empower broad-based action by removing obstacles; generate short-term wins; consolidate gains and produce more change; and anchor new approaches in the culture. For a small business, the steps compress, but the logic holds. The owner must explain why the change matters now, recruit a few respected people to lead it, describe clearly what the new way of working looks like, remove the obstacles that stop people using it (such as missing integrations or unclear rules), celebrate the first measurable wins and then make the new way part of how the business runs.

### ADKAR

The **ADKAR** model, developed by Jeffrey Hiatt at the change management firm Prosci, focuses on the individual. It holds that each person must reach five outcomes in order: **Awareness** of why the change is needed, **Desire** to take part, **Knowledge** of how to change, **Ability** to apply that knowledge in real work and **Reinforcement** that sustains the change. ADKAR is especially useful for diagnosing where a rollout is stuck. A salesperson who understands the tool (knowledge) but does not want to use it (desire) needs a different response from one who wants to use it but cannot make it work on their own accounts (ability).

### Comparing the frameworks

| Framework | Focus | Most useful for |
|---|---|---|
| Lewin | The organisation's overall movement | Remembering to embed change in systems and rewards |
| Kotter | Leadership actions in sequence | Planning what leaders do and when |
| ADKAR | Each individual's journey | Diagnosing why particular people have not adopted |
| Diffusion of innovations | How adoption spreads through a group | Choosing whom to start with and how to show results |
| Technology acceptance model | Beliefs that drive use of a tool | Designing the tool and the first experience |

A practical approach uses Kotter or Lewin to plan the overall rollout, diffusion of innovations to choose the first users, and ADKAR to diagnose individual resistance.

### Understanding resistance

Resistance to AI tools is rarely irrational. People resist for reasons that deserve respect and a direct answer:

- **Job security.** People worry that a tool which does part of their job will eventually replace them. Vague reassurance makes this worse. Leaders should say honestly what the business intends, what roles will change, and what support people will receive.
- **Loss of competence.** Experienced staff who are good at the old way may feel like beginners again. Involving them in shaping the new process, and recognising their expertise in reviewing AI output, turns a threat into a role.
- **Bad first experiences.** A tool that produced a wrong answer in the first week loses credibility. Fixing the issue and showing the fix restores trust better than telling people to keep trying.
- **Extra work.** If the tool adds steps, such as copying data between systems, people will avoid it. Remove the friction.
- **Unclear rules.** People unsure what they are allowed to send, or who is responsible if the AI makes a mistake, will play safe and not use it. Write the rules down.
- **Monitoring fears.** If the tool records activity, people may suspect it will be used to watch them. Be clear about what is measured, why and who sees it.

## 9. Running the rollout

This section turns the frameworks into a practical plan for introducing an AI tool to a team.

### Roles

- **Executive sponsor**: the owner or senior leader who explains why the change matters, commits resources, removes obstacles and visibly uses the tool. When the sponsor stops paying attention, adoption usually stalls.
- **Project owner**: the person accountable for the business case, the rollout plan and the results.
- **Champions**: respected members of each team who use the tool first, help colleagues, collect problems and report back. Champions should be chosen for credibility with peers, not just enthusiasm for technology.
- **Managers**: the people who coach their teams day to day, include the new way in one-to-one meetings and reinforce it.

### A step-by-step rollout

1. **Write the one-page business case**: the problem, the metric, the baseline, the expected improvement, the full cost, the break-even assumption and the review date.
2. **Define the new workflow.** Describe exactly how a typical task will be done with the tool, step by step, and what changes for each role. Train on this, not on the tool's features.
3. **Set the rules.** What the tool may and may not be used for, what must be reviewed before it reaches a customer, what data may be entered, and who is responsible for output.
4. **Pilot with champions and early adopters.** Fix problems while the group is small. Measure usage and outcomes against a comparison group where possible.
5. **Prepare the wider launch.** Use the pilot results, in the team's own numbers, to show the advantage. Prepare short workflow guides and examples from real work.
6. **Train by workflow.** Hold short sessions organised around daily tasks ("preparing for a discovery call", "writing a proposal"), using participants' own accounts and documents. Follow up with practice and coaching, not just a single session.
7. **Launch in waves.** Expand team by team, with a champion in each.
8. **Measure adoption and outcomes weekly at first**: active users, share of eligible tasks done with the tool, quality checks, and the business metric.
9. **Reinforce.** Include the new workflow in targets, reviews and recognition. Share wins publicly. Retire the old way once the new one is working, so people do not run both.
10. **Review at the planned date** and decide whether to continue, expand, adjust or stop.

### Adoption metrics

Measure adoption in layers, because each tells you something different.

- **Access**: how many people have accounts and have logged in at least once.
- **Activity**: how many use the tool in a typical week.
- **Depth**: what share of eligible tasks are done with the tool; for example, the share of proposals drafted with it.
- **Quality**: how often output needs heavy editing or is rejected, from review samples.
- **Outcome**: the business metric the project was meant to move.

A tool with high access and low depth has been installed but not adopted. A tool with high depth and poor outcomes is being used but is not helping, and needs redesign rather than more training.

### Communication

Communicate early, honestly and repeatedly. Explain why the change is happening, what will change for each person, what will not change, what support is available and how success will be measured. Give people a way to raise problems and show that problems raised are acted on. Communication from the sponsor matters most at the start; communication from managers and champions matters most during adoption.

### The ethics of workforce change

AI changes jobs. Some tasks disappear, others grow, and some roles change shape entirely. A responsible business plans for this rather than letting it happen by accident. Practical commitments include: being honest about the purpose of the change; offering training so people can move into the work that grows; involving staff in redesigning their own workflows; being careful and transparent about any monitoring of employees' use of AI tools; and making sure that people, not tools, remain accountable for decisions about customers and colleagues. Businesses should also check the employment, data protection and consultation rules that apply where they operate, which in some places require staff to be informed or consulted before monitoring tools or significant changes to work are introduced.

### Case study: Ridgeway Insurance Brokers rolls out an AI assistant

This case describes a fictional company. Ridgeway Insurance Brokers is not a real business, and its details are invented for teaching.

Ridgeway is a brokerage with 30 staff across three offices. Its owner bought an AI assistant to help brokers summarise client calls, draft renewal letters and compare policy options. She announced it in an all-staff email, gave everyone a login and asked them to "start using it".

**What went wrong.** Six weeks later, the usage report showed that 9 of 30 staff had logged in more than once, and only 3 used it weekly. In conversations, brokers gave several reasons. The assistant sat in a separate window, so using it meant copying client details out of the brokerage system. Two senior brokers had seen it produce a confident but wrong summary of a policy exclusion and concluded it could not be trusted. Several people were unsure whether they were allowed to paste client information into it. And a rumour had spread that the tool was the first step in closing one of the offices.

**What Ridgeway did.** The owner paused and restarted the rollout. She held a meeting in each office to explain her reasons, in her own words: the brokerage was losing renewals because letters went out late, and she wanted brokers spending more time advising clients, not formatting documents. She stated plainly that no office closure was planned and that no roles were being cut because of the tool. She asked one respected broker in each office to act as champion.

The champions and the owner wrote down the rules: client data could be entered only into the approved tool, which had been reviewed for data protection; every letter and every summary of policy terms had to be checked by a broker before use; and policy exclusions and coverage limits must always be taken from the policy documents, never from the assistant's summary. The owner paid for the integration that let the assistant draft from the brokerage system directly, removing the copying step.

Training was redone around three workflows: preparing a renewal, summarising a client call and drafting a comparison letter, each practised on real (anonymised) files. One of the senior brokers who had found the wrong summary was asked to build the checklist for reviewing policy summaries, and became one of the tool's most careful users. The owner tracked the share of renewal letters sent on time as the main outcome, alongside weekly active users.

**What it shows.** The first launch skipped almost every element of change management: urgency, a guiding coalition, clear rules, removing obstacles, workflow training and honest communication about jobs. The second addressed each of them. The technology did not change at all between the two launches, apart from the integration; the difference in adoption came from how people were led through the change.

## 10. Governing a portfolio of AI projects

Once a business runs several AI projects, it needs a way to manage them together, the way an investor manages a portfolio.

### Stage gates

A **stage-gate** process sets decision points through a project's life. A simple version has four gates:

1. **Idea to pilot**: is there a clear problem, a measurable metric and a rough business case? Is the data available, and are the risks acceptable?
2. **Pilot to rollout**: did the pilot show a measured benefit against a baseline or comparison group, at an acceptable cost, with adoption that suggests the wider team will use it?
3. **Rollout to steady state**: has adoption reached the planned level, and are benefits appearing as forecast?
4. **Periodic review**: is the project still earning its place against other uses of the money?

At each gate, the decision is one of four: **continue**, **expand**, **adjust** or **stop**.

### Benefits realisation

**Benefits realisation** is the practice of checking, after a project goes live, that the benefits in the business case actually appear. It sounds obvious, but many organisations approve projects on forecast benefits and never check. Set a review date in the business case, compare actual with forecast, and record the reasons for any gap. Over time, this builds the business's knowledge of which kinds of project deliver and which assumptions are usually too optimistic.

### Kill criteria

Stopping a project is hard once money and reputation have been spent on it. The temptation to keep going in the hope of recovering the investment is called the **sunk cost fallacy**: past spending cannot be recovered whatever you decide, so only future costs and benefits should count. Writing **kill criteria** into the business case at the start makes stopping easier. For example: "If weekly active use is below 50 percent of the team after 90 days of the full rollout, or if the measured benefit is below the break-even value at the six-month review, we stop or redesign."

### Re-testing after change

AI tools change underneath you. Vendors update models, change prices and add or remove features. A project that was profitable at one price, or accurate with one model version, may not be with the next. Treat each significant vendor change as a reason to re-check both quality, using a saved test set as Chapter 1 described, and unit economics.

### Common mistakes

- **Counting time saved that is never used.** Apply a redeployment factor based on evidence.
- **Leaving out hidden costs.** Include integration, data preparation, review, maintenance and the productivity dip.
- **No baseline.** Measure before the change, or the benefit can never be proven.
- **Assuming instant full adoption.** Model the adoption curve and the J-curve.
- **Announcing a tool without leading the change.** Provide a reason, rules, workflow training, champions and reinforcement.
- **Training on features instead of workflows.** People adopt what fits their daily work.
- **Ignoring unit economics.** Usage-based costs grow with success; route, reuse and price accordingly.
- **Keeping failing projects alive.** Set kill criteria in advance and honour them.
- **Being vague about jobs.** Uncertainty breeds resistance; be honest.

## SKA Field Case Study: Paying for AI where it earns its keep

This case comes from the operation of Sales King Academy itself, the platform on which you are taking this course. It concerns decisions made in September and October 2026 about what the platform's AI should cost, who it should be spent on and how to tell whether an investment in a model is working.

### The situation

Sales King Academy is run by one founder, who makes every engineering, product and spending decision. The platform offers 26 specialist AI agents, courses, a CRM, automations and Agent Builder, with usage paid in Beats, the platform's currency, at 1 Beat to 1 US dollar. Usage is metered by seconds of compute, and only full seconds are charged. Users on free and explorer tiers can use the platform alongside paying users.

Three facts set the economic context. On 10 September 2026, the platform moved to Cloudflare's free plan to keep fixed costs down; that move disabled R2 file storage and tightened database limits. On 6 October it returned to Cloudflare's Workers paid plan, at $5 a month. And the platform has its own small transformer model, of about 22 million parameters, which the founder is training.

### The problems it caused

**Spending the most on users who paid least.** An audit on 7 October found that unpaid users had been served by the most expensive model. Every free conversation therefore carried the highest cost per message, while producing no revenue. This is the pattern in Worked example 3: a variable cost that grows with free usage rather than with revenue.

**Fixed-cost savings that cost capability.** The free plan removed a $5 monthly charge but disabled file storage and tightened database limits, so the platform had to work within hard constraints that affected what it could do for users.

**A model investment that was not doing what it seemed.** On 8 October, testing of the platform's own 22-million-parameter transformer found that it copied its source passage rather than answering the question. Training had produced a model that looked as if it was working on the measure being watched, but had not learned the skill that mattered.

### What was done

**Routing by tier.** Routing was changed so that free and explorer tiers use a small free model, and paid tiers get the large models. The expensive capability now goes to the users whose payments can support it, while free users still receive answers.

**Returning to the paid plan.** On 6 October the platform returned to the Workers paid plan at $5 a month, judging that the capability restored was worth more than the fee saved.

**Changing how the model is trained and measured.** The retraining plan for the platform's own model switched to question-and-answer pairs drawn from the curriculum, so that it is trained, and can be tested, on answering questions rather than reproducing passages.

### What it shows

The case illustrates five points from this chapter.

1. **Unit economics decide whether a feature can scale.** Serving free users with the most expensive model made cost grow with the wrong thing. Routing by tier ties cost to revenue.
2. **The cheapest option is not always the best value.** Saving $5 a month on hosting cost capability; the platform judged the paid plan to be worth its fee. A business case must weigh what a saving takes away.
3. **Measure what was actually achieved.** The in-house model appeared to work until it was tested on the real task. Benefits realisation means checking the outcome that matters, not the activity that is easy to see.
4. **Investments need kill-or-redesign moments.** Finding that the model copied rather than answered led to a redesign of its training, not to continued spending on the same approach.
5. **Change affects users.** Moving free users to a smaller model changes their experience. Communicating clearly what each tier includes is part of managing that change.

### What remains open

Several figures are needed to judge these decisions fully, and only the founder holds them. What was the model cost per month before and after routing by tier? Did the change affect how many free users upgraded to paid tiers? What does the retrained model achieve on a held-out set of curriculum questions, compared with the rented models it might one day replace, and at what cost per answer?

## Summary

The return on an AI project depends on both its financial design and its adoption. ROI compares total benefit with total cost; payback shows how quickly the upfront investment is repaid; net present value accounts for the time value of money; and sensitivity analysis finds the break-even value of the most uncertain assumption.

The total cost of ownership includes integration, data preparation, training, change management, usage, review, maintenance, the productivity dip and exit costs, not just licences. Benefits must be measured against a baseline. Time saved has money value only when it is redeployed, so business cases should apply an evidence-based redeployment factor. Revenue and error-reduction benefits should be measured as incremental, using pilots with comparison groups and methods such as difference-in-differences.

AI features used at volume need sound unit economics: cost per task, routing to the smallest adequate model, reuse of stored answers, trimmed context and pricing that covers usage.

Adoption spreads unevenly through a group and performance often dips before it improves, so business cases should model an adoption curve. Change management frameworks, including Lewin's three stages, Kotter's eight steps, ADKAR, the diffusion of innovations and the technology acceptance model, guide the rollout. Practical rollouts need a sponsor, champions, workflow-based training, clear rules, honest communication about jobs, layered adoption metrics and reinforcement. Portfolios of AI projects are governed with stage gates, benefits realisation reviews and kill criteria set in advance.

The Sales King Academy field case showed routing by tier tying model cost to revenue, a hosting decision weighed against the capability it removed, and a model investment redesigned after testing showed it had not learned the intended skill.

## Key terms

- **Return on investment (ROI)**: total benefit minus total cost, divided by total cost.
- **Total cost of ownership (TCO)**: all one-time and recurring costs of getting a project working and keeping it working.
- **Baseline**: the measured level of performance before a change.
- **Redeployment factor**: the share of saved time that is turned into valuable work.
- **Payback period**: the time for cumulative net benefits to repay the upfront investment.
- **Discount rate**: the annual rate used to convert future amounts into today's value.
- **Net present value (NPV)**: the sum of discounted future net benefits minus the upfront investment.
- **Internal rate of return (IRR)**: the discount rate at which a project's NPV is zero.
- **Sensitivity analysis**: testing how results change when assumptions change.
- **Break-even value**: the value of an assumption at which NPV falls to zero.
- **Unit economics**: the cost and value of a single unit of work, such as one answered question.
- **Model routing**: sending each task to the smallest model that does it well enough.
- **Pilot**: a limited trial run to produce evidence before a wider rollout.
- **Comparison group**: a group that does not receive the change, used to separate its effect from other influences.
- **Difference-in-differences**: the change in a pilot group minus the change in a comparison group over the same period.
- **Diffusion of innovations**: Rogers's account of how new practices spread through a group, from innovators to laggards.
- **Technology acceptance model**: Davis's model in which perceived usefulness and ease of use drive intention to use a technology.
- **J-curve**: the pattern in which performance dips after a change before rising above its earlier level.
- **Change management**: the structured approach to moving people to a new way of working so the change is adopted and lasts.
- **ADKAR**: a model of individual change through Awareness, Desire, Knowledge, Ability and Reinforcement.
- **Champion**: a respected team member who adopts first, helps colleagues and reports problems.
- **Stage gate**: a decision point at which a project is continued, expanded, adjusted or stopped.
- **Benefits realisation**: checking after go-live that forecast benefits actually appear.
- **Sunk cost fallacy**: letting past, unrecoverable spending influence a decision about the future.
- **Kill criteria**: conditions, set in advance, under which a project will be stopped or redesigned.

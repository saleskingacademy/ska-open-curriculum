## Foundations

In physics electromagnetism, core definitions are essential for understanding the behavior of electric and magnetic fields. A **charge** is a fundamental property of matter that can be either positive or negative, and it is the source of electric fields. The **electric field** (E) is a vector field that surrounds charged particles and exerts a force on other charged particles, with its magnitude measured in units of newtons per coulomb (N/C). The **magnetic field** (B) is also a vector field, generated by the motion of charged particles or by changing electric fields, and its magnitude is measured in units of teslas (T).

The **Lorentz force equation** describes the force experienced by a charged particle in the presence of electric and magnetic fields: F = q(E + v × B), where F is the force, q is the charge, v is the velocity of the particle, and × denotes the cross product. **Gauss's law** relates the distribution of electric charge to the resulting electric field, stating that the flux of the electric field through a closed surface is proportional to the charge enclosed. **Ampere's law** with Maxwell's addition relates the magnetic field to the current and displacement current, which is essential for understanding the behavior of electromagnetic waves.

Understanding these foundational concepts and the vocabulary associated with them is crucial for practitioners in the field of physics electromagnetism, as they form the basis for more advanced topics such as electromagnetic induction, wave propagation, and the behavior of electromagnetic radiation.

## Mechanisms

The fundamental mechanism of electromagnetism involves the interaction between electrically charged particles, such as electrons and protons, and the electromagnetic field. The process begins with the acceleration of a charged particle, which generates a changing electric field. This changing electric field, in turn, induces a magnetic field, as described by Maxwell's equations. The magnetic field then exerts a force on other moving charged particles, causing them to accelerate. This acceleration leads to a change in the electric field, which again induces a magnetic field, and so on. The causal chain is as follows: 
1. Acceleration of a charged particle 
2. Generation of a changing electric field 
3. Induction of a magnetic field 
4. Exertion of a force on other moving charged particles 
5. Acceleration of these particles 
6. Change in the electric field 
7. Induction of another magnetic field. 
This continuous interplay between electric and magnetic fields is the underlying mechanism of electromagnetism, governing phenomena such as electromagnetic waves, electromagnetic induction, and the behavior of charged particles in magnetic fields. The Lorentz force equation, F = q(E + v x B), describes the force experienced by a charged particle in the presence of electric and magnetic fields, where F is the force, q is the charge, E is the electric field, v is the velocity of the particle, and B is the magnetic field.

The mechanisms of electromagnetism involve the interaction between electrically charged particles and the electromagnetic field. The process begins with the acceleration of charged particles, such as electrons, which creates a disturbance in the electromagnetic field. This disturbance propagates through space as an electromagnetic wave, consisting of oscillating electric and magnetic field components. The electric field component is generated by the separation of charged particles, while the magnetic field component is generated by the motion of charged particles. As the electromagnetic wave propagates, it exerts a force on other charged particles it encounters, causing them to accelerate. This acceleration, in turn, creates a new disturbance in the electromagnetic field, perpetuating the cycle. The causal chain is as follows: charged particle acceleration → electromagnetic field disturbance → electromagnetic wave propagation → force exertion on other charged particles → acceleration of other charged particles. This chain of events underlies all electromagnetic phenomena, including electromagnetic induction, electromagnetic radiation, and the transmission of electromagnetic signals. The key principle is that a changing electric field induces a magnetic field, and a changing magnetic field induces an electric field, illustrating the fundamental interconnection between electric and magnetic fields.

## Methods And Frameworks

In physics electromagnetism, several methods and frameworks are employed to analyze and solve problems. The Lorentz Force Equation, F = q(E + v × B), is used to calculate the force on a charged particle in an electromagnetic field, where q is the charge, E is the electric field, v is the velocity of the particle, and B is the magnetic field. This equation is applicable when the particle's velocity is non-relativistic. The failure mode of this equation occurs when the particle's velocity approaches the speed of light, requiring the use of relativistic corrections. 
The Biot-Savart Law, dB = (μ₀ \* I \* dl × r) / (4π \* r³), is used to calculate the magnetic field generated by a current-carrying wire, where μ₀ is the magnetic constant, I is the current, dl is the differential length of the wire, and r is the distance from the wire. This law is applicable for steady currents and fails when the current is time-varying, requiring the use of Maxwell's equations. 
Maxwell's equations, a set of four partial differential equations, provide a comprehensive framework for describing electromagnetic phenomena. They are applicable for all electromagnetic problems and are the foundation of classical electromagnetism. However, they fail to describe quantum effects, requiring the use of quantum electrodynamics. 
The method of images is used to solve electrostatic problems involving conductors and dielectrics, by replacing the conductors with equivalent charge distributions. This method is applicable when the conductors are perfect and the dielectrics are homogeneous, and fails when the conductors have finite conductivity or the dielectrics are inhomogeneous. 
The multipole expansion is used to describe the electromagnetic field of a localized charge or current distribution, by expanding the field in terms of multipoles. This method is applicable when the charge or current distribution is localized and the distance from the distribution is large compared to its size, and fails when the distance is small or the distribution is extended. Its failure mode occurs when relativistic effects become substantial, requiring the use of the relativistic Lorentz force equation. Maxwell's equations, comprising Gauss's law for electric fields, Gauss's law for magnetic fields, Faraday's law of induction, and Ampere's law with Maxwell's correction, form a comprehensive framework for analyzing electromagnetic phenomena. They are applicable for a wide range of problems, from static electric and magnetic fields to electromagnetic waves. Its failure mode occurs when the conductors are imperfect or the dielectrics are inhomogeneous, requiring the use of more complex methods such as the finite element method.

Using the formula F = BIL, where F is the force, B is the magnetic field strength, I is the current, and L is the length of the wire, we get F = 0.1 T * 5 A * 2 m = 1 N.
2. A coil of 100 turns, with a radius of 0.05 m, carries a current of 2 A. If the coil is placed in a magnetic field of strength 0.2 T, calculate the torque exerted on the coil. The magnetic moment of the coil is given by M = NIAR, where N is the number of turns, I is the current, and A is the area of the coil. The torque is given by τ = MB, where τ is the torque and B is the magnetic field strength. First, calculate the area of the coil: A = πr^2 = π(0.05 m)^2 = 0.0025 π m^2. ## Applications

One of the most significant applications is in the generation, transmission, and distribution of electrical power. Electromagnetic induction is the principle behind the operation of generators, motors, and transformers, which are essential components of power systems. Additionally, electromagnetic waves are used in wireless communication systems, such as radio, television, and mobile phones, to transmit information over long distances. In medicine, electromagnetism is used in magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) machines to produce detailed images of the body's internal structures. Electromagnetic radiation is also used in cancer treatment, such as radiation therapy, to destroy cancer cells. Furthermore, electromagnetism is used in various industrial processes, including welding, cutting, and heating materials. The principle of electromagnetic induction is also used in sensors, such as current transformers and position sensors, to measure physical quantities. Overall, the understanding of electromagnetism is crucial for the development of many modern technologies that rely on the interaction between electric and magnetic fields.

## Common Errors

In the study of physics electromagnetism, several common errors arise due to misconceptions or misapplications of fundamental principles. One such error is the incorrect assumption that the electric field inside a conductor is zero in all situations. While it is true that in electrostatic equilibrium, the electric field inside a conductor is zero, this does not hold during non-equilibrium conditions or when the conductor is moving in a magnetic field. Another mistake is neglecting the displacement current in Ampere's law, which is essential for consistency with the continuity equation and for predicting the behavior of electromagnetic waves. Additionally, some practitioners incorrectly apply the Lorentz force equation, forgetting that it describes the force on a charged particle due to both electric and magnetic fields, and that the magnetic force is always perpendicular to the velocity of the particle. These errors stem from a lack of understanding of the underlying physics and can lead to incorrect predictions and calculations. Furthermore, confusion between the terms "magnetic field" and "magnetic flux" is common, where magnetic field refers to the field itself, while magnetic flux refers to the amount of magnetic field that passes through a given area. Understanding these distinctions is crucial for accurately solving problems in electromagnetism.

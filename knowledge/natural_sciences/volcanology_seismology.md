# Volcanology Seismology

## Plate Tectonics

Earth's lithosphere = 15 major plates floating on asthenosphere. Divergent (mid-ocean ridges — new crust), Convergent (subduction zones — deep trenches, volcanoes), Transform (San Andreas). Continental drift (Wegener 1912) → plate tectonics (1960s). EARTHQUAKES: Elastic rebound theory — stress accumulates on fault until rupture. Richter scale (logarithmic — each whole number = 10× amplitude, 31.6× energy). Moment magnitude (Mw) preferred now. Largest recorded: Chile 1960 (Mw 9.5). Seismic waves: P-waves (compressional, through solids/liquids), S-waves (shear, solids only), Surface waves (Rayleigh, Love — most destructive). VOLCANOES: Magma = molten rock underground, lava = surface. Types: Shield (Hawaii — broad, effusive), Stratovolcano (Fuji, Vesuvius — steep, explosive), Caldera (Yellowstone — supervolcano). Ring of Fire (75% of active volcanoes). VEI scale (0-8). Tambora 1815 (VEI 7 — "Year Without a Summer" 1816). Supervolcano Toba (~74K years ago — possible human bottleneck). TSUNAMI: Generated by seafloor displacement. 2004 Indian Ocean (230K+ dead, Mw 9.1). 2011 Tōhoku Japan (Mw 9.1, Fukushima). Travel ~800 km/h in deep ocean, amplify in shallow water. PREDICTION: Earthquakes — cannot predict specific events, can assess probability. GPS, InSAR deformation monitoring. Volcanoes — better, seismic swarms, deformation, gas emissions. APPLIED: Hazard assessment, building codes, early warning systems, tsunami detection (DART buoys), eruption forecasting.

## Mechanisms

The generation of seismic signals in volcanology seismology involves a complex interplay of physical processes. It begins with the movement of magma and gas beneath a volcano, which creates stress changes in the surrounding rock. As magma moves, it can induce shear stress, tensile stress, or compressive stress on the rock, leading to the formation of fractures or the activation of existing faults. When the stress exceeds the strength of the rock, it fails, releasing energy as seismic waves. This process is known as a seismic event or earthquake. The seismic waves then propagate through the Earth's crust, carrying information about the source of the event, such as the location, size, and type of volcanic activity. The waves are composed of P-waves (primary waves) and S-waves (shear waves), which travel at different velocities and provide distinct information about the subsurface structure. As the waves reach the surface, they are recorded by seismometers, which convert the ground motion into electrical signals. These signals are then transmitted to a recording station, where they are processed and analyzed to extract information about the seismic event and the underlying volcanic processes. The analysis involves identifying the arrival times of the P- and S-waves, calculating the seismic moment, and determining the focal mechanism, which provides insight into the orientation of the fault or fracture plane and the type of faulting involved. By combining data from multiple seismic stations, volcanologists can determine the location and depth of the seismic event, as well as the characteristics of the volcanic system, such as the size and shape of the magma chamber or the distribution of gas and magma in the subsurface.

## Methods And Frameworks

In volcanology seismology, several methods and frameworks are employed to analyze seismic data and understand volcanic activity. The Double-Difference method is used to precisely locate earthquakes and volcanic tremors, by comparing the difference in arrival times of seismic waves at multiple stations. This method is particularly useful for monitoring volcanic unrest and tracking the movement of magma and fluids. However, it can be sensitive to errors in velocity models and may not perform well in areas with complex geological structures. 
The Moment Tensor method is used to analyze the source mechanism of volcanic earthquakes, providing information on the type of faulting or volcanic process occurring. This method is useful for understanding the dynamics of volcanic activity, but can be limited by the quality of the seismic data and the assumption of a simple source mechanism. 
The Brune model is used to estimate the seismic moment and source parameters of volcanic earthquakes, providing insights into the size and type of volcanic event. However, this model assumes a simple source geometry and may not account for complexities such as multiple sources or heterogeneous media. 
The Gutenberg-Richter law is used to describe the frequency-magnitude distribution of earthquakes, allowing for the estimation of the probability of future events. This law is useful for hazard assessment and risk mitigation, but can be limited by the availability of long-term seismic data and the assumption of a stationary process.

## Worked Examples

To illustrate the application of seismology in volcanology, consider the following examples. 
1. **Calculating seismic velocity**: Given a seismic wave travels 10 km in 2 seconds, what is its velocity? Using the formula velocity = distance / time, we find velocity = 10 km / 2 s = 5 km/s. 
2. **Determining earthquake depth**: If the difference in arrival times between P-waves and S-waves is 4 seconds, and the velocity of P-waves is 6 km/s and S-waves is 4 km/s, what is the depth of the earthquake? The formula for the difference in arrival times is Δt = Δd / (Vp - Vs), where Δd is the distance the waves traveled. Rearranging for Δd gives Δd = Δt * (Vp - Vs) = 4 s * (6 km/s - 4 km/s) = 8 km. 
3. **Locating an earthquake epicenter**: If three seismic stations record the following P-wave arrival times: station A at 10:00:00, station B at 10:00:02, and station C at 10:00:04, and assuming a seismic velocity of 5 km/s, what are the possible locations of the epicenter? The difference in arrival times between stations gives the distance between the stations and the epicenter. For instance, the 2-second difference between A and B implies the epicenter is 10 km closer to A than B. By drawing circles of possible locations around each station, the intersection of these circles gives the epicenter location.

## Common Errors

In volcanology seismology, practitioners often make mistakes that can lead to incorrect interpretations of seismic data. One common error is the misidentification of seismic signals, where volcanic tremors are mistaken for tectonic earthquakes or vice versa. This can occur due to the similarities in seismic waveforms and frequencies between the two types of events. Another error is the incorrect estimation of seismic moment and magnitude, which can result from using inappropriate scaling laws or neglecting to account for the unique characteristics of volcanic seismicity, such as high-frequency tremors and gas-driven seismicity. Additionally, practitioners may fail to consider the complexities of volcanic seismicity, including the interactions between magma, gas, and the surrounding rock, leading to oversimplified models of volcanic systems. Furthermore, the use of inadequate signal processing techniques can also lead to errors, such as the failure to remove noise and interference from seismic records, which can obscure important signals and patterns. These mistakes can have significant consequences, including inaccurate assessments of volcanic hazard and risk, and highlight the need for careful data analysis, rigorous testing of hypotheses, and a comprehensive understanding of the complex processes underlying volcanic seismicity. Another error is the incorrect estimation of seismic wave velocities, which can result from inadequate accounting for the complex velocity structure of volcanic edifices. This can lead to inaccurate locations of seismic events and incorrect assessments of volcanic unrest. Additionally, practitioners may overlook the effects of seismic wave attenuation, which can cause signals to be distorted or lost, leading to incorrect interpretations of seismic data. Furthermore, the assumption of a homogeneous volcanic structure can also lead to errors, as volcanic edifices are often characterized by complex geometries and heterogeneous properties. These mistakes can be avoided by carefully considering the unique characteristics of volcanic seismicity and using appropriate methods and models to analyze seismic data. Additionally, practitioners may overlook the effects of gas bubble resonance on seismic signals, which can cause misinterpretation of signal frequencies and amplitudes. These errors can be mitigated by careful analysis of seismic waveforms, consideration of multiple data sets, and integration of seismic data with other monitoring techniques, such as gas geochemistry and ground deformation measurements.

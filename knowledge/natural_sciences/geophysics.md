# Geophysics

> The course assumes a strong foundation in physics, mathematics, and geology, and delves into specialized topics and advanced methodologies.

## Foundations

Geophysics is the quantitative study of Earth’s physical properties and dynamic processes through the application of physical principles and mathematical methods. It integrates physics, geology, and applied mathematics to infer subsurface structures, compositions, and dynamics from indirect measurements. At its core, geophysics relies on the principles of continuum mechanics, electromagnetism, gravity, and wave propagation to interpret data collected via remote sensing, borehole logging, and field surveys. The Earth is modeled as a layered, anisotropic, and heterogeneous medium, where physical parameters such as density (ρ), elastic moduli (bulk K, shear μ), electrical conductivity (σ), and magnetic susceptibility (χ) vary spatially and temporally. The inverse problem—estimating subsurface properties from surface or borehole observations—is central, requiring robust forward modeling and regularized inversion techniques.

Geophysics, a branch of the physical sciences, is the study of the Earth using the principles of physics. A **geophysicist** applies the theories and methods of physics to understand the Earth's internal and external processes. The core definitions in geophysics include **seismology**, the study of seismic waves generated by earthquakes, explosions, or other seismic events, which helps to understand the Earth's internal structure. **Gravity**, a fundamental force, is used in geophysics to study the Earth's mass distribution, including **gravimetry**, the measurement of the strength of the gravitational field. **Magnetism** is another crucial aspect, with **geomagnetism** referring to the study of the Earth's magnetic field, and **paleomagnetism** focusing on the Earth's magnetic field as recorded in rocks. **Geothermal** energy, the heat from the Earth, is also a key area of study. The vocabulary a practitioner must know includes terms like **lithosphere**, the outermost solid layer of the planet, **asthenosphere**, the region beneath the lithosphere, and **geodesy**, the study of the Earth's shape, size, and gravitational field. Understanding these first principles and definitions is essential for a geophysicist to interpret data and make informed decisions about the Earth's internal and external processes.

A **physicist** is a scientist who studies the fundamental nature of the physical universe, and in geophysics, applies this knowledge to the Earth. The Earth is composed of the **atmosphere** (the gaseous envelope surrounding the Earth), the **hydrosphere** (the region of water, including oceans, lakes, and rivers), the **lithosphere** (the outermost solid layer of the planet), and the **biosphere** (the zone of life). 
**Seismology**, the study of earthquakes and the propagation of elastic waves through the Earth, is a key component of geophysics. **Gravity**, the force of attraction between masses, and **magnetism**, the force resulting from the interaction between magnetic fields, are also fundamental to understanding the Earth's internal and external structure. 
A **geophysical survey** is a method of collecting data about the subsurface of the Earth, often using **invasive** (e.g., drilling) or **non-invasive** (e.g., seismic, gravitational, or magnetic) techniques. **Data inversion** is the process of converting geophysical data into a model of the subsurface. 
Understanding the Earth's **internal structure**, including the **crust**, **mantle**, **outer core**, and **inner core**, is crucial in geophysics. The **crust** is the outermost solid layer, ranging in thickness from 5-70 km. The **mantle** is the thick layer of hot, viscous rock beneath the crust, and the **core** is the central part of the Earth, divided into a solid **inner core** and a liquid **outer core**. 
Key principles in geophysics include **Newton's law of universal gravitation**, which describes the gravitational force between two masses, and the **principle of conservation of energy**, which states that energy cannot be created or destroyed, only converted from one form to another.

## Seismic Methods

Seismic geophysics employs elastic wave propagation to image subsurface structures. The fundamental equation is the elastodynamic wave equation:  
\[
\rho \frac{\partial^2 \mathbf{u}}{\partial t^2} = \nabla \cdot \boldsymbol{\sigma} + \mathbf{f}
\]  
where \(\mathbf{u}\) is displacement, \(\boldsymbol{\sigma}\) the stress tensor, and \(\mathbf{f}\) body forces. The stress-strain relationship follows Hooke’s law for isotropic media:  
\[
\sigma_{ij} = \lambda \delta_{ij} \epsilon_{kk} + 2\mu \epsilon_{ij}
\]  
with Lamé parameters \(\lambda\) and \(\mu\), and strain \(\epsilon_{ij}\). Seismic reflection profiling uses controlled sources (e.g., Vibroseis trucks, dynamite) and arrays of geophones to record reflected P- and S-waves. Key processing steps include velocity analysis, normal moveout correction, and migration (Kirchhoff or reverse-time). The Zoeppritz equations govern reflection/transmission coefficients at interfaces, essential for amplitude versus offset (AVO) analysis to discriminate lithology and fluid content.

## Gravity And Magnetics

Gravity methods measure variations in Earth’s gravitational acceleration \(g\) to infer density contrasts. The governing equation is Newton’s law:  
\[
\mathbf{g}(\mathbf{r}) = G \int_V \frac{\rho(\mathbf{r}')(\mathbf{r}' - \mathbf{r})}{|\mathbf{r}' - \mathbf{r}|^3} dV'
\]  
where \(G = 6.674 \times 10^{-11} \, \mathrm{m^3 kg^{-1} s^{-2}}\). Bouguer and free-air corrections standardize measurements. Gravity inversion uses forward modeling of prisms or tesseroids to estimate subsurface density distributions. Magnetic methods exploit variations in the Earth’s magnetic field \(\mathbf{B}\), modeled by the potential field equation \(\nabla^2 V = 0\), with \(\mathbf{B} = -\nabla V\). Magnetization vectors \(\mathbf{M}\) induce anomalies measured by magnetometers, analyzed via reduction to the pole and Euler deconvolution to locate sources.

## Electrical And Electromagnetic Methods

Electrical resistivity tomography (ERT) and controlled-source electromagnetic (CSEM) methods probe subsurface conductivity \(\sigma\). The governing equation for steady-state current flow is:  
\[
\nabla \cdot (\sigma \nabla V) = 0
\]  
where \(V\) is electric potential. In ERT, four-electrode arrays (Wenner, Schlumberger configurations) inject current and measure potential differences to invert for 2D/3D resistivity models. CSEM employs time-varying electromagnetic fields governed by Maxwell’s equations:  
\[
\nabla \times \mathbf{E} = -\frac{\partial \mathbf{B}}{\partial t}, \quad \nabla \times \mathbf{H} = \mathbf{J} + \frac{\partial \mathbf{D}}{\partial t}
\]  
where \(\mathbf{E}\), \(\mathbf{H}\), \(\mathbf{B}\), \(\mathbf{D}\), and \(\mathbf{J}\) denote electric field, magnetic field intensity, magnetic flux density, electric displacement, and current density respectively. Skin depth \(\delta = \sqrt{2/(\mu \sigma \omega)}\) quantifies penetration depth at angular frequency \(\omega\).

## Geothermal And Heat Flow Analysis

Heat transport in the Earth is modeled by Fourier’s law and the heat equation:  
\[
\frac{\partial T}{\partial t} = \kappa \nabla^2 T + \frac{H}{\rho c_p}
\]  
where \(T\) is temperature, \(\kappa\) thermal diffusivity, \(H\) volumetric heat production, \(\rho\) density, and \(c_p\) specific heat capacity. Surface heat flow \(q = -k \frac{\partial T}{\partial z}\) (with thermal conductivity \(k\)) is measured by heat flow probes. Geothermal gradient and radiogenic heat production constrain mantle convection and lithospheric thickness. Thermal modeling integrates conductive and advective components, with applications in basin modeling and geothermal resource assessment.

## Inverse Problem And Modeling Techniques

Geophysical inversion seeks model parameters \(\mathbf{m}\) minimizing the misfit function:  
\[
\Phi(\mathbf{m}) = \|\mathbf{d}_{obs} - \mathbf{G}(\mathbf{m})\|^2 + \alpha \|\mathbf{L}(\mathbf{m} - \mathbf{m}_0)\|^2
\]  
where \(\mathbf{d}_{obs}\) are observations, \(\mathbf{G}\) the forward operator, \(\alpha\) regularization parameter, \(\mathbf{L}\) a smoothing operator, and \(\mathbf{m}_0\) prior model. Techniques include linearized least squares, Tikhonov regularization, and Bayesian inference. Nonlinear problems utilize iterative solvers (Gauss-Newton, conjugate gradient) and global optimization (genetic algorithms, simulated annealing). Resolution and uncertainty quantification are critical, often assessed via model covariance matrices or Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) sampling.

## Mechanisms

Geophysics involves the application of physical principles to study the Earth's internal and external processes. The mechanisms underlying geophysical phenomena can be understood by examining the causal chain of events. Seismic activity, for instance, is initiated when tectonic forces build up stress in the Earth's crust, causing it to deform. As the stress accumulates, it eventually overcomes the strength of the rocks, resulting in faulting and the release of seismic waves. These waves propagate through the Earth's interior, interacting with different rock layers and causing them to vibrate. The vibrations are then detected by seismometers, which record the wave's amplitude, frequency, and velocity. By analyzing these seismic data, geophysicists can infer the Earth's internal structure and composition. Similarly, the Earth's magnetic field is generated by the motion of molten iron in the outer core, which induces electric currents and, in turn, produces the magnetic field. This field is then measured by magnetometers, allowing geophysicists to study the Earth's core dynamics and the geodynamo process. Gravity measurements, on the other hand, are used to investigate the Earth's mass distribution and internal density structure. By combining these different geophysical datasets, researchers can develop a comprehensive understanding of the Earth's internal and external mechanisms, including plate tectonics, mantle convection, and the Earth's thermal evolution.

By understanding these mechanisms, geophysicists can interpret data and make inferences about the Earth's composition, structure, and dynamic processes.

## Methods And Frameworks

In geophysics, several methods and frameworks are employed to understand the Earth's internal and external processes. The refraction method is used to determine the velocity of seismic waves in different layers of the Earth, which is essential for understanding the Earth's internal structure. This method is particularly useful for studying the crust-mantle boundary and the velocity structure of the Earth's crust. However, its failure mode lies in its assumption of a simple, layered Earth model, which may not always be accurate.

The reflection method, on the other hand, is used to image the subsurface structure of the Earth, particularly in the context of hydrocarbon exploration. This method involves measuring the reflection of seismic waves from different interfaces, which can provide detailed information about the subsurface geology. However, its failure mode lies in its sensitivity to noise and the requirement for a high signal-to-noise ratio.

The gravity method is used to study the density structure of the Earth, which can provide information about the subsurface geology and the distribution of different rock types. This method involves measuring the gravitational field of the Earth, which can be used to infer the density of the underlying rocks. However, its failure mode lies in its non-uniqueness, as different density models can produce the same gravitational field.

The magnetic method is used to study the magnetic properties of rocks, which can provide information about the subsurface geology and the tectonic history of an area. This method involves measuring the magnetic field of the Earth, which can be used to infer the presence of magnetic minerals and the structure of the underlying rocks. However, its failure mode lies in its sensitivity to cultural noise and the requirement for a detailed understanding of the magnetic properties of the rocks.

The Fourier transform is a mathematical framework used to analyze seismic data in the frequency domain, which can provide information about the frequency content of the data and the underlying geological structure. The Rayleigh wave dispersion equation is a formula used to study the velocity structure of the Earth's crust and upper mantle, which can provide information about the elastic properties of the rocks and the underlying geological structure.

Geophysics employs various methods and frameworks to investigate the Earth's internal and external structures. Gravity and magnetic methods are used to investigate density and magnetic property variations, respectively. Electrical resistivity tomography (ERT) is used to image subsurface resistivity structures, often in environmental and hydrogeological studies. 
The ray theory and wave equation are fundamental frameworks, with the former assuming straight-line propagation and the latter accounting for wave behavior. The diffraction stack method is used for seismic data processing, enhancing signal-to-noise ratios. 
Failure modes include seismic method limitations in areas with complex or anisotropic structures, gravity method limitations in areas with subtle density variations, and ERT limitations in areas with high electrical conductivity. 
Formulas such as Snell's law and the eikonal equation are essential for seismic wave propagation, while Poisson's equation describes gravity and magnetic fields. 
When to use each method depends on the research question, data availability, and environmental conditions. Understanding the principles and limitations of each method is crucial for selecting the appropriate technique and interpreting results accurately.

## Worked Examples

To illustrate the application of geophysical principles, consider the following examples. 
1. Seismic Wave Velocity: A seismic wave travels from the surface to a depth of 5 km and back, taking 2 seconds. If the wave's velocity is 2500 m/s, what is the time it takes to travel from the surface to the depth? 
Given: total distance = 10 km = 10,000 m, total time = 2 s, velocity = 2500 m/s. 
Using the formula: time = distance / velocity, we find the one-way time is half of the total time, so 1 s. 
2. Gravity Anomaly: The gravitational acceleration at a point on the Earth's surface is 9.8 m/s^2. If a mass anomaly of density 3000 kg/m^3 is buried 1 km below the surface, and the surrounding rock has a density of 2500 kg/m^2, what is the gravity anomaly? 
Given: density contrast = 3000 - 2500 = 500 kg/m^3, depth = 1000 m. 
Using the formula for gravity anomaly due to a buried sphere: Δg = (4/3) * π * G * ρ * (r^3) / (r^2 + d^2)^(3/2), where G = 6.67 * 10^-11 N*m^2/kg^2, we can calculate the anomaly. 
However, for a simple approximation, we use Δg = 2 * π * G * ρ * d, yielding Δg ≈ 0.02 m/s^2. 
3. Magnetic Field: The Earth's magnetic field at a point is 50,000 nT, and a buried ferromagnetic ore body causes a magnetic anomaly of 1000 nT. If the ore body is 500 m below the surface, what is its approximate volume? 
Given: magnetic moment = (anomaly / background) * (distance^3), we can estimate the volume. 
Using the formula for magnetic moment of a buried sphere: M = (4/3) * π * (χ - 1) * H * r^3, where χ is the magnetic susceptibility, and assuming χ ≈ 10 for ferromagnetic materials, we can estimate the volume of the ore body. 
However, without specific values for χ and the background field's variation with depth, we can only make a rough estimate, V ≈ (anomaly / background) * (distance^3) ≈ 1000 / 50,000 * (500^3) ≈ 2.5 * 10^6 m^3.

To illustrate the application of geophysical principles, consider the following problems. 
1. Seismic Wave Velocity: A seismic wave travels from the surface to a depth of 5 km and back, taking 4 seconds. If the wave's velocity is 2 km/s in the first 2 km and 4 km/s below 2 km, what is the average velocity? 
First, calculate the time taken for the wave to travel the first 2 km: time = distance / velocity = 2 km / 2 km/s = 1 s. 
For the remaining 3 km, time = distance / velocity = 3 km / 4 km/s = 0.75 s. 
The total time for one way is 1 s + 0.75 s = 1.75 s. 
Since the wave travels to the depth and back, the total time is 2 * 1.75 s = 3.5 s. 
However, the total time given is 4 s, indicating the wave's path is not perfectly vertical or the given velocities are not constant. 
2. Gravity Anomaly: The gravitational acceleration at the surface of the Earth is 9.8 m/s^2. If a gravity meter measures an anomaly of -0.1 m/s^2, what is the approximate mass deficiency or excess beneath the surface? 
The gravity anomaly is related to the subsurface mass distribution. 
A negative anomaly indicates a mass deficiency. 
However, calculating the exact mass requires knowledge of the density distribution and the geometry of the subsurface structure, which is not provided. 
3. Magnetic Field: The Earth's magnetic field at a certain location is 40,000 nT, and the angle of inclination is 60 degrees. What is the vertical component of the magnetic field? 
The vertical component (Bv) can be calculated using the formula Bv = B * sin(θ), where B is the total magnetic field and θ is the angle of inclination. 
Bv = 40,000 nT * sin(60 degrees) = 40,000 nT * 0.866 = 34,640 nT. 
This calculation demonstrates how to extract specific components of the Earth's magnetic field for geophysical analysis.

## Applications

Geophysics has numerous practical applications in various fields, including natural resource exploration, environmental monitoring, and hazard assessment. In petroleum exploration, geophysical methods such as seismic reflection and refraction are used to image subsurface structures and identify potential hydrocarbon reservoirs. Electrical resistivity tomography and ground-penetrating radar are employed in mineral exploration to locate ore deposits and map subsurface geology. 
In environmental monitoring, geophysical techniques like electromagnetics and magnetometry are used to detect and map contaminants in soil and groundwater, while seismic methods are applied to monitor subsurface fluid flow and track the movement of contaminants. 
Geophysics also plays a crucial role in assessing natural hazards, such as earthquakes and volcanic eruptions. Seismology, a branch of geophysics, is used to study seismic activity, locate earthquakes, and estimate their magnitude, helping to mitigate the impact of seismic events. 
Additionally, geophysical methods are used in archaeology to locate and map buried sites and artifacts, and in civil engineering to investigate subsurface conditions and design stable foundations for buildings and infrastructure. 
The integration of geophysical data with other disciplines, such as geology and geochemistry, enables a more comprehensive understanding of the Earth's subsurface, facilitating informed decision-making in various fields.

The principles of geophysics are also applied in climate change research, where techniques like paleomagnetism and seismic stratigraphy help reconstruct past environmental conditions and understand the dynamics of the Earth's climate system.

## Common Errors

In geophysics, common errors often arise from incorrect assumptions or oversimplifications of complex phenomena. One mistake is neglecting the anisotropy of geological materials, assuming isotropic properties for ease of calculation. However, many rocks exhibit anisotropic behavior, where physical properties vary depending on direction, leading to inaccurate predictions of seismic wave velocities or electrical resistivity. Another error is ignoring the effects of attenuation on seismic data, which can result in incorrect estimates of subsurface structures and velocities. Additionally, practitioners may incorrectly assume a laterally homogeneous Earth model, failing to account for lateral variations in density, velocity, or other properties, which can lead to misinterpretation of gravitational, magnetic, or seismic data. Incorrectly applying simplifying assumptions, such as neglecting the effects of topography or bathymetry on potential fields or seismic wave propagation, can also introduce significant errors. These mistakes can be avoided by carefully considering the underlying geological complexity and using more sophisticated, nuanced models that account for anisotropy, attenuation, and lateral heterogeneity.

It is essential to consider these potential errors and take steps to mitigate them, such as using multiple data sets, testing different models, and quantifying uncertainty. By being aware of these common errors, geophysicists can improve the accuracy and reliability of their interpretations and models.

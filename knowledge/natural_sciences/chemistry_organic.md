# Chemistry Organic

> The course assumes foundational knowledge of chemistry and builds upon it with core principles of organic chemistry.

## Foundations

Organic chemistry is the branch of chemistry focused on the structure, properties, composition, reactions, and synthesis of carbon-containing compounds, primarily hydrocarbons and their derivatives. Central to organic chemistry is the tetravalency of carbon, enabling diverse bonding patterns including chains, rings, and frameworks with sp³, sp², and sp hybridizations. The foundation rests on understanding covalent bonding, electron delocalization, functional groups, stereochemistry, and reaction mechanisms governed by principles of thermodynamics and kinetics. Key first principles include the octet rule, resonance stabilization, acid-base theory (Brønsted-Lowry and Lewis), and the concept of nucleophiles and electrophiles.

In organic chemistry, a branch of physical sciences, the core definitions and first principles are rooted in the study of carbon-based compounds. A **compound** is a substance formed when two or more different elements are chemically bonded. **Organic compounds** are defined as chemical compounds that contain carbon (C) and hydrogen (H) atoms, often with other elements such as oxygen (O), nitrogen (N), and sulfur (S). The term **organic** in this context refers to the presence of carbon-hydrogen bonds, not to be confused with the term's meaning in other fields. **Molecules** are the smallest units of a compound that retain the properties of the compound, consisting of atoms bonded together through **covalent bonds**, where electrons are shared between atoms. **Functional groups** are specific groups of atoms within a molecule that determine its chemical properties and reactivity. Understanding these foundational concepts, including the definitions of **atoms** (the smallest units of an element), **elements** (substances consisting of only one type of atom), and **chemical bonds** (the attractive and repulsive forces between atoms), is crucial for a practitioner in organic chemistry. The **periodic table** provides a framework for understanding the properties of elements and their potential to form compounds. **Valence electrons** (electrons in the outermost shell of an atom) play a key role in the formation of chemical bonds. Familiarity with these core definitions and principles is essential for the study and practice of organic chemistry in the physical sciences.

In organic chemistry, a subset of physical sciences, the core definitions and first principles are rooted in the study of carbon-based compounds and their properties. A **compound** is defined as a substance formed when two or more different elements are chemically bonded. **Organic compounds** are typically characterized by the presence of carbon-hydrogen (C-H) bonds, and often contain other elements such as oxygen, nitrogen, and sulfur. The **atomic structure** of these compounds is composed of a **nucleus** (containing protons and neutrons) surrounded by **electrons** in specific energy levels or **orbitals**. **Chemical bonds** form when electrons are shared between atoms, with **covalent bonds** being a key characteristic of organic compounds, where electrons are shared in a stable configuration. The **molecular structure** of an organic compound refers to the three-dimensional arrangement of its atoms, which influences its **physical properties** (such as melting point and boiling point) and **chemical properties** (such as reactivity). Understanding these foundational concepts, including the definitions of **functional groups** (specific groups of atoms within a molecule that determine its chemical properties), **isomers** (compounds with the same molecular formula but different structures), and **stereochemistry** (the study of the three-dimensional arrangement of atoms in molecules), is essential for a practitioner in the field of organic chemistry.

## Section

STRUCTURE AND BONDING  
Framework: Valence Bond Theory and Molecular Orbital Theory applied to organic molecules.  
- Carbon hybridization states: sp³ (tetrahedral, 109.5°), sp² (trigonal planar, 120°), sp (linear, 180°).  
- Bond lengths and strengths: C–C single bond ~1.54 Å, 83 kcal/mol; C=C double bond ~1.34 Å, 146 kcal/mol; C≡C triple bond ~1.20 Å, 200 kcal/mol.  
- Resonance: Delocalization of π electrons in conjugated systems (e.g., benzene with equal C–C bond lengths ~1.39 Å). Use resonance structures to predict reactivity and stability.  
- Inductive and mesomeric effects: Electron-withdrawing/donating groups influence reactivity via σ and π systems.

FUNCTIONAL GROUPS AND NOMENCLATURE  
Framework: IUPAC systematic naming rules and functional group hierarchy.  
- Priority order: Carboxylic acids > anhydrides > esters > acid halides > amides > nitriles > aldehydes > ketones > alcohols > amines > alkenes > alkynes > alkanes.  
- Key functional groups: Alcohols (-OH), Aldehydes (-CHO), Ketones (RCOR’), Carboxylic acids (-COOH), Amines (-NH₂), Ethers (ROR’), Halides (R–X).  
- Naming steps: Identify longest carbon chain, assign locants to highest priority groups, use prefixes and suffixes accordingly, apply stereochemical descriptors (R/S, E/Z).  
- Example: (2R,3S)-3-bromo-2-methylpentanoic acid.

STEREOCHEMISTRY  
Framework: Chirality, stereoisomerism, and conformational analysis.  
- Chirality centers: Tetrahedral carbons with four different substituents; assigned R/S configuration using Cahn-Ingold-Prelog priority rules.  
- Enantiomers: Non-superimposable mirror images; differ in optical rotation (measured in degrees, e.g., +25°).  
- Diastereomers: Stereoisomers not related as mirror images.  
- Conformations: Newman projections to analyze staggered (anti, gauche) vs eclipsed conformers; energy differences ~3 kcal/mol between anti and gauche in butane.  
- E/Z nomenclature for alkenes based on substituent priority.

REACTION MECHANISMS  
Framework: Electron-pushing formalism and classification of mechanisms.  
- Nucleophilic substitution: SN1 (unimolecular, carbocation intermediate, rate = k[substrate]) vs SN2 (bimolecular, backside attack, inversion of configuration, rate = k[substrate][nucleophile]).  
- Elimination: E1 (carbocation intermediate) vs E2 (concerted, anti-periplanar proton abstraction).  
- Addition reactions: Electrophilic addition to alkenes (e.g., HX addition with Markovnikov’s rule), radical addition (e.g., HBr with peroxides).  
- Rearrangements: Hydride and alkyl shifts to stabilize carbocations.  
- Use curved arrow notation to track electron flow.

SPECTROSCOPIC METHODS  
Framework: Structure elucidation using NMR, IR, MS, UV-Vis.  
- ¹H NMR: Chemical shifts (δ) 0–12 ppm, multiplicity (singlet, doublet, triplet), coupling constants (J in Hz), integration corresponds to proton count.  
- ¹³C NMR: Chemical shifts 0–220 ppm, DEPT experiments distinguish CH₃, CH₂, CH, and quaternary carbons.  
- IR spectroscopy: Functional group identification by characteristic absorptions (e.g., C=O stretch ~1700 cm⁻¹, O–H broad stretch 3200–3600 cm⁻¹).  
- Mass spectrometry: Molecular ion peak (M⁺), fragmentation patterns for structural clues.  
- UV-Vis: Conjugated π systems absorb UV light; λ_max shifts with conjugation length.

ORGANIC SYNTHESIS STRATEGIES  
Framework: Retrosynthetic analysis and functional group interconversions (FGI).  
- Disconnection approach: Identify strategic bonds to break, generate synthons, and plan synthetic routes backward from target molecule.  
- Protecting groups: E.g., TBDMS for alcohols, Boc for amines; installed and removed under specific conditions to avoid side reactions.  
- Key reactions:  
  - Grignard addition (RMgX + carbonyl → alcohol),  
  - Wittig reaction (Ph₃P=CHR + aldehyde/ketone → alkene),  
  - Suzuki coupling (aryl boronic acid + aryl halide, Pd catalyst),  
  - Diels-Alder cycloaddition (diene + dienophile → cyclohexene).  
- Stereoselective synthesis: Use chiral auxiliaries or catalysts (e.g., Sharpless epoxidation).

ORGANIC REACTION KINETICS AND THERMODYNAMICS  
Framework: Rate laws, activation energy, equilibrium constants.  
- Arrhenius equation: k = A e^(–Ea/RT), relate temperature to rate constant.  
- Hammond postulate: Transition state resembles structure of species (reactants or products) closer in energy.  
- Thermodynamics: ΔG = ΔH – TΔS; reactions spontaneous if ΔG < 0.  
- Curtin-Hammett principle: Product distribution controlled by difference in transition state energies, not ground state conformers.  
- Use Eyring equation for activation parameters from temperature-dependent rate data.

## Mechanisms

In organic chemistry, a mechanism refers to the step-by-step description of how a chemical reaction occurs, including the formation and breaking of bonds, and the movement of electrons. The mechanism of a reaction is often represented by a series of curved arrows that indicate the flow of electrons. Understanding the mechanism of a reaction is crucial in predicting the outcome of a reaction and identifying the factors that influence its rate and selectivity. A typical organic reaction mechanism involves several key steps: initiation, propagation, and termination. Initiation involves the formation of a reactive intermediate, such as a free radical or a carbocation, which then reacts with other molecules to form products. Propagation involves the transfer of electrons or atoms from one molecule to another, resulting in the formation of new bonds. Termination occurs when the reactive intermediate is neutralized, resulting in the formation of the final product. The causal chain of a reaction mechanism can be influenced by various factors, including the presence of catalysts, the concentration of reactants, and the reaction conditions, such as temperature and pressure. By analyzing the mechanism of a reaction, chemists can identify the rate-determining step, which is the slowest step in the reaction sequence, and develop strategies to optimize the reaction conditions and improve the yield and selectivity of the desired product. A key principle in understanding mechanisms is the concept of electron pushing and pulling, where electrons are moved from one atom to another through the formation of new bonds or the breaking of existing ones. The causal chain in a mechanism can be broken down into several key steps: initiation, where the reactants are converted into a reactive intermediate; propagation, where the intermediate reacts with other molecules to form new intermediates; and termination, where the final product is formed. The curved arrow notation is used to represent the movement of electrons, with the arrowhead indicating the direction of electron flow.

## Methods And Frameworks

In organic chemistry, several methods and frameworks are employed to understand and predict the behavior of molecules. The Huckel method is used to calculate the molecular orbitals of planar, conjugated systems, providing insight into the delocalization of electrons. It is applicable when the molecule has a planar, ring-like structure, but fails when the system is non-planar or has a large number of atoms. The Woodward-Hoffmann rules are a framework for predicting the stereochemistry of pericyclic reactions, such as cycloadditions and sigmatropic rearrangements. These rules are used when the reaction involves a concerted, single-step mechanism, but are limited by their inability to account for non-concerted or stepwise processes. The Hammond-Leffler postulate is a model for understanding the transition state of a reaction, relating the structure of the transition state to the stability of the reactants and products. It is applied when the reaction has a well-defined transition state, but fails when the reaction is complex or has multiple competing pathways. The frontier molecular orbital (FMO) theory is a method for predicting the reactivity of molecules, based on the interaction of the highest occupied molecular orbital (HOMO) and the lowest unoccupied molecular orbital (LUMO). It is used when the reaction involves a simple, two-orbital interaction, but is limited by its oversimplification of the molecular orbitals and neglect of other contributing factors. The PMO (perturbational molecular orbital) theory is an extension of the FMO theory, allowing for the consideration of multiple orbital interactions and providing a more nuanced understanding of molecular reactivity. The Marcus theory is a model for understanding electron transfer reactions, relating the rate of reaction to the free energy change and the reorganization energy. It is applied when the reaction involves a simple, outer-sphere electron transfer, but fails when the reaction is complex or has a large inner-sphere reorganization energy. The Molecular Orbital (MO) theory is used to describe the electronic structure of molecules, providing insight into their reactivity and stability. The MO method is particularly useful for predicting the properties of conjugated systems and the reactivity of molecules towards electrophiles and nucleophiles. However, it can be limited by its reliance on simplifying assumptions, such as neglecting electron correlation.

The Valence Bond (VB) theory, on the other hand, focuses on the localized bonds between atoms, providing a more intuitive understanding of molecular structure and reactivity. The VB method is useful for predicting the stereochemistry of reactions and the stability of radicals, but it can be limited by its difficulty in describing delocalized systems.

The Hard-Soft Acid-Base (HSAB) principle is a framework used to predict the reactivity of molecules towards different types of acids and bases. It is based on the idea that hard acids tend to react with hard bases, while soft acids tend to react with soft bases. The HSAB principle is useful for predicting the outcome of acid-base reactions, but it can be limited by its oversimplification of complex systems.

The Hammond-Leffler postulate is a model used to predict the transition state of a reaction, based on the idea that the transition state will resemble the reactant or product that it is closest in energy to. This postulate is useful for predicting the stereochemistry of reactions and the stability of intermediates, but it can be limited by its reliance on simplifying assumptions about the reaction coordinate.

The Arrhenius equation (k = Ae^(-Ea/RT)) is a formula used to describe the temperature dependence of reaction rates, where k is the rate constant, A is the pre-exponential factor, Ea is the activation energy, R is the gas constant, and T is the temperature. This equation is useful for predicting the effect of temperature on reaction rates, but it can be limited by its assumption of a single, well-defined activation energy.

## Worked Examples

To illustrate key concepts in organic chemistry, consider the following problems. 
1. Calculate the empirical formula of a compound with a molecular weight of 120 g/mol, composed of 60% carbon, 10% hydrogen, and 30% oxygen by mass. 
First, assume a 100g sample, then calculate the number of moles of each element: carbon (60g / 12g/mol = 5 mol), hydrogen (10g / 1g/mol = 10 mol), oxygen (30g / 16g/mol = 1.875 mol). 
Divide each by the smallest number of moles (1.875) to get the simplest whole-number ratio: carbon (5/1.875 = 2.67, round to 2.7 or 8/3), hydrogen (10/1.875 = 5.33, round to 16/3), oxygen (1.875/1.875 = 1). 
Thus, the empirical formula is C8/3H16/3O, which simplifies to C8H16O3 for whole numbers, but the exact empirical formula should be expressed as C2.67H5.33O or simplified further if the exact ratio is an integer multiple of a simpler formula.
2. Determine the degree of unsaturation in a molecule with the molecular formula C5H8. 
The formula for the degree of unsaturation is (2C + 2 + N - H - X)/2, where C is the number of carbon atoms, N is the number of nitrogen atoms, H is the number of hydrogen atoms, and X is the number of halogen atoms. 
For C5H8, the calculation is (2*5 + 2 - 8)/2 = (10 + 2 - 8)/2 = 4/2 = 2. 
This means the molecule has 2 degrees of unsaturation, which could be 2 double bonds, 1 triple bond, or 1 ring and 1 double bond.
3. Calculate the number of possible isomers for butane (C4H10). 
Butane has 4 carbon atoms, which can be arranged in a straight chain (n-butane) or with a methyl group attached to the second carbon (isobutane). 
Considering the general formula for alkanes (CnH2n+2), butane follows this formula and thus is an alkane. 
The possible isomers are n-butane and isobutane, giving 2 possible structural isomers.

To illustrate key concepts in organic chemistry, let's consider the following problems.

1. **Calculating Molar Mass of an Organic Compound**: Find the molar mass of benzene (C6H6). The atomic masses are approximately carbon = 12.01 g/mol and hydrogen = 1.008 g/mol. 
Molar mass of C6H6 = (6 * 12.01) + (6 * 1.008) = 72.06 + 6.048 = 78.108 g/mol.

2. **Determining Empirical Formula from Percent Composition**: A compound is found to be 40.0% carbon, 6.7% hydrogen, and 53.3% oxygen by mass. Assuming a 100g sample, calculate the empirical formula. 
First, convert percentages to masses: carbon = 40g, hydrogen = 6.7g, oxygen = 53.3g. 
Then, convert these masses to moles: carbon = 40 / 12.01 = 3.33 mol, hydrogen = 6.7 / 1.008 = 6.65 mol, oxygen = 53.3 / 16.00 = 3.33 mol. 
Divide each by the smallest number of moles (3.33) to get the simplest whole number ratio: carbon = 1, hydrogen = 2, oxygen = 1. Thus, the empirical formula is CH2O.

3. **Calculating Percentage Yield of an Organic Reaction**: In a synthesis of acetone (C3H6O), 25.0 g of propan-2-ol (C3H8O) yields 15.0 g of acetone. Calculate the percentage yield if the theoretical yield from 25.0 g of propan-2-ol is 20.6 g of acetone. 
Percentage yield = (actual yield / theoretical yield) * 100 = (15.0 / 20.6) * 100 = 72.8%.

## Applications

Organic chemistry has numerous applications in various fields, including pharmaceuticals, materials science, and energy. In pharmaceuticals, organic chemistry is used to design and synthesize new drugs, such as antibiotics and anticancer agents. For example, the synthesis of penicillin, a widely used antibiotic, involves the use of organic chemistry principles to form the beta-lactam ring, a key structural feature of the molecule. In materials science, organic chemistry is used to develop new materials, such as polymers and nanomaterials, with specific properties, like conductivity or optical activity. The production of polyethylene, a common plastic, involves the use of organic chemistry to form the polymer chain through a process known as polymerization. In energy, organic chemistry is used to develop new fuels, such as biofuels, and to improve the efficiency of energy storage devices, like batteries. The synthesis of biodiesel, a renewable energy source, involves the use of organic chemistry to convert vegetable oils into fatty acid methyl esters. Additionally, organic chemistry is used in agriculture to develop new pesticides and fertilizers, and in environmental science to understand and mitigate the effects of pollution. The analysis of organic pollutants, such as polychlorinated biphenyls (PCBs), involves the use of organic chemistry principles to identify and quantify these compounds in the environment. Overall, organic chemistry plays a critical role in the development of new technologies and products that impact various aspects of our daily lives.

## Common Errors

In organic chemistry, practitioners often make mistakes due to a lack of understanding of fundamental principles. One common error is the incorrect assignment of stereochemistry, where the three-dimensional arrangement of atoms in space is misidentified. This can lead to incorrect predictions of chemical reactivity and physical properties. Another mistake is the failure to consider the effects of resonance on molecular stability, resulting in incorrect calculations of thermodynamic properties. Additionally, the misuse of terminology, such as confusing "polar" and "ionic" bonds, can lead to misunderstandings of chemical bonding and reactivity. Furthermore, neglecting to account for the effects of solvent and temperature on reaction rates and mechanisms can result in incorrect predictions of chemical outcomes. These errors often arise from a lack of attention to detail and a failure to apply fundamental principles to complex molecular systems. By recognizing and addressing these common errors, practitioners can improve their understanding and application of organic chemistry principles.

## Advanced

In advanced organic chemistry, researchers delve into complex reactions, novel synthetic methodologies, and the application of physical principles to understand molecular interactions. Graduate-level studies often focus on organometallic chemistry, exploring the role of metals in catalyzing reactions and the development of new catalysts. Asymmetric synthesis, a crucial aspect of modern organic chemistry, involves the design of chiral molecules with specific properties, which is essential in pharmaceutical and materials science applications. Open questions in the field include the development of more efficient and sustainable synthetic routes, the understanding of reaction mechanisms at the molecular level, and the integration of computational methods to predict and optimize reaction outcomes. The field is moving towards the application of machine learning and artificial intelligence to accelerate the discovery of new reactions and materials, as well as the development of novel analytical techniques, such as ultrafast spectroscopy, to probe molecular dynamics and reaction intermediates. Furthermore, the intersection of organic chemistry with other disciplines, such as biology and materials science, is driving innovation in areas like chemical biology, biomimetic synthesis, and the design of functional materials.

In advanced organic chemistry, graduate-level studies delve into complex reaction mechanisms, such as those involving radical intermediates, organometallic compounds, and asymmetric catalysis. The field of physical organic chemistry explores the application of quantum mechanics and statistical mechanics to understand reaction kinetics and thermodynamics. Current research focuses on the development of sustainable and environmentally friendly synthesis methods, including the use of green solvents, catalysis, and flow chemistry. Open questions in the field include the development of more efficient and selective catalysts for complex reactions, such as C-H activation and cross-coupling reactions. The integration of organic chemistry with other fields, such as materials science and biology, is also an active area of research, with applications in the development of new materials, pharmaceuticals, and agrochemicals. Furthermore, the use of computational methods, such as density functional theory (DFT) and molecular dynamics simulations, is becoming increasingly important in the design and optimization of organic synthesis routes. The field is moving towards a more integrated and interdisciplinary approach, with collaborations between experimental and theoretical chemists, biologists, and materials scientists.

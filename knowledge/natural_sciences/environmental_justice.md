# Environmental Justice

> The course assumes foundational knowledge of environmental concepts and focuses on applying principles to real situations and issues.

## Principles

Fair treatment and meaningful involvement of all people regardless of race, income, national origin in environmental decision-making. HISTORY: Warren County PCB landfill (1982), Bullseye Report (1983), Executive Order 12898 (1994). ISSUES: Disproportionate pollution burden, food deserts, water contamination (Flint, MI), climate vulnerability. FRAMEWORK: Distributive justice (equitable allocation), procedural justice (inclusive decision-making), recognition justice (acknowledging diverse experiences). TOOLS: EJScreen (EPA), CalEnviroScreen, cumulative impact assessment. POLICY: Title VI (Civil Rights Act), permit conditions, community benefit agreements. MOVEMENT: Frontline communities, Indigenous rights, green gentrification, just transition.

## Foundations

Environmental justice refers to the fair distribution of environmental benefits and burdens, with a focus on ensuring that no group of people, including racial, ethnic, or socioeconomic groups, bears a disproportionate share of negative environmental impacts. A key principle is **equity**, which involves ensuring that all individuals have equal access to environmental resources and are not disproportionately exposed to environmental hazards. **Sustainability** is the foundation of environmental justice, implying a balance between human needs and environmental protection, to meet the needs of the present without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own needs. **Environmental racism** is a specific form of environmental injustice, where communities of color are disproportionately affected by environmental pollution and degradation. **Vulnerability** refers to the susceptibility of individuals or communities to environmental harm, often due to factors such as poverty, lack of access to resources, or limited political power. **Resilience** is the ability of individuals or communities to withstand and recover from environmental stressors and shocks. **Human rights** are fundamental to environmental justice, as they provide a framework for ensuring that individuals have access to a safe and healthy environment. **Ecological justice** emphasizes the intrinsic value of non-human entities, such as ecosystems and species, and seeks to protect their rights and interests. **Participatory justice** involves the active involvement of affected communities in decision-making processes related to environmental policies and projects. Understanding these core definitions and principles is essential for practitioners working in the field of environmental justice. ## Mechanisms

Environmental justice operates through several mechanisms that link social and environmental factors to unequal distributions of environmental benefits and burdens. The causal chain begins with the identification of environmentally vulnerable populations, often low-income or minority communities, which are disproportionately exposed to environmental hazards such as pollution, toxic waste, and climate change impacts. These populations typically have limited access to resources, including information, political power, and economic opportunities, making them more susceptible to environmental degradation. As a result, they bear a disproportionate share of environmental costs, including health problems, reduced quality of life, and decreased economic opportunities. The lack of representation and participation in environmental decision-making processes further exacerbates these issues, leading to a cycle of environmental injustice. Key mechanisms driving this process include environmental racism, unequal enforcement of environmental regulations, and inadequate consideration of cumulative impacts. The environmental justice framework seeks to address these mechanisms by promoting community-led decision-making, increasing transparency and accountability, and ensuring that environmental policies and practices prioritize the needs and rights of vulnerable populations.

As public pressure builds, governments and regulatory agencies are prompted to take action, which can involve the implementation of policies and regulations aimed at reducing environmental hazards and promoting sustainable development. A crucial mechanism in this process is the principle of free, prior, and informed consent, which requires that affected communities be consulted and agree to any development projects that may impact their environment. Additionally, mechanisms such as environmental impact assessments and strategic environmental assessments are used to evaluate the potential effects of development projects and ensure that they are designed and implemented in a way that minimizes harm to the environment and local communities. The causal chain is further reinforced by the use of legal frameworks, such as human rights law and environmental law, which provide a basis for communities to seek redress for environmental injustices and hold governments and corporations accountable for their actions. Ultimately, the goal of these mechanisms is to promote a more equitable and sustainable distribution of environmental benefits and burdens, and to ensure that all communities have the opportunity to thrive in a healthy and sustainable environment. Next, these concerns are channeled into policy and regulatory frameworks, such as the US Environmental Protection Agency's (EPA) Environmental Justice Program, which provides guidelines for incorporating environmental justice into decision-making processes. The program's core principles, including public participation, fair treatment, and enforcement of environmental laws, are then applied to specific cases, such as the permitting of new industrial facilities or the cleanup of contaminated sites. As a result, regulatory agencies and private companies are held accountable for ensuring that their actions do not disproportionately harm vulnerable populations. Ultimately, the mechanisms of environmental justice aim to achieve equitable distribution of environmental benefits and burdens, and to promote sustainable development that prioritizes both human well-being and environmental protection.

## Methods And Frameworks

In the context of Environmental Justice within Sustainability Environment, several methods and frameworks are employed to analyze and address environmental injustices. The Environmental Justice Screening and Mapping Tool (EJScreen) is used to identify areas with potential environmental justice concerns, by mapping demographic and environmental data. The Cumulative Impacts Assessment (CIA) framework is applied to evaluate the combined effects of multiple environmental stressors on vulnerable populations. The Human Rights-Based Approach (HRBA) framework is utilized to integrate human rights principles into environmental decision-making, ensuring that the rights of marginalized communities are respected and protected. The Precautionary Principle is applied when there is a risk of significant environmental harm, even if the science is not yet definitive, to prevent harm and prioritize caution. The failure mode of these methods often lies in inadequate data, lack of community engagement, and insufficient consideration of cumulative impacts. The Life Cycle Assessment (LCA) method is used to evaluate the environmental impacts of products and systems, from raw material extraction to end-of-life, but may fail to account for social justice implications. The Community-Based Participatory Research (CBPR) approach is employed to engage communities in the research process, but may be limited by power imbalances and inadequate resources. The Human Rights-Based Approach (HRBA) framework is utilized to assess environmental injustices through the lens of human rights, focusing on the rights to life, health, and an adequate standard of living. The Pollution Prevention (P2) framework is employed to reduce environmental pollution at the source, rather than controlling it after release. Each of these methods has its failure mode, such as EJSCREEN's reliance on available data, CIA's complexity in quantifying cumulative impacts, HRBA's potential for vague or unenforceable recommendations, and P2's limitations in addressing existing pollution. Understanding these methods and their limitations is crucial for effective application in Sustainability Environment contexts. The Human Rights-Based Approach (HRBA) framework is utilized to assess environmental policies and projects through a human rights lens, ensuring that they respect, protect, and fulfill human rights. Calculating the Environmental Justice Index (EJI) for a community involves assessing the distribution of environmental benefits and burdens. Example 1: A city has two neighborhoods, one with a high percentage of low-income residents and the other with a high percentage of affluent residents. The city plans to build a new park, which will increase property values and provide recreational space. However, the park will be built in the affluent neighborhood, while the low-income neighborhood will receive a new waste management facility. To calculate the EJI, we assign a score of +10 for the park and -5 for the waste facility. The affluent neighborhood receives a score of +10, while the low-income neighborhood receives a score of -5. The EJI is calculated as the difference between the scores, resulting in an EJI of +15, indicating environmental injustice. 
Example 2: A company plans to build a wind farm in a rural area, which will provide renewable energy and create jobs. Calculating the Environmental Justice Index for a community involves assessing the distribution of environmental benefits and burdens among different populations. Here are three worked examples:

1. **Air Pollution Disparities**: A study finds that a predominantly low-income neighborhood has an average particulate matter (PM2.5) concentration of 15 μg/m³, while a nearby affluent neighborhood has an average concentration of 5 μg/m³. If the acceptable limit is 10 μg/m³, calculate the environmental justice index. 
Index = (Low-income concentration - Acceptable limit) / Acceptable limit = (15 - 10) / 10 = 0.5, indicating a significant disparity.

2. **Toxic Waste Facility Siting**: A proposal to build a hazardous waste facility near a community with 30% minority population and 20% low-income population is being evaluated. If the regional average for minority population is 10% and for low-income population is 15%, calculate the environmental justice index for this proposal.
Index = (Proposed site minority population - Regional average minority population) / Regional average minority population = (30 - 10) / 10 = 2, and for low-income population = (20 - 15) / 15 = 0.33, indicating potential environmental injustice.

3. **Green Space Distribution**: An urban area has 20 parks, with 5 located in low-income neighborhoods and 15 in high-income neighborhoods. If the population of low-income neighborhoods is 40% of the total, calculate the environmental justice index for green space distribution.
Index = (Low-income neighborhood parks / Total parks) / (Low-income population / Total population) = (5 / 20) / (0.4) = 0.625, indicating unequal distribution of environmental benefits.

## Common Errors

In the pursuit of environmental justice within sustainability environments, practitioners often commit mistakes that undermine the effectiveness of their efforts. One common error is the failure to engage with local communities, resulting in top-down approaches that neglect the unique needs and perspectives of affected populations. This can lead to the imposition of solutions that are not tailored to the specific context, ultimately perpetuating environmental injustices. Another mistake is the conflation of environmental justice with environmental conservation, where the focus shifts from addressing the disproportionate impact of environmental degradation on marginalized communities to solely preserving natural resources. This oversight ignores the critical intersection of social and environmental factors that underpin environmental justice. Furthermore, practitioners may overlook the historical and systemic roots of environmental injustices, instead attributing them to individual actions or isolated events. This narrow focus can obscure the need for broader, structural changes that address the underlying drivers of environmental inequality. By recognizing and avoiding these common errors, practitioners can develop more nuanced and effective approaches to promoting environmental justice in sustainability environments.

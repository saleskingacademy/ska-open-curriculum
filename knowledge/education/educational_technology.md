# Educational Technology

> The course covers core concepts, key terminology, and principles of educational technology, assuming some basic knowledge but not specialized expertise.

## Foundations

Educational technology refers to the study and practice of using technology to support and enhance teaching and learning. A foundational concept is **instructional design**, which involves the systematic planning, development, and implementation of instructional materials and activities to achieve specific learning objectives. **Learning objectives** are statements that define what learners are expected to know, understand, or be able to do as a result of instruction. **Pedagogy** refers to the theory and practice of teaching, including the methods, techniques, and strategies used to facilitate learning. **Andragogy**, a related concept, focuses on the teaching and learning of adults, emphasizing self-directed learning and experiential approaches. Key vocabulary includes **synchronous** (real-time) and **asynchronous** (self-paced) learning, as well as **digital literacy**, which encompasses the skills and knowledge required to effectively use technology to access, evaluate, and create information. **Educational software** and **learning management systems** (LMS) are examples of technologies used to support teaching and learning. Understanding these core definitions and principles is essential for effective practice in educational technology.

## Mechanisms

Educational technology operates through a series of interconnected mechanisms that facilitate learning. The process begins with the identification of learning objectives, which inform the selection of appropriate educational technologies. These technologies can include learning management systems (LMS), online platforms, educational software, and multimedia resources. Once selected, these technologies are integrated into the instructional design, which outlines the sequence and organization of learning activities. The instructional design is then implemented, and learners interact with the educational technologies, accessing digital content, participating in online discussions, and completing assignments. As learners engage with the technologies, data is generated, which can be used to assess learning outcomes, inform instruction, and make data-driven decisions. The causal chain is as follows: learning objectives influence the selection of educational technologies, which in turn affect the design of instruction, and ultimately impact learner outcomes. Effective integration of educational technology requires consideration of the technical, pedagogical, and content knowledge (TPCK) framework, which highlights the complex interplay between technological, pedagogical, and content expertise. By understanding these mechanisms, educators can harness the potential of educational technology to enhance teaching and learning.

## Methods And Frameworks

The ADDIE model is a widely used framework for designing educational technology, consisting of Analysis, Design, Development, Implementation, and Evaluation phases. It is suitable for large-scale projects with clear objectives, but may be too rigid for smaller, more flexible initiatives. The Dick and Carey Systems Approach Model is another framework that emphasizes a systematic approach to instructional design, focusing on the learner, learning environment, and learning outcomes. It is effective for designing comprehensive learning systems, but can be overly complex for simple projects. The Keller's ARCS model focuses on motivating learners through Attention, Relevance, Confidence, and Satisfaction, and is useful for designing engaging online courses, but may not address deeper learning needs. The TPACK framework (Technological Pedagogical Content Knowledge) highlights the importance of integrating technical, pedagogical, and content knowledge in educational technology design, and is essential for teachers to effectively integrate technology into their practice, but can be challenging to implement in resource-constrained environments. The SAMR model (Substitution, Augmentation, Modification, Redefinition) provides a framework for evaluating the effectiveness of technology integration, from basic substitution to transformative redefinition, and is useful for assessing the impact of educational technology on teaching and learning, but may not account for contextual factors influencing technology adoption.

## Worked Examples

To illustrate the application of educational technology in real-world scenarios, consider the following examples. 
1. A teacher wants to create a virtual field trip for a class of 30 students to explore a historical site. The cost of software and equipment is $500, and the teacher estimates 10 hours of preparation time, valued at $25 per hour. What is the total cost of the virtual field trip? 
Total cost = cost of software and equipment + (preparation time * hourly rate) 
Total cost = $500 + (10 * $25) 
Total cost = $500 + $250 
Total cost = $750 
2. An educational institution plans to implement a learning management system (LMS) for 500 students, with a subscription fee of $10 per student per year. If the institution also needs to allocate 5% of the total cost for technical support, what is the total annual cost of the LMS? 
Total cost = number of students * subscription fee 
Total cost = 500 * $10 
Total cost = $5000 
Technical support cost = 5% of total cost 
Technical support cost = 0.05 * $5000 
Technical support cost = $250 
Total annual cost = total cost + technical support cost 
Total annual cost = $5000 + $250 
Total annual cost = $5250 
3. A school district wants to provide online professional development for 200 teachers, with a course fee of $50 per teacher. If the district also needs to pay a facilitator $1000 to lead the course, what is the total cost of the professional development program? 
Total cost = number of teachers * course fee + facilitator fee 
Total cost = 200 * $50 + $1000 
Total cost = $10000 + $1000 
Total cost = $11000

If the video editing software costs $10 per hour, and it takes 2 hours to edit the introduction, 3 hours to edit the presentation, and 1 hour to edit the conclusion, what is the total editing cost? 
Total editing cost = (cost per hour * hours to edit introduction) + (cost per hour * hours to edit presentation) + (cost per hour * hours to edit conclusion) = ($10 * 2) + ($10 * 3) + ($10 * 1) = $20 + $30 + $10 = $60.

Let's calculate: time per lesson = 30 minutes (video) + 15 minutes (quiz) = 45 minutes. Time per module = 4 lessons/module * 45 minutes/lesson = 180 minutes. Convert to hours: 180 minutes / 60 = 3 hours.

Let's calculate: total cost of software = 500 licenses * $50/license = $25,000. Amortized cost per year = $25,000 / 5 years = $5,000 per year. Percentage of annual budget = ($5,000 / $10,000) * 100 = 50%.

## Common Errors

Practitioners in educational technology often make mistakes that can hinder the effective integration of technology into the learning environment. One common error is assuming that simply providing students with access to digital tools and resources will automatically lead to improved learning outcomes. This overlooks the need for careful planning, pedagogical consideration, and alignment with learning objectives. Another mistake is focusing too much on the technology itself, rather than on how it can be used to support teaching and learning. This can result in the use of technology for its own sake, rather than as a means to achieve specific educational goals. Additionally, some practitioners may underestimate the importance of digital literacy and the need to provide students with guidance on how to effectively use technology to support their learning. Furthermore, the failure to consider issues of equity and access can lead to the exacerbation of existing inequalities, as some students may not have equal access to technology outside of the classroom. By being aware of these common errors, educators can take steps to avoid them and ensure that technology is used in a way that truly supports the learning process.

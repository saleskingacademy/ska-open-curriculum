# Curriculum Design

> The course assumes foundational knowledge and builds on it with practical applications of curriculum design principles.

## Overview

BACKWARD DESIGN (Wiggins & McTighe): 1. Identify desired results (standards, enduring understandings), 2. Determine assessment evidence (performance tasks, other evidence), 3. Plan learning experiences. BLOOM'S TAXONOMY: Remember (recall) → Understand (explain) → Apply (use) → Analyze (differentiate) → Evaluate (judge) → Create (produce). Revised taxonomy adds knowledge dimensions: factual, conceptual, procedural, metacognitive. LEARNING OBJECTIVES: SMART format. Measurable verbs aligned to Bloom's level. ASSESSMENT: Formative (ongoing feedback: exit tickets, observations, quizzes) vs summative (end evaluation: exams, projects, portfolios). Authentic assessment (real-world tasks). Rubrics (analytic vs holistic). DIFFERENTIATION: Content (what), process (how), product (evidence of learning), environment. Tiered assignments. Scaffolding. Universal Design for Learning (UDL): multiple means of engagement, representation, action/expression. ALIGNMENT: Standards → objectives → instruction → assessment must be aligned. Constructive alignment (Biggs).

## Foundations

Curriculum design refers to the systematic process of planning, developing, and implementing a curriculum, which is a planned sequence of learning experiences intended to achieve specific learning objectives. A **curriculum** is defined as the totality of learning experiences provided to students, including the knowledge, skills, and attitudes they are expected to acquire. **Learning objectives** are specific statements that outline what students are expected to know, understand, or be able to do as a result of their learning experiences. The **curriculum design process** involves several key stages, including **needs analysis**, which identifies the learning needs of students, and **goal setting**, which establishes the overall purpose and direction of the curriculum. **Curriculum frameworks** provide a structured approach to curriculum design, outlining the key components, such as **subjects** or **learning areas**, and the relationships between them. A **subject** refers to a specific area of study, such as mathematics or language arts, while a **learning area** is a broader category that encompasses multiple subjects, such as humanities or sciences. Understanding these core definitions and principles is essential for effective curriculum design, as they provide the foundation for creating a coherent and effective learning program.

## Mechanisms

The curriculum design process involves a series of interconnected steps that ultimately lead to the creation of a cohesive educational program. It begins with needs assessment, where educators identify the learning gaps and requirements of the target student population. This information is then used to define the learning objectives, which outline the specific knowledge, skills, and attitudes that students are expected to acquire. The next step is to select and organize the curriculum content, which involves choosing the most relevant and effective learning materials and structuring them in a logical and sequential manner. 
The chosen content is then translated into specific learning activities and assessments, which are designed to engage students and measure their progress towards achieving the learning objectives. The assessments, in turn, provide feedback that informs future instructional decisions, creating a continuous cycle of improvement. 
Throughout this process, educators must consider various factors, including the educational context, available resources, and the diverse needs of students. By explicitly linking each step to the overall goal of student learning, educators can create a curriculum that is both effective and responsive to the needs of their students. 
Ultimately, the curriculum design process is a deliberate and systematic approach to creating educational programs that promote student learning and achievement.

## Methods And Frameworks

The ADDIE model is a commonly used framework for curriculum design, consisting of Analysis, Design, Development, Implementation, and Evaluation phases. It is suitable for large-scale curriculum projects and helps ensure a systematic approach. However, it can be rigid and may not accommodate rapid changes or iterations. 
The Backward Design approach, developed by Wiggins and McTighe, involves setting learning goals, assessing student understanding, and designing instructional activities. It is effective for creating focused and coherent curricula, but may lead to an overemphasis on assessment. 
The Understanding by Design (UbD) framework, also by Wiggins and McTighe, emphasizes the development of deep understanding and transferable knowledge. It is useful for designing curricula that promote critical thinking and problem-solving, but can be time-consuming to implement. 
The TPACK model (Technological Pedagogical Content Knowledge) highlights the importance of considering the intersection of technology, pedagogy, and content knowledge in curriculum design. It is essential for designing technology-integrated curricula, but may overlook other crucial factors such as student needs and context. 
The Dick and Carey Systems Approach Model provides a comprehensive framework for designing instructional systems, including curriculum, instruction, and assessment. It is suitable for complex, large-scale projects, but can be overly complex and difficult to apply in practice. 
Each of these methods and frameworks has its strengths and limitations, and the choice of which to use depends on the specific curriculum design context, goals, and needs.

To illustrate the application of curriculum design principles, consider the following examples. 
1. A high school mathematics curriculum requires 120 hours of instruction per year. If the school year consists of 40 weeks, and there are 5 hours of mathematics instruction per week, the curriculum can be fully covered. However, if the instruction time is reduced to 4 hours per week, the curriculum will not be fully covered, resulting in a deficit of 20 hours. 
2. A primary school language arts curriculum aims to develop reading comprehension skills in students. The curriculum is designed to allocate 30% of the total instruction time to reading comprehension. If the total instruction time per week is 20 hours, then 6 hours should be dedicated to reading comprehension. 
3. A university science curriculum requires students to complete a minimum of 12 credits in laboratory courses. If each laboratory course is worth 3 credits, and the students have already completed 2 laboratory courses, they still need to complete 6 credits, or 2 more laboratory courses, to meet the curriculum requirement. 
In each of these examples, the curriculum design principles of time allocation, content coverage, and credit requirements are applied to ensure that the educational goals are met.

## Common Errors

Practitioners in curriculum design often make mistakes that can have significant consequences on student learning outcomes. One common error is the failure to align learning objectives with assessment methods, resulting in a mismatch between what is taught and what is tested. This can lead to students being assessed on knowledge or skills they were not explicitly taught, causing confusion and unfair evaluation. Another error is the lack of consideration for diversity and inclusivity, where curriculum designers neglect to incorporate diverse perspectives, examples, and resources, potentially marginalizing certain student groups. Additionally, some practitioners may prioritize content coverage over depth of understanding, leading to a superficial treatment of complex topics and a lack of opportunities for students to develop critical thinking and problem-solving skills. Furthermore, the failure to regularly review and update the curriculum can result in outdated content and pedagogies, rendering the curriculum irrelevant and ineffective in preparing students for an ever-changing world. These errors can be attributed to a lack of understanding of curriculum design principles, inadequate training, or insufficient time and resources to develop a well-designed curriculum.

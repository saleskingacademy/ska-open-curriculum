## Foundations

Administrative law refers to the body of law that governs the administration and regulation of government agencies, their powers, and their interactions with citizens. A **government agency** is an entity established by the government to perform specific functions, such as rulemaking, adjudication, and enforcement. **Rulemaking** is the process by which agencies create regulations, which are rules that have the force of law. **Adjudication** refers to the process of resolving disputes between parties, often through a hearing or other formal procedure. **Enforcement** involves the application of sanctions or penalties to ensure compliance with regulations. 
Key concepts in administrative law include **jurisdiction**, which refers to the authority of an agency to take action, and **delegation**, which is the transfer of power from one entity to another, such as from the legislature to an agency. **Ultra vires** actions are those that exceed an agency's authorized powers. The **administrative process** encompasses the various procedures and activities undertaken by agencies, including investigation, prosecution, and decision-making. Understanding these core definitions and principles is essential for practitioners of administrative law.

## Mechanisms

Administrative law operates through a complex interplay of institutions, procedures, and principles. The causal chain begins with the enactment of legislation by the legislature, which grants power to administrative agencies to implement and enforce the law. These agencies, in turn, create regulations and policies through a process known as rulemaking, which involves notice, comment, and publication in the government gazette or federal register. The regulations are then applied to individual cases through adjudication, where the agency makes decisions regarding the rights and obligations of parties. The decisions of administrative agencies are subject to review by the courts, which ensure that the agency has acted within its jurisdiction, followed proper procedures, and made reasonable decisions. This review process, known as judicial review, involves the application of principles such as ultra vires, natural justice, and proportionality. The courts may quash, affirm, or remit the agency's decision, thereby influencing the outcome of the case. Ultimately, the mechanisms of administrative law aim to ensure that the power of administrative agencies is exercised in a lawful, reasonable, and accountable manner, promoting good governance and protecting individual rights.

## Methods And Frameworks

In administrative law, various methods and frameworks are employed to analyze and review administrative decisions. The Wednesbury unreasonableness test is used to determine if a decision is so unreasonable that no reasonable decision-maker could have made it. This test is applied when a decision appears to be irrational or arbitrary. However, its failure mode lies in its subjective nature, as the threshold for unreasonableness can be difficult to determine. 
The proportionality test, on the other hand, assesses whether a decision is proportionate to the aim it seeks to achieve. This test is used when a decision involves a limitation on individual rights. Its failure mode arises when the decision-maker fails to properly weigh the competing interests. 
The Hard Look doctrine requires a thorough and searching review of administrative decisions, ensuring that they are based on a consideration of the relevant factors. This framework is applied when a decision involves complex technical or policy issues. Its failure mode occurs when the decision-maker fails to provide a sufficient explanation for the decision. 
The Legitimate Expectations doctrine is used to protect individuals' reasonable expectations, such as a expectation of a fair hearing or a expectation of being treated in a certain way. This doctrine is applied when a decision affects an individual's rights or interests. Its failure mode lies in its uncertainty, as it can be difficult to determine what constitutes a legitimate expectation. 
These methods and frameworks provide a structured approach to analyzing administrative decisions, but their effective application requires a nuanced understanding of their strengths and limitations.

## Worked Examples

In administrative law, judicial review is a crucial mechanism for challenging decisions made by public authorities. Here are three worked examples illustrating key principles:

1. **Mandamus**: A local council refuses to issue a building permit to a developer, despite the development plan being approved. The developer seeks a writ of mandamus to compel the council to issue the permit. The court grants the writ, as the council has a public duty to issue the permit and has failed to do so. The reasoning is that the council's decision is a clear breach of its statutory duty, and the developer has a legitimate expectation of the permit being issued.

2. **Procedural Fairness**: A university student is accused of plagiarism and is expelled without being given an opportunity to respond to the allegations. The student seeks judicial review, arguing that the university's decision was unfair. The court agrees, as the university failed to provide the student with natural justice, including the right to be heard. The university's decision is quashed, and the matter is remitted for rehearing.

3. **Ultra Vires**: A government minister makes a decision to revoke a company's license without following the required statutory procedure. The company seeks judicial review, arguing that the minister's decision is ultra vires (beyond their power). The court agrees, as the minister has failed to comply with the statutory requirements, rendering the decision invalid. The court's reasoning is that the minister's decision is a clear breach of the statutory framework, and the company's rights have been affected as a result.

## Common Errors

In administrative law, practitioners often make mistakes when applying the principles of judicial review, particularly in relation to the grounds of review. One common error is failing to distinguish between errors of law and errors of fact. Errors of law are reviewable, whereas errors of fact are generally not, unless they involve a misapplication of the law or a failure to consider a relevant factor. Another mistake is misunderstanding the concept of jurisdictional error, which occurs when a decision-maker acts outside their statutory power or fails to exercise their discretion according to law. Practitioners may also incorrectly assume that a decision is immune from review due to the presence of a privative clause, which can be overridden by the court in certain circumstances. Furthermore, the error of failing to identify the correct standard of review, such as reasonableness or no evidence, can lead to unsuccessful applications for judicial review. Additionally, practitioners may overlook the requirement for procedural fairness, including the duty to provide affected parties with an opportunity to be heard and to have their concerns considered. These mistakes can result in unsuccessful applications, wasted costs, and delayed justice, highlighting the importance of a thorough understanding of administrative law principles.

In administrative law, practitioners often make mistakes that can have significant consequences for their clients. One common error is failing to distinguish between jurisdictional and non-jurisdictional errors of law. Jurisdictional errors occur when a decision-maker lacks the authority to make a decision, whereas non-jurisdictional errors occur when a decision-maker makes an incorrect decision within their jurisdiction. Practitioners must understand this distinction to properly advise their clients on the available grounds for review. Another error is misunderstanding the scope of judicial review, which is limited to reviewing the decision-making process, not the merits of the decision itself. Practitioners must also be aware of the rules regarding standing, which dictate who has the right to bring a claim for judicial review. Additionally, failing to identify and challenge procedural unfairness, such as bias or lack of natural justice, can be a critical mistake. Furthermore, practitioners often overlook the importance of exhausting internal review mechanisms before seeking judicial review, which can be a prerequisite for bringing a claim. These errors can result in unsuccessful claims, wasted costs, and delayed justice, highlighting the need for practitioners to have a thorough understanding of administrative law principles.

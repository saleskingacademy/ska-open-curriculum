## Foundations

Human Rights Law is based on the principle that all individuals are entitled to certain fundamental rights and freedoms, inherent to their dignity as human beings. **Human rights** refer to the universal, inalienable, and indivisible rights to which every person is entitled, as established by international law. The **Universal Declaration of Human Rights (UDHR)**, adopted by the United Nations General Assembly in 1948, sets out the foundational principles of human rights, including the right to life, liberty, and security of person. **International human rights law** is a subset of public international law, comprising treaties, customary international law, and general principles of law, which regulate the relationship between states and individuals. **International covenant** refers to a treaty that sets out specific human rights obligations for states parties, such as the International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights (ICCPR) and the International Covenant on Economic, Social and Cultural Rights (ICESCR). **Jus cogens** norms, or peremptory norms, are fundamental principles of international law that are binding on all states, including the prohibition of torture, genocide, and slavery. A **state party** is a country that has ratified or acceded to a human rights treaty, thereby undertaking to respect, protect, and fulfill the rights enshrined in the treaty. **Jurisdiction** refers to the territorial and personal scope of a state's authority to enforce its laws and ensure compliance with human rights obligations. **Extraterritorial jurisdiction** arises when a state's actions or omissions have an impact on individuals outside its territorial boundaries, triggering human rights obligations. **Human rights obligations** comprise the duties of states to respect, protect, and fulfill human rights, including the duty to refrain from interfering with the enjoyment of rights (**respect**), to prevent violations by third parties (**protect**), and to take positive measures to ensure the realization of rights (**fulfill**).

## Mechanisms

Human Rights Law operates through a complex interplay of international and domestic mechanisms. The process begins with the drafting and adoption of international human rights treaties and conventions by the United Nations General Assembly or other international bodies. These treaties, such as the International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights (ICCPR) and the Convention Against Torture (CAT), establish the substantive rights and obligations that states parties must respect and ensure. Upon ratification, states are bound to implement these treaties domestically, which may involve enacting or amending national laws to conform to the treaty obligations. Domestic implementation is crucial as it enables individuals to claim their rights before national courts. The causal chain is as follows: treaty adoption leads to state ratification, which in turn obliges states to domesticate the treaty provisions, thereby allowing individuals to invoke these rights in national jurisdictions. International monitoring bodies, such as the Human Rights Committee (for ICCPR) and the Committee Against Torture (for CAT), oversee state compliance through periodic reporting and individual complaint mechanisms. These mechanisms provide a pathway for individuals to seek redress for human rights violations, which can lead to recommendations or decisions that states are expected to implement, thereby reinforcing the protection and promotion of human rights.

The principle of universal jurisdiction further enables states to investigate and prosecute human rights violations committed abroad, ensuring that perpetrators are held accountable regardless of where the violation occurred.

## Methods And Frameworks

In Human Rights Law, several methods and frameworks are employed to analyze and address human rights issues. The Normative Framework Approach is used to examine the legal norms and standards that underpin human rights, identifying gaps and inconsistencies in domestic and international law. The Violations Approach is utilized to investigate and document specific human rights abuses, often in crisis situations. The Rights-Holder Approach focuses on the individual or group whose rights have been violated, emphasizing their experiences and perspectives. The Duty-Bearer Approach, in contrast, examines the obligations and responsibilities of states and other actors to respect, protect, and fulfill human rights. The Impact Assessment Framework is used to evaluate the potential human rights consequences of policies, projects, or legislation, helping to identify and mitigate adverse effects. Each of these methods has its strengths and limitations, and may be subject to failure modes such as cultural or contextual insensitivity, inadequate consideration of power dynamics, or overly narrow focus on individual rights to the detriment of collective or group rights.

In Human Rights Law, several methods and frameworks are employed to analyze and apply human rights norms. The Normative Content Approach is used to determine the scope and content of a particular right, by examining the treaty text, preparatory works, and subsequent practice. The Violations Approach is applied when a state's actions or omissions allegedly breach human rights obligations, requiring an examination of the state's duties and the victim's rights. The Proportionality Test is utilized to balance competing rights or interests, assessing whether a limitation on a right is reasonable, necessary, and proportionate to the aim pursued. The Margin of Appreciation doctrine is employed by the European Court of Human Rights, allowing states a degree of flexibility in implementing Convention rights, but risking inconsistent application if overused. The Failure Mode of these methods can occur when they are applied inflexibly or without consideration of the specific context, leading to inconsistent or unjust outcomes.

This doctrine is useful in acknowledging the complexity of human rights issues and the need for nuanced decision-making, but may be criticized for introducing uncertainty and inconsistency in the application of human rights standards.

## Worked Examples

In the case of a state violating the right to life, as per Article 6 of the International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights (ICCPR), a claimant may seek compensation. For instance, if a person is unlawfully killed by state agents, their family may be entitled to $100,000 in compensation, as seen in the European Court of Human Rights' (ECHR) judgment in McCann v. UK.

In a scenario where an individual is subjected to inhuman or degrading treatment, as prohibited by Article 3 of the European Convention on Human Rights (ECHR), they may be awarded damages. For example, if a prisoner is awarded $20,000 in damages for being held in inhuman conditions, as in the ECHR's judgment in Peers v. Greece, this amount may be subject to reduction if the state can prove that the prisoner contributed to their own poor conditions.

In a case involving a breach of the right to a fair trial, as guaranteed by Article 6 of the ECHR, a claimant may be awarded costs and expenses. If a court awards a claimant $50,000 in costs for a violation of their right to a fair trial, as seen in the ECHR's judgment in Barberà, Messegué and Jabardo v. Spain, this amount may be subject to taxation, where the court assesses the reasonableness of the costs incurred.

In the context of Human Rights Law, the European Convention on Human Rights (ECHR) is a cornerstone of protection. Consider the following examples:

1. A prisoner is detained in a cell with insufficient space, violating Article 3 of the ECHR (prohibition of inhuman or degrading treatment). If the cell measures 6 square meters and the prisoner is detained for 23 hours a day, the court may apply the threshold of 3 square meters per person for Category A prisons, as established in the case of Delazarus v. United Kingdom.

2. A journalist is fined €10,000 for publishing an article criticizing a government official, potentially violating Article 10 of the ECHR (freedom of expression). The court would need to balance the right to freedom of expression against the right to reputation, considering factors such as the public interest in the information, the journalist's good faith, and the severity of the fine.

3. A same-sex couple is denied the right to adopt a child, potentially violating Article 14 of the ECHR (prohibition of discrimination) in conjunction with Article 8 (right to respect for private and family life). The court would examine whether the distinction made by the law is justified, considering factors such as the best interests of the child, the couple's ability to provide a stable family environment, and the margin of appreciation afforded to the state in this matter.

In the context of Human Rights Law, the application of principles to concrete cases is crucial. Here are three worked examples:

1. **Article 3 of the European Convention on Human Rights (ECHR) - Prohibition of Torture**: A migrant, detained in a facility, alleges that the conditions are inhumane, with overcrowding, poor sanitation, and inadequate access to medical care. To assess whether Article 3 is breached, we consider the severity of the conditions. If the conditions reach a minimum level of severity, which may be relative but must attain a minimum threshold, and are prolonged, this could amount to inhuman or degrading treatment.

2. **Right to a Fair Trial under Article 6 of the ECHR**: A defendant is tried in a court without access to legal representation due to lack of funds. The court must consider whether the defendant has been afforded a fair trial, including the right to legal assistance. If the lack of representation significantly prejudices the defendant's case, this could constitute a breach of Article 6.

3. **Freedom of Expression under Article 10 of the ECHR**: A journalist is fined for publishing an article critical of the government. To determine if this constitutes a breach of Article 10, we must balance the right to freedom of expression against the need to protect national security or prevent disorder. If the restriction on the journalist's expression is proportionate to the legitimate aim pursued, it may not breach Article 10. However, if the restriction is excessive or not necessary in a democratic society, it could amount to a violation of the journalist's rights.

## Applications

In practice, Human Rights Law is applied through various mechanisms and institutions to protect and promote human rights. The Universal Declaration of Human Rights (UDHR) and international human rights treaties, such as the International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights (ICCPR) and the International Covenant on Economic, Social and Cultural Rights (ICESCR), provide the foundation for human rights law. These instruments are used by national courts, international tribunals, and human rights bodies, such as the United Nations Human Rights Council (UNHRC) and the European Court of Human Rights (ECHR), to interpret and enforce human rights. Domestic courts apply human rights law through judicial review, where they assess the compatibility of national laws and policies with international human rights standards. Additionally, human rights law is applied through administrative mechanisms, such as human rights commissions and ombudsmen, which investigate complaints and provide remedies for human rights violations. The application of human rights law also involves the use of treaty bodies, such as the Human Rights Committee (HRC) and the Committee on Economic, Social and Cultural Rights (CESCR), which monitor states' compliance with international human rights obligations. Furthermore, non-governmental organizations (NGOs) and civil society play a crucial role in promoting and protecting human rights, by advocating for policy changes, providing legal assistance, and raising awareness about human rights issues.

## Common Errors

The application of human rights law is often marred by common errors that practitioners make, stemming from misunderstandings of key principles. One such error is the conflation of absolute and qualified rights. Absolute rights, such as the right to be free from torture, cannot be limited under any circumstances. In contrast, qualified rights, like the right to freedom of expression, may be subject to limitations necessary in a democratic society. Practitioners often mistakenly apply the same level of scrutiny to both types, failing to recognize that qualified rights allow for a balancing of competing interests. Another error is the failure to distinguish between vertical and horizontal application of human rights law. Vertical application refers to the relationship between the state and the individual, whereas horizontal application concerns the relationship between private parties. Misunderstanding this distinction can lead to incorrect assessments of when human rights law is applicable. Furthermore, practitioners may incorrectly assume that human rights law only applies to actions of the state, neglecting the concept of positive obligations, which requires states to take proactive measures to protect human rights in certain circumstances. These errors highlight the importance of a nuanced understanding of human rights law principles to ensure accurate and effective application.

In the application of Human Rights Law, practitioners often make mistakes that can have significant consequences. One common error is the failure to distinguish between absolute and qualified rights. Absolute rights, such as the right to be free from torture, cannot be limited or restricted under any circumstances. In contrast, qualified rights, like the right to freedom of expression, may be subject to limitations and restrictions in certain situations. Practitioners must carefully consider the specific right at issue and the relevant limitations or restrictions that may apply. Another mistake is the incorrect application of the margin of appreciation doctrine, which allows states a degree of flexibility in implementing European Convention on Human Rights (ECHR) provisions. This doctrine should not be used to justify violations of core rights or to undermine the effectiveness of human rights protections. Additionally, practitioners may err by not considering the evolving nature of human rights law, including the development of new rights and the expansion of existing ones. The failure to stay up-to-date with recent case law and jurisprudence can lead to outdated and incorrect legal arguments. Furthermore, the incorrect assumption that human rights law only applies to state actors is a common mistake, as non-state actors, such as corporations, may also be subject to human rights obligations in certain circumstances.

## Advanced

At the graduate level, human rights law delves into complex and nuanced issues, including the intersectionality of rights, the extraterritorial application of human rights treaties, and the relationship between human rights and international humanitarian law. The concept of "margin of appreciation" in the European Convention on Human Rights is a key area of study, as it allows for flexibility in the application of human rights standards across different jurisdictions. Another critical area of exploration is the role of international human rights law in addressing emerging issues such as climate change, artificial intelligence, and biotechnology. The tension between universalism and cultural relativism in human rights discourse is also a subject of advanced study, with scholars examining the challenges of implementing human rights standards in diverse cultural contexts. Furthermore, graduate-level research in human rights law often focuses on the effectiveness of international human rights mechanisms, including the United Nations human rights treaty bodies and the International Criminal Court, and the ways in which these mechanisms can be strengthened to better protect human rights. Ultimately, advanced study in human rights law seeks to equip scholars and practitioners with a deep understanding of the complexities and challenges of human rights implementation, and to foster innovative thinking and problem-solving in this critical area of international law.

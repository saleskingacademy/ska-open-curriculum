# Tort Law

## Negligence

Duty (foreseeable plaintiff, Palsgraf) → breach (reasonable person standard) → causation (actual cause but-for test + proximate cause foreseeability) → damages (compensatory: economic + non-economic + punitive). DEFENSES: Comparative negligence (pure: reduce by fault %; modified: barred if >50%), contributory negligence (any fault bars recovery — minority), assumption of risk. STRICT LIABILITY: Abnormally dangerous activities, wild animals, product liability. PRODUCT LIABILITY: Manufacturing defect (deviation from design), design defect (risk-utility test, consumer expectation test), failure to warn. INTENTIONAL TORTS: Battery (harmful/offensive contact), assault (apprehension of imminent battery), false imprisonment (confinement without consent), IIED (extreme + outrageous conduct → severe emotional distress), trespass (land, chattels), conversion. DEFAMATION: Publication of false statement of fact. Libel (written) vs slander (spoken). Public figures: actual malice (NY Times v. Sullivan — knowledge of falsity or reckless disregard). VICARIOUS LIABILITY: Respondeat superior (employer liable for employee acts in scope of employment).

## Foundations

Tort law is a branch of civil law that deals with **torts**, which are **civil wrongs** committed by one person against another, resulting in harm or injury. A **tortfeasor** is a person who commits a tort. The primary purpose of tort law is to provide **remedies**, such as **damages** or **injunctions**, to victims of torts. **Damages** refer to monetary compensation awarded to a plaintiff for losses suffered as a result of a tort. **Injunctions** are court orders that require a defendant to stop engaging in a particular activity. 
Key concepts in tort law include **duty of care**, which refers to a person's obligation to act with reasonable care to avoid harming others, and **breach of duty**, which occurs when a person fails to meet their duty of care. **Causation** refers to the link between the defendant's breach of duty and the plaintiff's harm or injury. **Remoteness** refers to the requirement that the harm or injury suffered by the plaintiff must be reasonably foreseeable. 
Practitioners of tort law must understand these core definitions and principles, as well as the vocabulary of tort law, including terms such as **negligence**, which refers to a failure to exercise reasonable care, and **intentional torts**, which refer to deliberate acts that cause harm or injury to others. ## Mechanisms

In tort law, the mechanisms by which a claimant can seek redress for a wrong committed by a defendant involve several key steps. First, the claimant must establish that a duty of care was owed to them by the defendant. This duty of care is a legal obligation to ensure the claimant's safety or well-being, and it is typically established by demonstrating a sufficiently close relationship between the parties. Once a duty of care is established, the claimant must show that the defendant breached this duty, either by acting negligently or by failing to act as a reasonable person would in similar circumstances. This breach of duty must then be shown to have caused the claimant harm, which can take the form of physical injury, emotional distress, or financial loss. The causal chain is critical here: the claimant must demonstrate that the defendant's breach of duty was the direct cause of their harm. Finally, the claimant must prove that the harm they suffered was reasonably foreseeable, meaning that a reasonable person would have anticipated the potential for harm arising from the defendant's actions. If all these elements are established, the claimant may be entitled to compensation for their losses, which can include damages for pain and suffering, medical expenses, lost wages, and other related costs. The court's role is to weigh the evidence presented and determine whether the defendant's actions or omissions constituted a tort, and if so, what remedies are available to the claimant.

In tort law, the mechanism of establishing liability involves a step-by-step process where the plaintiff must prove that the defendant's actions or omissions caused them harm. The causal chain begins with the duty of care, which is a legal obligation imposed on the defendant to act with reasonable care towards the plaintiff. The defendant's breach of this duty, either through an act or omission, must be proven to have directly caused the plaintiff's injury or damage. This breach is typically established through the principle of negligence, where the defendant fails to meet the standard of care expected of a reasonable person in similar circumstances. The plaintiff must then demonstrate that the breach of duty was the proximate cause of their injury, meaning that it was a direct and foreseeable consequence of the defendant's actions. Finally, the plaintiff must show that they have suffered damages as a result of the defendant's breach, which can include physical harm, emotional distress, or financial loss. The court will then consider these factors in determining the defendant's liability and the extent of their responsibility for the plaintiff's damages.

## Methods And Frameworks

In tort law, several methods and frameworks are employed to determine liability and assess damages. The Hand Formula, also known as the Learned Hand test, is used to determine negligence by weighing the burden of taking precautions against the likelihood and potential harm of an accident. It is applied when a defendant's actions may have contributed to an injury, and the court must decide whether the defendant was reasonable in their actions. The failure mode of the Hand Formula lies in its subjective nature, as the calculation of burden and likelihood can be influenced by personal biases. 
The Duty-Risk Analysis is another framework used to establish a duty of care, where the court assesses the foreseeability of harm and the relationship between the parties. This method is used when the court needs to determine whether a duty of care existed between the parties. Its failure mode lies in its complexity, as the analysis can become overly complicated and lead to inconsistent results. 
The Thin Skull Rule is a principle used to determine the extent of damages, where the defendant must take the plaintiff as they find them, including any pre-existing vulnerabilities. This principle is applied when the plaintiff has a pre-existing condition that was exacerbated by the defendant's actions. Its failure mode lies in its potential to lead to disproportionate liability, as the defendant may be held liable for damages that are out of proportion to their actions. 
The Loss of Chance Doctrine is a framework used to assess damages in cases where the defendant's actions may have reduced the plaintiff's chances of recovery or survival. This doctrine is applied when the plaintiff can show that the defendant's actions likely caused a loss of chance, even if the exact outcome is uncertain. Its failure mode lies in its difficulty in quantifying the loss of chance, which can lead to inconsistent and arbitrary awards of damages.

## Applications

In practice, Tort Law is applied to provide remedies for individuals who have suffered harm or injury due to the wrongful acts of others. This includes cases of negligence, where a person or organization fails to meet the standard of care expected of them, resulting in harm to another. For example, in a medical malpractice case, a doctor may be held liable for damages if they fail to diagnose a condition or provide substandard treatment, leading to harm to the patient. 
Tort Law is also used to address intentional torts, such as battery, assault, and defamation, where one person intentionally causes harm to another. In cases of product liability, manufacturers and suppliers can be held liable for damages if their products cause harm to consumers due to design or manufacturing defects. 
The application of Tort Law involves the consideration of various factors, including duty of care, breach of duty, causation, and damages. Courts must weigh the evidence and apply legal principles to determine liability and award compensation to victims. 
In addition, Tort Law plays a crucial role in shaping public policy and promoting safety standards, as individuals and organizations are incentivized to take reasonable care to avoid causing harm to others. This, in turn, contributes to the overall well-being of society and the protection of individual rights. This can include cases of personal injury, such as slip and fall accidents, medical malpractice, or product liability. Tort law is also used to address cases of intentional torts, such as battery, assault, or defamation, where one person intentionally causes harm to another. Additionally, tort law is applied in cases of nuisance, where a person's use of their property interferes with another's enjoyment of their property. Courts consider factors such as duty of care, breach of duty, causation, and damages when determining liability and awarding compensation in tort cases.

## Common Errors

In tort law, practitioners often make mistakes that can significantly impact the outcome of cases. One common error is failing to properly plead the duty of care, which is a fundamental element of a tort claim. This can occur when the pleadings do not adequately specify the scope of the duty or the standard of care required. Another mistake is confusing the concepts of proximate cause and remoteness, leading to incorrect assessments of damages. Additionally, practitioners may incorrectly apply the principles of vicarious liability, such as failing to recognize the distinction between an employee's authorized and unauthorized acts. Furthermore, errors can arise in the application of defences, including the misuse of the defence of contributory negligence or the failure to properly raise the defence of voluntary assumption of risk. These mistakes can be attributed to a lack of understanding of the underlying principles of tort law, inadequate preparation, or insufficient attention to detail. It is essential for practitioners to be aware of these potential pitfalls and to carefully consider the specific facts and circumstances of each case to avoid making these common errors. One common error is failing to distinguish between intentional torts and negligence. Intentional torts, such as battery and assault, require proof of intent to harm, whereas negligence requires proof of a breach of duty of care. Another error is misunderstanding the concept of duty of care, which is a critical element in negligence claims. Practitioners must carefully consider the relationship between the parties, the foreseeability of harm, and the proximity of the parties to establish a duty of care. Additionally, some practitioners mistakenly believe that the "but-for" test is the sole test for causation, when in fact, it is often used in conjunction with the "material contribution" test. Furthermore, errors in pleading and particularizing claims can lead to striking out of claims or significant delays in proceedings.

At the graduate level, students delve into advanced topics such as the intersection of tort law with human rights, particularly in cases involving violations of the right to life, liberty, and security of person. The concept of vicarious liability is also explored in depth, including its application in complex scenarios like corporate liability for employee actions. Furthermore, the issue of causation and remoteness of damage remains a contentious area, with courts continuing to refine the tests for determining whether a defendant's actions were the proximate cause of the plaintiff's harm. Another area of ongoing development is the law of nuisance, particularly in the context of environmental pollution and climate change. The role of tort law in addressing systemic injustices, such as discrimination and inequality, is also a subject of academic inquiry. Additionally, the impact of technological advancements, like artificial intelligence and autonomous vehicles, on tort liability is being explored, raising questions about the potential for new forms of liability and the need for legislative reform. Ultimately, the field of tort law is moving towards a more nuanced understanding of the complex relationships between individuals, corporations, and the state, and the ways in which the law can be used to promote justice and accountability.

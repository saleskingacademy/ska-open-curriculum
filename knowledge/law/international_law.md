## Mechanisms

International law operates through a complex interplay of mechanisms that facilitate its creation, implementation, and enforcement. The process begins with the sources of international law, which include treaties, customary international law, general principles of law, and judicial decisions. Treaties are created through a process of negotiation, adoption, and ratification by states, and they can be either bilateral or multilateral. Customary international law emerges from the consistent practice of states, coupled with a sense of legal obligation. Once established, these sources of law give rise to rules and norms that are binding on states. The implementation of international law is facilitated by international organizations, such as the United Nations, which provide a framework for cooperation and dispute resolution. States are also obligated to implement international law through their domestic legal systems, which may involve the incorporation of international treaties and norms into national law. Enforcement mechanisms include diplomatic pressure, economic sanctions, and, in some cases, judicial proceedings before international courts and tribunals, such as the International Court of Justice. The causal chain is as follows: sources of law give rise to rules and norms, which are then implemented by states and international organizations, and ultimately enforced through various mechanisms. This process is underpinned by the principles of sovereignty, consent, and good faith, which are fundamental to the operation of international law.

Each of these methods and frameworks has its own failure mode, such as the risk of subjective interpretation under the Vienna Convention Rules or the potential for abuse of the precautionary principle. Effective application of these methods and frameworks requires a nuanced understanding of their strengths, limitations, and potential pitfalls.

The excess amount of $500,000 would be dealt with under the specific laws of South Africa as the incident occurred within its territorial waters, considering the IMO conventions provide a framework but the implementation and liability beyond the set limits often fall under the jurisdiction of the state where the incident occurred.

The investor could seek compensation through the International Centre for Settlement of Investment Disputes (ICSID), which provides a mechanism for resolving investment disputes between states and foreign investors.

## Applications

International law has numerous practical applications in various domains. In dispute resolution, it is used in international courts and tribunals, such as the International Court of Justice (ICJ) and the International Criminal Court (ICC), to settle disputes between states and prosecute individuals for international crimes. International law also guides diplomatic relations and state interactions, as seen in the Vienna Convention on Diplomatic Relations, which outlines the rules for diplomatic missions and immunities. Furthermore, it informs international trade and commerce, with the World Trade Organization (WTO) relying on international law to regulate trade agreements and resolve trade disputes. Additionally, international law plays a crucial role in human rights protection, with treaties like the International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights (ICCPR) and the Convention against Torture (CAT) setting standards for states to respect and protect human rights. In environmental law, international agreements such as the Paris Agreement and the Convention on International Trade in Endangered Species of Wild Fauna and Flora (CITES) aim to address global environmental challenges. International law also applies to armed conflicts, with the Geneva Conventions and their Additional Protocols establishing rules for the conduct of war and the protection of civilians and prisoners of war. These examples demonstrate the significance of international law in shaping state behavior and promoting cooperation and stability in the international community.

## Common Errors

Practitioners of international law often make mistakes in applying the principles of jurisdiction, particularly in distinguishing between territorial and extraterritorial jurisdiction. A common error is assuming that a state's jurisdiction is limited to its territorial boundaries, ignoring the concept of extraterritorial jurisdiction which allows states to exercise jurisdiction over acts committed abroad that have effects within their territory. Another mistake is the failure to recognize the difference between jus ad bellum (the law governing the use of force) and jus in bello (the law governing the conduct of hostilities), leading to incorrect application of international humanitarian law. Additionally, practitioners may incorrectly assume that customary international law is only created through the practice of states, neglecting the role of opinio juris (the belief that an action is legally required) in the formation of customary international law. These errors can have significant consequences, including the misapplication of international law and potential violations of state sovereignty. Understanding these common errors is essential for practitioners to accurately apply international law and avoid potential pitfalls.

Another mistake is to conflate the concepts of jus cogens and erga omnes, where jus cogens refers to peremptory norms that are binding on all states, and erga omnes refers to obligations that are owed to the international community as a whole. These mistakes can have significant consequences, including undermining the legitimacy and effectiveness of international law.

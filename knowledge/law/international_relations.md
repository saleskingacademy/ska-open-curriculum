# International Relations

> The course introduces foundational concepts and theories of international relations, assuming no prior study.

## Foundations

International Relations (IR) refers to the study of interactions among sovereign states, international organizations, and non-state actors, examining the complex relationships, conflicts, and cooperation that shape global politics. A practitioner must understand core definitions, including **sovereignty**, which denotes a state's supreme authority within its territory, and **state**, a political entity with a defined territory, population, and government. **International system** refers to the global network of states, institutions, and norms that govern interactions among them. Key concepts include **anarchy**, the absence of a central authority in international relations, and **interdependence**, the mutual dependence of states on each other for economic, political, or security reasons. **Realism**, **liberalism**, and **constructivism** are fundamental theoretical approaches, each offering distinct perspectives on the nature of international relations. **Realism** emphasizes state security, power, and self-interest, while **liberalism** focuses on cooperation, institutions, and the promotion of peace. **Constructivism** highlights the role of ideas, norms, and identity in shaping state behavior. Understanding these foundational concepts and theories is essential for analyzing and explaining international relations.

## Mechanisms

In the context of international relations, mechanisms refer to the processes and structures that facilitate interactions between states, international organizations, and non-state actors. The causal chain of international relations mechanisms can be broken down into several steps. First, states and other actors formulate their interests and preferences through domestic political processes, which involve the interaction of various stakeholders, including governments, bureaucracies, and civil society. These interests are then translated into foreign policy goals, which guide the actions of states in the international arena. Next, states engage in diplomatic interactions, such as negotiations, summits, and conferences, to pursue their interests and achieve their goals. These interactions are often facilitated by international institutions, such as the United Nations, the European Union, and the World Trade Organization, which provide a framework for cooperation and conflict resolution. The outcomes of these interactions can lead to the formation of international agreements, treaties, and regimes, which establish rules and norms governing state behavior. Finally, the implementation and enforcement of these agreements involve a range of mechanisms, including monitoring, verification, and sanctions, which can influence state compliance and shape the behavior of other actors in the international system. Throughout this process, power dynamics, including the distribution of military, economic, and soft power, play a crucial role in shaping the outcomes of international interactions.

The response is influenced by a range of factors, including national interests, international norms, and the distribution of power among states. International institutions, such as the United Nations, play a crucial role in facilitating cooperation and managing conflict by providing a framework for negotiation and the implementation of international law. The outcomes of these interactions can lead to the establishment of international agreements, the resolution of conflicts, or the escalation of tensions. Throughout this process, non-state actors, including NGOs and multinational corporations, can exert influence by lobbying states, participating in international forums, and shaping public opinion. The dynamic and reciprocal nature of these interactions underscores the complexity and unpredictability of international relations, highlighting the need for a nuanced understanding of the mechanisms at play.

## Methods And Frameworks

In the study of International Relations, several methods and frameworks are employed to analyze and understand global interactions. The Realist approach, for instance, emphasizes the role of power and security in shaping state behavior, and is often used to analyze conflicts and alliances. The Liberal approach, on the other hand, focuses on the importance of international institutions, trade, and diplomacy in promoting cooperation. 
The Neorealist model, as outlined by Kenneth Waltz, uses a systemic approach to understand the behavior of states within the international system, and is particularly useful for analyzing the impact of structural changes, such as shifts in the global balance of power. 
The Constructivist framework, by contrast, highlights the role of identity, norms, and ideas in shaping state behavior, and is often used to analyze issues related to human rights, environmental governance, and international law. 
Game Theory, a mathematical approach, is used to model and analyze strategic interactions between states, and is particularly useful for understanding issues related to cooperation, deterrence, and negotiation. 
Each of these methods and frameworks has its own strengths and limitations, and the choice of which to use depends on the specific research question and context. A key failure mode of these approaches is the tendency to oversimplify complex issues, or to prioritize one factor over others.

This theory is useful for analyzing the role of norms and identity in shaping state behavior, but its failure mode lies in underestimating the impact of material factors, such as power and interests. 
The Game Theory model, including the Prisoner's Dilemma, is also used to analyze international interactions, particularly in the context of cooperation and conflict. This model is useful for understanding the strategic interactions between states, but its failure mode lies in assuming that states act rationally and have complete information, which is often not the case. 
Lastly, the Neoliberal Institutionalism framework emphasizes the role of international institutions in promoting cooperation and stability, using formulas such as the concept of absolute and relative gains. This framework is useful for understanding the impact of institutions on state behavior, but its failure mode lies in overestimating the ability of institutions to constrain state behavior and neglecting the role of power and interests.

## Worked Examples

To illustrate key concepts in international relations, consider the following examples. 
1. **Trade Agreement**: Suppose the US and China negotiate a trade agreement, where the US reduces tariffs on Chinese imports by 10% and China increases imports of US goods by 15%. If the initial US tariff revenue is $100 million and initial Chinese imports of US goods are $500 million, calculate the new tariff revenue and Chinese imports. 
New US tariff revenue = $100 million * (1 - 0.10) = $90 million. 
New Chinese imports of US goods = $500 million * (1 + 0.15) = $575 million. 
2. **Foreign Aid**: Assume the UK provides £50 million in foreign aid to a developing country, which has a GDP of £500 million. If the aid is used to increase the country's healthcare expenditure by 20%, calculate the new healthcare expenditure as a percentage of GDP. 
Initial healthcare expenditure = 10% of £500 million = £50 million (assuming). 
New healthcare expenditure = £50 million + (£50 million * 0.20) = £60 million. 
New healthcare expenditure as a percentage of GDP = (£60 million / £500 million) * 100 = 12%. 
3. **Sanctions**: Suppose the EU imposes sanctions on a country, reducing its oil exports by 25%. If the initial oil export revenue is €200 million and the country's total revenue is €1 billion, calculate the new oil export revenue and its percentage of total revenue. 
New oil export revenue = €200 million * (1 - 0.25) = €150 million. 
New oil export revenue as a percentage of total revenue = (€150 million / €1 billion) * 100 = 15%.

## Applications

Diplomats and policymakers utilize realism, liberalism, and constructivism to inform their decisions on matters such as trade agreements, military interventions, and human rights. Non-governmental organizations (NGOs) employ International Relations theories to develop strategies for promoting development, resolving conflicts, and advocating for social justice. Additionally, multinational corporations apply International Relations principles to navigate global markets, manage geopolitical risks, and engage in corporate diplomacy. The United Nations and other international institutions rely on International Relations theories to facilitate cooperation, prevent conflicts, and address global challenges such as climate change, pandemics, and economic inequality. Furthermore, scholars and researchers apply International Relations theories to study and analyze complex issues like terrorism, migration, and cybersecurity, providing valuable insights for policymakers and practitioners. By applying International Relations theories and concepts, individuals and organizations can better understand and navigate the complexities of the global system, ultimately contributing to more effective and sustainable solutions to global problems.

## Common Errors

Practitioners of international relations often make mistakes due to misconceptions about the field's core principles. One common error is the assumption that international relations are solely driven by state actors, neglecting the significant roles of non-state actors such as NGOs, multinational corporations, and international organizations. This oversight can lead to an incomplete understanding of global dynamics and the ways in which various entities interact and influence each other. Another mistake is the reliance on a purely realist approach, which prioritizes state security and power above all else, failing to account for the complexities of international cooperation, global governance, and the impact of economic interdependence. Furthermore, some practitioners may conflate international relations with foreign policy, treating the two as synonymous when, in fact, international relations encompass a broader range of interactions and relationships beyond the policies of individual states. These errors can result from a lack of interdisciplinary understanding, as international relations draw on insights from political science, economics, sociology, and history, among other fields. By recognizing these common errors, scholars and practitioners can work to develop a more nuanced and comprehensive understanding of international relations.

The mistake of relying solely on realist or liberal theories, without considering the insights of other approaches, such as constructivism or feminism, can also lead to incomplete analyses. Furthermore, the error of assuming that international relations can be understood solely through the lens of Western experiences and perspectives, neglecting the diversity of non-Western voices and viewpoints, can result in a lack of understanding of the complexities of global politics. These mistakes can be attributed to a lack of interdisciplinary approaches, insufficient consideration of the complexities of international relations, and a failure to engage with diverse perspectives and theories.

## Foundations

In the context of Immigration Law, core definitions and first principles are crucial for practitioners to understand. **Immigration** refers to the act of entering and settling in a country or region to which one is not native, with the intention of residing there permanently or temporarily. A **migrant** is an individual who moves from one country to another, often in search of better economic, social, or political opportunities. **Emigration** is the act of leaving one's own country to settle in another, while **immigrant** refers to a person who enters and settles in a country where they are not born. 
**Naturalization** is the process by which a non-citizen becomes a citizen of a country, typically through a formal application and examination process. A **national** is a person who owes allegiance to a particular country and is entitled to its protection. **Citizenship**, on the other hand, is the status of being a citizen, with rights and duties as defined by the laws of that country. 
**Asylum** refers to the protection granted to individuals who flee their home country due to persecution or fear of persecution based on their race, religion, nationality, membership in a particular social group, or political opinion. **Refugee** status is granted to individuals who meet specific criteria, including a well-founded fear of persecution. 
Understanding these definitions and principles is essential for practitioners of Immigration Law to navigate the complex legal framework surrounding immigration.

## Mechanisms

Immigration law operates through a complex interplay of administrative, legislative, and judicial mechanisms. The process typically begins with an individual submitting an application for a visa or other immigration benefit, which is then reviewed by the relevant immigration authority. In the United States, for example, this authority is U.S. Citizenship and Immigration Services (USCIS). The application is evaluated based on the applicant's eligibility under the relevant immigration statute, such as the Immigration and Nationality Act (INA). If the application is approved, the individual is granted the requested immigration benefit, such as a visa, green card, or citizenship. If the application is denied, the individual may appeal the decision to an administrative appeals body, such as the Board of Immigration Appeals (BIA). The BIA's decision can then be reviewed by a federal court, which examines the decision for legal errors or abuses of discretion. Throughout this process, the immigration authority must balance the individual's rights and interests against the government's interests in enforcing immigration laws and maintaining national security. The causal chain is as follows: application submission → administrative review → decision → appeal (if necessary) → judicial review (if necessary). This chain is governed by a complex array of statutes, regulations, and case law that shape the immigration law landscape.

Throughout this process, the individual's rights and obligations are governed by statutory law, regulations, and judicial precedents, which provide the framework for immigration authorities to make decisions and for individuals to navigate the system.

The Categorical Approach is used to determine whether a conviction qualifies as an aggravated felony, involving a two-step process: first, examining the statutory definition of the offense, and second, comparing it to the generic definition of the crime. This approach is used when evaluating the immigration consequences of a conviction. 
The Social Group Approach is utilized in asylum cases to identify a particular social group, considering factors such as immutability, visibility, and social visibility. 
The Chevron Framework is applied when interpreting ambiguous statutes, involving a two-step process: first, determining whether Congress has spoken to the issue, and second, evaluating whether the agency's interpretation is reasonable. 
Failure modes include overly broad or narrow interpretations, and failure to consider relevant factors or precedents. 
The Last Arrival/First Departure Rule is used to calculate the period of unlawful presence, considering the date of last arrival and first departure. 
The 90/180-Day Rule is applied to determine eligibility for a visa waiver, examining the duration of stay and purpose of visit.

## Worked Examples

In immigration law, calculating the duration of stay and eligibility for residency or citizenship can be complex. Here are three worked examples:

1. **Temporary Work Visa**: John, a Canadian citizen, enters the United States on a temporary work visa (H-1B) valid for 3 years. He works for 2 years and 6 months before his employer files for an extension. Assuming the extension is approved, how long can John stay in total? 
Under U.S. immigration law, the initial H-1B visa is valid for up to 3 years, with possible extensions up to a total of 6 years. Since John has already worked for 2.5 years, he can extend his stay for an additional 3.5 years, making his total stay 6 years.

2. **Family-Based Green Card**: Maria, a U.S. citizen, petitions for her sister, Ana, who is a citizen of Mexico. Ana's priority date is 2018. Assuming the current wait time for a visa number is 5 years for siblings of U.S. citizens, when can Ana expect to receive her green card? 
The U.S. immigration system operates on a preference system with limited visa numbers available each year. For siblings of U.S. citizens (Fourth Preference), the wait can be several years. If Ana's priority date is 2018 and the current wait time is 5 years, she can expect her visa to be available in 2023, after which she can proceed with her green card application.

3. **Naturalization Eligibility**: David, a green card holder from the United Kingdom, has been a lawful permanent resident for 4 years and 9 months. He has been married to a U.S. citizen for the past 3 years and has been physically present in the United States for at least 18 months out of the past 3 years. Is David eligible for naturalization? 
To be eligible for naturalization, a green card holder must have been a resident for at least 5 years (or 3 years if married to a U.S. citizen) and meet physical presence and continuous residence requirements. Since David has been a resident for almost 5 years and has been married to a U.S. citizen for over 3 years, he will be eligible for naturalization after meeting the 5-year residency requirement or the 3-year requirement as the spouse of a U.S. citizen, considering other eligibility criteria are met.

Calculation: 6 years (required residency) - 5 years (actual residency) = 1 year. Since Emily has a 3-month absence, she will need to reside in the country for 1 year + 3 months = 15 months to meet the residency requirement.

## Applications

In practice, immigration law is applied through various procedures and institutions. The primary application of immigration law is in the processing of visa applications, which involves evaluating an individual's eligibility to enter and remain in a country. This evaluation is based on criteria such as the purpose of the visit, the applicant's country of origin, and their potential impact on the host country's economy and society. Immigration law also governs the process of asylum and refugee claims, where individuals fleeing persecution or violence in their home country can seek protection in another country. Furthermore, immigration law is applied in deportation proceedings, where individuals who have entered or remained in a country unlawfully are removed. The application of immigration law involves a complex interplay between government agencies, such as border control and immigration authorities, and the judiciary, which reviews decisions and ensures that they are in accordance with the law. Additionally, immigration law intersects with other areas of law, including constitutional law, administrative law, and human rights law, which can impact the rights and obligations of individuals and states in the context of immigration.

In practice, immigration law is applied through various procedures and institutions. Governments use immigration laws to regulate the flow of foreign nationals into their territories, balancing national security, economic, and humanitarian concerns. Immigration applications are typically submitted to governmental agencies, such as the U.S. Citizenship and Immigration Services (USCIS) in the United States or the UK Visa and Immigration department in the United Kingdom. These applications may include petitions for family-based immigration, employment-based visas, or asylum and refugee status. The process involves careful scrutiny of applicants' backgrounds, including criminal history checks, medical examinations, and verification of identity and qualifications. Immigration lawyers play a crucial role in guiding applicants through the complex legal requirements and procedures, ensuring compliance with relevant statutes, regulations, and case law. Additionally, immigration laws are enforced by border control agencies, such as customs and border protection services, which are responsible for detecting and preventing illegal immigration, smuggling, and human trafficking. The application of immigration law also involves the use of technology, such as biometric data collection and electronic travel authorization systems, to facilitate the processing and monitoring of immigration applications. Ultimately, the effective application of immigration law requires a nuanced understanding of the complex interplay between national and international laws, policies, and procedures.

## Common Errors

In immigration law, practitioners often make mistakes that can have significant consequences for their clients. One common error is failing to properly document and maintain records of a client's immigration history, leading to difficulties in establishing eligibility for future immigration benefits. Another mistake is misinterpreting the nuances of visa categories, such as confusing the requirements for an H-1B specialty occupation visa with those for an L-1 intracompany transferee visa. Additionally, practitioners may incorrectly advise clients on the implications of criminal convictions on immigration status, neglecting to consider the specific provisions of the Immigration and Nationality Act (INA) regarding grounds of inadmissibility and deportability. Furthermore, errors in preparing and filing immigration petitions and applications, such as omitting required supporting documentation or failing to meet deadlines, can result in delays, denials, or even deportation proceedings. These mistakes often stem from a lack of understanding of the complex and constantly evolving nature of immigration law, highlighting the need for practitioners to stay up-to-date on the latest developments and regulations in the field.

# Video Editing

> The course assumes foundational knowledge and focuses on applying principles to real situations using standard tools like Adobe Premiere Pro.

## Foundations

Video editing is the art and technical process of manipulating and rearranging video shots to create a coherent and compelling narrative or visual experience. At its core, video editing operates on the principles of temporal sequencing, continuity, and visual storytelling, integrating audio, motion, and effects to enhance meaning and emotional impact. The foundational elements include shot selection, timing, pacing, transitions, and synchronization with sound. Editing is both a creative and technical craft, requiring mastery of software tools (e.g., Adobe Premiere Pro, Avid Media Composer, DaVinci Resolve) and a deep understanding of cinematic grammar, montage theory, and perceptual psychology.

In the arts subject of Video Editing, core definitions and first principles are crucial for practitioners to understand. **Video Editing** refers to the process of selecting, arranging, and modifying video clips to create a cohesive narrative or visual flow. A **video clip** is a segment of video footage, defined as a sequence of images captured at a rate of 24 or 25 frames per second, depending on the region and format. **Footage** is the raw, unedited video material, which can be sourced from various devices, including cameras, smartphones, or archival recordings. 
The **editor** is the practitioner responsible for shaping the narrative, pacing, and tone of the video through the editing process. **Editing software**, such as Adobe Premiere Pro or Avid Media Composer, provides the technical tools for editors to manipulate and arrange video clips. Key vocabulary includes **cut**, which refers to the transition between two consecutive clips, and **sequence**, which denotes a series of clips arranged in a specific order. **Timeline** is the visual representation of the video's chronological structure, allowing editors to organize and navigate the sequence of clips. Understanding these foundational concepts and terms is essential for effective video editing in the arts. **Frames** are individual still images that, when played in rapid succession, create the illusion of motion. The **frame rate** measures the number of frames displayed per second, influencing the smoothness and realism of the video. **Resolution** refers to the number of pixels (tiny units of digital information) that make up each frame, determining the video's clarity and detail. **Pixels** are the basic building blocks of digital images, arranged in a grid to form the video picture. **Aspect ratio** defines the proportional relationship between the video image's width and height, commonly expressed as 16:9 or 4:3. Understanding these fundamental concepts and vocabulary is crucial for effective communication and collaboration among video editing practitioners.

## Section 1

THE 180-DEGREE RULE & CONTINUITY EDITING  
Framework: The 180-degree rule maintains spatial orientation by keeping the camera on one side of an imaginary axis between two subjects, preserving screen direction and viewer comprehension. Breaking this rule risks disorienting the audience. Continuity editing uses match cuts, eyeline matches, and the 30-degree rule (camera angle changes by at least 30° between cuts to avoid jump cuts) to create seamless narrative flow.  
Steps:  
1. Establish the axis of action.  
2. Position cameras within the 180° arc.  
3. Use cutaways and reaction shots to maintain continuity.  
4. Apply the 30-degree rule to ensure visual variety without jarring jumps.  
5. Match eyelines and action to preserve spatial logic.

## Section 2

RHYTHM & PACE FORMULA  
Framework: Editing pace is governed by shot length (SL) and rhythm, which modulate viewer engagement and emotional tone. The average shot length (ASL) varies by genre: ~2-5 seconds for action, ~6-10 seconds for drama, ~12+ seconds for contemplative scenes. Rhythm can be mathematically modeled as SL_n = SL_(n-1) ± Δ, where Δ controls acceleration or deceleration of pace.  
Method:  
- Define target ASL based on genre and scene intensity.  
- Use incremental changes in SL to build tension or relaxation.  
- Employ montage sequences with rapidly decreasing SL for climax (e.g., Hitchcock’s Psycho shower scene averages ~2 seconds per shot).  
- Balance shot duration with audio cues and narrative beats.

## Section 3

COLOR GRADING & LUMINANCE CURVE ADJUSTMENT  
Framework: Color grading manipulates luminance and chrominance to establish mood, continuity, and visual style. The primary tools include Lift (shadows), Gamma (midtones), and Gain (highlights) adjustments, often represented as curves or vectors in YUV or RGB color space.  
Steps:  
1. Normalize footage using waveform monitors and vectorscopes to match exposure and white balance.  
2. Apply Lift adjustments to control shadow depth (e.g., reduce Lift by -10% to deepen blacks).  
3. Adjust Gamma to fine-tune midtones for skin tone accuracy (targeting ~70 IRE on waveform).  
4. Modify Gain to brighten highlights without clipping (keep below 100 IRE).  
5. Use LUTs (Look-Up Tables) for stylized grading, ensuring proper calibration to Rec.709 or DCI-P3 standards.

## Section 4

AUDIO SYNC & SOUND DESIGN IN EDITING  
Framework: Precise audio-video synchronization is critical; lip-sync errors greater than ±20 ms are perceptible. The editing process integrates diegetic sounds, Foley, ADR, and music to reinforce narrative and emotional cues.  
Method:  
- Use timecode or waveform matching for initial sync.  
- Employ the J-cut and L-cut techniques to overlap audio and video transitions, enhancing flow.  
- Apply equalization and compression to dialogue tracks to maintain clarity.  
- Balance sound levels using LUFS (Loudness Units relative to Full Scale), targeting -23 LUFS for broadcast standards.  
- Layer ambient sounds and effects to create immersive soundscapes.

## Section 5

MONTAGE THEORY & INTELLECTUAL MONTAGE  
Framework: Derived from Eisenstein’s montage theory, intellectual montage juxtaposes shots to create new meaning beyond individual images. The formula involves thesis + antithesis = synthesis in sequential shots.  
Example:  
- Shot A: A calm landscape (thesis).  
- Shot B: A ticking clock (antithesis).  
- Shot C: A character’s anxious face (synthesis).  
Steps:  
1. Identify thematic opposites in footage.  
2. Arrange shots to provoke cognitive association.  
3. Use rhythmic cutting to emphasize conceptual tension.  
4. Employ superimpositions or graphic matches to reinforce synthesis.

## Section 6

MULTI-CAMERA EDITING & SYNCING WORKFLOW  
Framework: Multi-camera shoots require precise synchronization and switching between angles to maintain narrative clarity and energy.  
Steps:  
1. Sync cameras using common timecode or audio claps.  
2. Use software multicam features (e.g., Premiere Pro’s Multi-Camera Source Sequence).  
3. Mark sync points and create angle groups.  
4. Edit live or in post by switching angles on the fly, prioritizing coverage and performance.  
5. Maintain continuity in eyelines and screen direction across cuts.

## Section 7

EXPORT FORMATS & CODECS OPTIMIZATION  
Framework: Deliverables must balance quality, compression, and compatibility. Common codecs include H.264 (widely compatible, efficient), ProRes (high quality, editing-friendly), and DNxHD (broadcast standard).  
Guidelines:  
- For web: H.264, 1920x1080, 8 Mbps bitrate, 30 fps.  
- For broadcast: ProRes 422 HQ, 10-bit 4:2:2 color, 1920x1080, 25 or 30 fps.  
- For archival: Uncompressed or lossless codecs (e.g., FFV1).  
- Always check color space (Rec.709), frame rate consistency, and audio channel layout (stereo or 5.1).

## Mechanisms

The video editing process involves a series of technical and creative steps. It begins with ingest, where footage is imported into the editing system, either by transferring files from a camera or other storage device, or by digitizing analog footage. Next, the editor organizes the footage into a hierarchical structure, creating bins, folders, and labels to categorize and prioritize the clips. The editor then reviews and selects the desired footage, often creating a rough assembly or stringout of the selected clips. This is followed by the addition of audio elements, such as music, sound effects, and dialogue, which are synchronized with the video using a process called multi-track editing. The editor then refines the edit, making fine adjustments to the timing, pacing, and flow of the sequence, using techniques such as cutting, trimming, and slipping. Transitions and effects are added to enhance the visual and emotional impact of the edit, and color correction and grading are applied to establish a consistent aesthetic. Finally, the edited sequence is output in a suitable format for distribution, whether that be a digital file, a physical tape, or a cinematic print. Throughout this process, the editor works within a non-linear editing system, which allows for flexible and iterative refinement of the edit, with each change having a direct causal impact on the final output.

## Methods And Frameworks

In video editing for the arts, several methods and frameworks guide the creative process. The Three-Act Structure is a narrative framework used to build a story, comprising setup, confrontation, and resolution. It is effective for creating a cohesive storyline but may fail if the acts are not balanced, leading to pacing issues. 
The 180-Degree Rule is a camera placement method that maintains spatial continuity by keeping the camera on one side of an imaginary line between characters. It is useful for establishing a consistent perspective but may fail if not applied consistently, causing disorientation. 
The Match Cut technique involves cutting between two shots with similar visual elements, creating a seamless transition. It is effective for conveying a passage of time or a change in location but may fail if the visuals are not similar enough, making the cut jarring. 
The Kuleshov Effect is a editing principle that demonstrates how the same shot can have different meanings depending on the context provided by adjacent shots. It is useful for creating complex emotions and themes but may fail if the context is not clear, leading to confusion. 
The Continuity Editing method involves creating a seamless flow of action across cuts by matching elements such as action, camera angle, and lighting. It is effective for creating a realistic and immersive experience but may fail if the continuity is not maintained, causing distractions. 
These methods and frameworks provide a foundation for video editing in the arts, but their effective application depends on the editor's understanding of the story, audience, and visual language.

In video editing for arts, worked examples illustrate the application of editing techniques to create a visually cohesive narrative. 
1. **Pacing and Timing**: A film editor is working on a scene where a character is walking towards the camera. The editor wants to create a sense of urgency, so they decide to use a series of quick cuts to shorten the duration of the walk. If the original clip is 30 seconds long and the editor wants to reduce it to 10 seconds, they would need to cut the clip into 3 sections, each 3.33 seconds long, to achieve the desired pacing.
2. **J-Cut and L-Cut**: An editor is working on a dialogue scene between two characters. To create a smooth transition between the two speakers, the editor decides to use a J-Cut, where the audio of the second speaker starts playing before the video cuts to them. If the audio of the second speaker starts 2 seconds before the video cuts, and the video cut is 1 second long, the editor would need to extend the audio of the first speaker by 1 second to create a seamless transition.
3. **Color Grading**: A colorist is working on a music video and wants to create a consistent color palette throughout the project. If the colorist wants to increase the saturation of a particular shot by 20% and the current saturation level is 50%, they would need to adjust the color curve to achieve a new saturation level of 60%. This would involve adjusting the RGB values of the shot to create a more vibrant and cohesive look.

## Applications

In the arts, video editing is a crucial post-production process used to shape the narrative, tone, and aesthetic of a film, television show, or video project. Editors use specialized software, such as Avid Media Composer, Adobe Premiere Pro, or Final Cut Pro, to assemble footage, add visual effects, and fine-tune the pacing and rhythm of a scene. The goal of video editing is to create a cohesive and engaging visual story that enhances the overall impact of the project. In practice, editors work closely with directors and producers to ensure that the final product aligns with their creative vision. They apply various techniques, including continuity editing, montage, and cross-cutting, to control the flow of information, build tension, and evoke emotions. Video editing is also used in music videos, commercials, and live events, where it plays a key role in shaping the audience's experience and perception of the performance or message. Additionally, editors may use color grading and sound design to enhance the mood and atmosphere of a scene, further emphasizing the importance of video editing in the arts. By manipulating the visual and auditory elements of a project, editors can significantly influence the audience's interpretation and response to the work.

## Common Errors

In video editing, as studied in the arts, common mistakes can detract from the overall narrative, aesthetic, and emotional impact of a film or video. One prevalent error is inconsistent pacing, where the editor fails to balance the duration of shots, leading to a disjointed viewing experience. This can be due to a lack of consideration for the emotional resonance of each scene or an inability to effectively convey the passage of time. Another mistake is the overuse or misuse of transitions, such as excessive application of cross-fades or dissolves, which can distract from the content and create a sense of visual chaos. Furthermore, editors may neglect to maintain continuity, resulting in jarring discrepancies in lighting, camera angles, or character placement between cuts. Additionally, the inappropriate use of music or sound effects can disrupt the mood or atmosphere of a scene, pulling the viewer out of the narrative. These errors often stem from a lack of attention to detail, insufficient planning in the pre-production phase, or inadequate understanding of the fundamental principles of storytelling and visual composition. By recognizing and addressing these common mistakes, video editors can refine their craft and produce more engaging, cohesive, and effective visual narratives.

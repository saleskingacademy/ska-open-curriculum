# Architecture

> The course applies architectural principles to real situations using standard tools and methods.

## Foundations

Architecture is the disciplined art and science of designing and constructing built environments that mediate human experience, functionality, and aesthetics within spatial and temporal contexts. At its core, architecture synthesizes form, function, structure, and context through iterative design processes governed by principles of proportion, scale, materiality, and environmental responsiveness. First principles include Vitruvius’s triad—firmitas (durability), utilitas (utility), venustas (beauty)—which remain foundational. Architecture operates at multiple scales, from tectonic detail to urban morphology, integrating technical systems (structural, mechanical, electrical) with cultural, social, and ecological imperatives. It is both a tangible artifact and a conceptual framework, requiring mastery of spatial cognition, construction technology, and regulatory frameworks.

In the context of arts, architecture refers to the design and planning of built environments, encompassing aesthetic, functional, and technical considerations. A practitioner, or architect, must understand core definitions, including **tectonics**, which denotes the science and art of construction, and **spatiality**, referring to the relationship between physical spaces and human experience. Key vocabulary includes **form**, the physical appearance of a building, and **function**, its intended purpose. **Scale** and **proportion** are also crucial, as they relate to the size and harmony of architectural elements. **Materiality** is another essential concept, concerning the properties and applications of various building materials. Architects must also consider **context**, the social, cultural, and environmental setting of a structure. First principles include understanding the interplay between **mass** and **void**, the balance of solid and empty spaces, and the role of **light** in shaping the user's experience. Familiarity with **orthographic projection**, a method of representing three-dimensional spaces through two-dimensional drawings, is also necessary. By grasping these foundational concepts, architects can develop a nuanced understanding of the complex relationships between built forms, users, and environments. **Form** and **function** are fundamental principles, with form describing the physical appearance and structure of a building, and function relating to its purpose and usability. The vocabulary of architecture includes terms like **facade**, the exterior face of a building, and **elevation**, a two-dimensional representation of a building's exterior. **Materiality** and **texture** also play crucial roles, influencing the aesthetic and tactile qualities of a built environment. Understanding these foundational concepts enables architects to create cohesive, effective, and beautiful designs that balance artistic vision with technical requirements.

## Section 1

DESIGN METHODOLOGY – THE DESIGN THINKING FRAMEWORK  
Design Thinking in architecture follows five iterative stages: Empathize, Define, Ideate, Prototype, and Test (as codified by IDEO).  
- Empathize: Conduct ethnographic research and site analysis using tools like GIS mapping and environmental impact assessments.  
- Define: Synthesize findings into a clear problem statement, e.g., “Design a mixed-use community center that fosters social cohesion while achieving LEED Gold certification.”  
- Ideate: Generate 100+ concepts using methods such as morphological charts and SCAMPER (Substitute, Combine, Adapt, Modify, Put to another use, Eliminate, Reverse).  
- Prototype: Develop physical or digital models via BIM software (Revit, ArchiCAD) and rapid prototyping (3D printing, CNC milling).  
- Test: Conduct user experience simulations and daylighting analysis (Radiance, DIVA for Rhino) to validate design hypotheses.

## Section 2

STRUCTURAL SYSTEMS – THE LOAD PATH FORMULA  
Structural integrity relies on understanding load paths and force distribution. The fundamental formula governing axial load-bearing elements is:  
\[ \sigma = \frac{P}{A} \]  
where \(\sigma\) is stress (Pa), \(P\) is axial load (N), and \(A\) is cross-sectional area (m²).  
Key structural frameworks include:  
- Load-bearing masonry (compressive strength ~10-50 MPa) with buttressing for lateral loads.  
- Steel frame construction using moment-resisting frames with yield strength ~250 MPa (A36 steel).  
- Reinforced concrete combining compressive strength (~30 MPa) and tensile reinforcement (rebar, typically 500 MPa yield strength).  
Design steps: determine loads (dead, live, wind, seismic per ASCE 7-16), calculate member stresses, select materials, and detail connections per AISC or Eurocode standards.

## Section 3

SUSTAINABILITY FRAMEWORK – THE PASSIVE HOUSE STANDARD  
The Passive House (Passivhaus) standard mandates rigorous energy efficiency:  
- Annual heating demand ≤ 15 kWh/m²/year  
- Primary energy demand ≤ 120 kWh/m²/year  
- Airtightness ≤ 0.6 air changes per hour at 50 Pa pressure (blower door test)  
- Thermal bridge-free design and high-performance insulation (U-values ≤ 0.15 W/m²K for walls).  
Design steps: optimize building orientation, maximize solar gain, use triple-glazed windows with low-e coatings, incorporate mechanical ventilation with heat recovery (MVHR), and employ thermal mass for diurnal temperature regulation.

## Section 4

URBAN DESIGN – THE TRANSECT MODEL  
The Rural-Urban Transect organizes urban form into six zones (T1-T6) from natural to urban core:  
- T1 Natural (preserved ecosystems)  
- T2 Rural (agriculture, low-density housing)  
- T3 Sub-urban (single-family homes, low density)  
- T4 General Urban (mixed-use, moderate density)  
- T5 Urban Center (high density, commercial)  
- T6 Urban Core (central business district, tallest buildings)  
Framework application: zoning regulations, street hierarchy, and building typologies are aligned with transect zones to promote walkability, mixed-use development, and sustainable infrastructure. Steps include transect mapping, overlaying demographic data, and calibrating FAR (floor area ratio) limits per zone.

## Section 5

DIGITAL TOOLS – BIM IMPLEMENTATION PROTOCOL  
Building Information Modeling (BIM) integrates multidisciplinary data into a shared 3D model. The BIM Execution Plan (BEP) defines:  
- Level of Development (LOD) standards (LOD 100 conceptual to LOD 500 as-built)  
- Data exchange formats (IFC, COBie)  
- Clash detection protocols using Navisworks or Solibri (thresholds for allowable interference <5mm)  
- Model coordination cycles (weekly sprints with issue tracking)  
- Integration with cost estimation (5D BIM) and scheduling (4D BIM)  
Steps: establish project standards, assign roles (BIM Manager, Coordinators), set up cloud collaboration (Autodesk BIM 360), and conduct regular model audits.

## Section 6

MATERIALITY – THE THERMAL MASS FORMULA  
Thermal mass moderates indoor temperature fluctuations by absorbing and releasing heat. The heat storage capacity \(Q\) is calculated as:  
\[ Q = m \times c \times \Delta T \]  
where \(m\) = mass (kg), \(c\) = specific heat capacity (J/kg·K), \(\Delta T\) = temperature change (K).  
Common materials: concrete (c ≈ 880 J/kg·K), brick (c ≈ 840 J/kg·K), rammed earth (c ≈ 1000 J/kg·K).  
Design involves calculating required mass to buffer diurnal temperature swings, integrating with insulation and ventilation strategies to optimize energy performance.

## Section 7

REGULATORY COMPLIANCE – THE INTERNATIONAL BUILDING CODE (IBC)  
The IBC (latest edition 2021) governs safety, accessibility, and egress:  
- Occupancy classifications (e.g., Assembly A-2, Business B, Residential R-2) dictate fire-resistance ratings (minimum 1-hr for Type II construction).  
- Means of egress calculations: minimum width = occupant load × 0.2 inches per occupant for stairways.  
- Seismic design per ASCE 7 seismic response coefficients, importance factors, and site class.  
- Accessibility compliance per ADA Standards: minimum door width 32 inches clear, ramp slope ≤ 1:12.  
Steps: determine occupancy and construction type, calculate occupant load, design egress paths, and obtain plan review approvals.

## Mechanisms

In the context of arts, architecture's mechanisms involve a complex interplay of creative, technical, and functional elements. The process begins with the conception of a design, where an architect's vision is influenced by factors such as the client's requirements, site conditions, budget, and cultural context. This initial phase involves brainstorming, sketching, and developing a preliminary design. The next step is the development of a schematic design, which refines the initial concept into a more detailed and coherent plan. This is followed by the design development phase, where the architect selects materials, specifies systems, and finalizes the layout. The construction documents phase then translates the design into a set of technical drawings and specifications that can be used for construction. Throughout these phases, the architect must balance aesthetic considerations with functional and technical requirements, ensuring that the building is not only visually appealing but also safe, sustainable, and meets the needs of its users. The causal chain is thus: conception influences schematic design, which in turn affects design development, and ultimately, construction documents determine the final built form. Understanding these mechanisms is crucial for appreciating how architectural designs are transformed into physical structures that interact with their environment and users.

## Methods And Frameworks

In the arts, architectural analysis employs various methods and frameworks to deconstruct and understand built environments. The Space Syntax method, developed by Bill Hillier, is used to analyze the social and spatial structures of buildings and cities, identifying patterns of movement and interaction. It is particularly effective when studying urban planning and design, but may fail to account for cultural or historical contexts. 
The Chora model, inspired by the work of Jacques Derrida, examines the relationships between buildings, users, and contexts, highlighting the complexities of architectural meaning. It is useful when analyzing the symbolic or metaphorical aspects of buildings, but may become overly theoretical and detached from practical considerations. 
The Deconstructivist approach, influenced by the work of Jacques Derrida and Peter Eisenman, challenges traditional notions of architecture and encourages experimentation with unconventional forms and materials. It is effective when pushing the boundaries of innovative design, but may result in buildings that are impractical or inaccessible. 
The SWOT analysis framework (Strengths, Weaknesses, Opportunities, Threats) is used to evaluate the feasibility of architectural projects, considering factors such as site constraints, budget, and user needs. It is useful for identifying potential problems and opportunities, but may oversimplify complex issues or overlook critical factors. 
The Layered Analysis method, developed by N.J. Habraken, involves breaking down buildings into distinct layers, such as site, structure, and services, to understand their composition and performance. It is effective when analyzing existing buildings or designing new ones, but may fail to account for the dynamic interactions between layers. 
Each of these methods and frameworks has its strengths and limitations, and the effective architect must be able to select and combine them appropriately, depending on the specific project requirements and context.

## Worked Examples

To analyze the composition of a building's façade, consider the example of the Pantheon in Rome. Suppose we want to calculate the golden ratio (φ) in the design of the Pantheon's portico. The height of the portico is approximately 33.5 meters, and the width is about 20.1 meters. To find the golden ratio, we use the formula: φ = (a + b) / a, where a and b are the dimensions of the rectangle. However, since the golden ratio is an irrational number (approximately 1.618), we can use the portico's dimensions to see how close they come to this ratio.

Another example is the analysis of the spatial relationships in a building, such as the Guggenheim Museum in Bilbao. The museum's atrium has a height of 45.7 meters and a diameter of 37.1 meters. To find the ratio of the height to the diameter, we divide the height by the diameter: 45.7 / 37.1 ≈ 1.23. This ratio can be compared to other architectural elements to understand the designer's intentions.

Lastly, consider the example of calculating the area of a building's floor plan, such as the Farnsworth House. The house has a rectangular floor plan with dimensions 24.4 meters by 12.2 meters. To find the area, we multiply the length by the width: 24.4 * 12.2 = 298.08 square meters. This calculation can help in understanding the spatial efficiency and layout of the building.

To analyze the architectural elements of a building, consider the following examples. 
1. Calculating the Golden Ratio in a facade: The Parthenon's facade is approximately 30.88 meters wide and 13.72 meters high. To determine if the width-to-height ratio approaches the Golden Ratio (approximately 1.618), divide the width by the height: 30.88 / 13.72 ≈ 2.25, which does not exactly match the Golden Ratio, indicating the Parthenon's design may prioritize other aesthetic principles.
2. Determining the proportion of elements in a composition: In Andrea Palladio's Villa Capra, the central block is 18.45 meters wide, and the wings are each 9.22 meters wide. To find the proportion of the central block to the wings, divide the width of the central block by the width of a wing: 18.45 / 9.22 ≈ 2, demonstrating a 1:2 proportion, which is a common principle in Palladian architecture.
3. Analyzing the balance of a building's massing: The Guggenheim Museum in Bilbao, designed by Frank Gehry, has a total floor area of approximately 24,000 square meters. If the building's mass is divided into two main sections - the atrium (4,000 square meters) and the exhibition space (20,000 square meters) - the ratio of the atrium to the exhibition space is 4,000 / 20,000 = 0.2, or 1:5, illustrating how the building's massing is balanced through asymmetry and the use of negative space.

## Applications

In the arts, architecture is applied in various practices, including building design, urban planning, and landscape architecture. Architects use their knowledge of spatial relationships, materials, and aesthetics to create functional and visually appealing structures. They consider factors such as climate, culture, and social context to design buildings that meet the needs of users. For instance, a building designed for a hot and humid climate may incorporate features such as large overhangs, courtyards, and natural ventilation to reduce the need for air conditioning. In urban planning, architects apply their understanding of human behavior, transportation systems, and community development to design public spaces that promote social interaction, safety, and sustainability. Landscape architecture involves the design of outdoor spaces, such as parks, gardens, and public plazas, to create aesthetically pleasing and functional environments that balance human needs with environmental concerns. Architects also apply their skills in restoration and conservation of historic buildings, adapting them for modern use while preserving their cultural significance. Additionally, architecture informs the design of temporary structures, such as exhibition spaces, stage sets, and installation art, which require creative and innovative solutions to spatial and functional challenges. By considering the social, cultural, and environmental implications of their designs, architects can create buildings and spaces that enhance the human experience and contribute to the richness and diversity of the built environment.

In the arts, architecture is applied in various practices that combine creative vision, technical skills, and historical context. Architects use their knowledge of design principles, materials, and construction methods to create functional and aesthetically pleasing buildings and spaces. The application of architecture in practice involves a deep understanding of the social, cultural, and environmental factors that influence building design. For instance, architects consider the needs of users, the character of the surrounding landscape, and the availability of resources when designing a building. They also draw upon historical and contemporary architectural styles, such as Modernism, Art Deco, or Deconstructivism, to inform their design decisions. Additionally, architects must balance artistic expression with practical considerations like sustainability, accessibility, and structural integrity. The use of computer-aided design (CAD) software and building information modeling (BIM) has also become essential in architectural practice, enabling architects to create detailed, precise designs and collaborate with other professionals like engineers and contractors. Ultimately, the application of architecture in practice requires a nuanced understanding of the complex interplay between artistic vision, technical expertise, and social responsibility.

## Common Errors

In the study of architecture as an arts subject, practitioners often make mistakes that can compromise the aesthetic and functional integrity of a building or structure. One common error is the misuse of historical styles, where elements from different periods and movements are combined inappropriately, resulting in a pastiche that lacks coherence and authenticity. For example, adding Gothic arches to a building with a predominantly Art Deco facade can create a jarring visual discord. Another mistake is the failure to consider the context and scale of a building, leading to structures that overwhelm or clash with their surroundings. This can be seen in cases where a large, modern building is inserted into a historic neighborhood, disrupting the existing character and proportions of the area. Additionally, some practitioners neglect to balance form and function, prioritizing visual appeal over practical considerations such as sustainability, accessibility, and user experience. This can result in buildings that are impressive to look at but difficult or uncomfortable to inhabit. By understanding these common errors, architects and artists can develop a more nuanced and informed approach to designing and analyzing buildings, one that takes into account the complex interplay of aesthetic, historical, and functional factors.

In the study of architecture as an arts subject, several common mistakes are observed among practitioners. One of the primary errors is the misconception that architectural styles are solely defined by their visual characteristics, such as the use of arches, columns, or domes. This oversimplification neglects the historical, cultural, and social contexts that shape architectural movements. For instance, the Gothic style is often reduced to its pointed arches and ribbed vaults, while its origins in medieval Europe and its relationship to the Catholic Church are overlooked. Another mistake is the failure to consider the functional and spatial aspects of a building, focusing solely on its aesthetic appeal. This can lead to an incomplete understanding of the architect's intentions and the building's purpose. Furthermore, the misattribution of architectural styles or periods can occur when practitioners rely on superficial similarities rather than a thorough analysis of the building's design, materials, and historical context. Additionally, the lack of consideration for the social and cultural impact of architecture on its users and the surrounding community is a significant error, as it neglects the fundamental role of architecture in shaping human experience. By recognizing these common errors, practitioners can develop a more nuanced and comprehensive understanding of architecture as an arts subject.

## Advanced

In the realm of arts, advanced architectural studies delve into the intricacies of theoretical frameworks, critiquing and expanding upon existing notions of space, form, and function. Graduate-level research often explores the intersections of architecture with other disciplines, such as philosophy, sociology, and environmental studies. Key areas of investigation include the phenomenology of architecture, examining how built environments shape human experience and perception. The concept of "atmosphere" in architecture, as coined by philosopher Gernot Böhme, is also a subject of inquiry, highlighting the emotional and sensory qualities of spaces. Furthermore, the field is moving towards a more nuanced understanding of sustainability, incorporating principles of ecological design, social justice, and community engagement. Open questions persist regarding the role of technology in architectural practice, including the impact of digital tools on the design process and the potential for emerging technologies like augmented reality to redefine the built environment. Additionally, the relationship between architecture and globalization is a pressing concern, as the discipline navigates the tensions between cultural homogenization and local identity. Ultimately, advanced studies in architecture seek to foster a deeper understanding of the complex interplay between built forms, social contexts, and human values.

# Documentary Filmmaking

> The course teaches applied practice of documentary filmmaking, including pre-production, production, editing, and distribution.

## Pre-Production

Research, story development, treatment writing, securing access/subjects, funding (grants, commissions, crowdfunding). PRODUCTION: Interview techniques (B-roll, A-roll), observational/vérité filming, ethical considerations (informed consent, power dynamics). Archival research. STYLES: Expository, observational, participatory, reflexive, poetic, performative (Nichols modes). EDITING: Story structure (three-act, thematic, chronological), paper edit, assembly, rough cut, fine cut. Sound design, music licensing. DISTRIBUTION: Festival circuit (Sundance, IDFA, Hot Docs), broadcast, streaming platforms, educational distribution. ETHICS: Truth vs manipulation, subject relationships, fair portrayal, verification of claims. BUSINESS: Production budget, completion funds, impact campaigns.

## Foundations

In media communications, documentary filmmaking refers to a non-fiction film genre that documents reality, typically focusing on informational, educational, or historical content. A **documentary** is a film that aims to inform, educate, or persuade the audience about a particular topic or issue. The core principles of documentary filmmaking involve **observation**, **interviews**, and **narration**, which provide the foundation for storytelling and conveying information. Key vocabulary includes **cinéma vérité**, a filmmaking style that emphasizes observational techniques, and **expository mode**, a narrative approach that uses voiceover, interviews, and visuals to convey information. **Ethnographic filmmaking** is a subgenre that focuses on the study of human cultures and societies. A **practitioner** must understand these core concepts, as well as terms like **treatment**, a written document outlining the film's concept, and **pitch**, a brief presentation used to secure funding or support for a project. Additionally, familiarity with **film language**, including **mise-en-scène**, **montage**, and **diegesis**, is essential for effective storytelling and communication.

## Mechanisms

Documentary filmmaking involves a series of mechanisms that work together to create a cohesive narrative. The process begins with research and development, where filmmakers conduct interviews, gather archival footage, and collect data to form the foundation of their story. This information is then used to create a treatment, a written document outlining the film's concept, structure, and tone. The treatment serves as a guide for the next stage: pre-production, where the filmmaking team secures funding, locations, and equipment. 
During production, the team captures footage and conducts interviews, following a shot list and schedule to ensure efficient use of time and resources. The footage is then edited into a rough cut, which is refined through a series of revisions until a final cut is achieved. Sound design, music composition, and visual effects are added to enhance the narrative and emotional impact of the film.

Each stage relies on the previous one, creating a linear progression from concept to completion. ## Methods And Frameworks

In documentary filmmaking, several methods and frameworks guide the creative process. The Observational method involves direct observation of events, often using handheld cameras, and is suitable for capturing real-time events or intimate moments. The Expository method, on the other hand, relies on voiceover narration and interviews to convey information, making it ideal for educational or informative documentaries. The Participatory method involves the filmmaker's active participation in the story, often blurring the line between observer and participant, and is useful for building trust with subjects. The Reflexive method acknowledges the filmmaker's presence and influence on the story, making it suitable for self-aware, meta-documentaries. 
The Kurosawa framework emphasizes the importance of capturing the "truth" of a moment, rather than staging or manipulating events. The Cinéma Vérité approach, developed by Jean Rouch, emphasizes the use of handheld cameras and natural lighting to create a sense of immediacy and realism. 
Failure modes for these methods include the Observer Effect, where the filmmaker's presence alters the behavior of the subjects, and the risk of manipulation or exploitation of subjects in Participatory or Expository approaches. Additionally, an over-reliance on formulaic structures can lead to a lack of creativity and authenticity in the final product.

## Common Errors

In documentary filmmaking, practitioners often make mistakes that compromise the integrity and effectiveness of their films. One common error is the failure to obtain proper release forms from interview subjects, which can lead to legal issues and compromise the film's distribution. Another mistake is the lack of transparency in editing, where filmmakers manipulate interviews or events to fit a predetermined narrative, rather than allowing the story to unfold organically. Additionally, some filmmakers neglect to provide context and balance in their storytelling, presenting a biased or one-sided perspective that undermines the film's credibility. Furthermore, poor research and inadequate fact-checking can lead to inaccuracies and misinformation, damaging the film's reputation and the filmmaker's credibility. These errors can be avoided by adhering to ethical standards, being mindful of the power dynamics between filmmaker and subject, and prioritizing accuracy and fairness in the storytelling process.

Sculpture refers to a three-dimensional art form created through various techniques and materials, where an artist shapes, molds, or assembles objects to convey meaning, express ideas, or explore form and texture. A practitioner of sculpture is called a sculptor. The core definitions in sculpture include form, which refers to the overall structure and shape of a three-dimensional work, and texture, which is the surface quality or "feel" of the artwork. 
First principles of sculpture involve understanding the elements of art, including line, shape, value, and space. Line in sculpture refers to the path the viewer's eye follows when looking at the work, while shape encompasses the overall configuration of the three-dimensional form. Value denotes the lightness or darkness of the sculpture's surface, and space refers to the positive and negative areas within and around the work. 
Key vocabulary in sculpture includes modeling, which involves shaping or molding a material, such as clay, to create a form; carving, which entails cutting or removing material to reveal a shape; and casting, a technique where a liquid material is poured into a mold to create a replica of an original form. Assemblage refers to the process of combining disparate objects to create a new whole, while relief sculpture involves creating a design or image that projects from a flat background. 
A sculptor must also be familiar with various materials, including metals, such as bronze or steel; stones, like marble or granite; and woods, which can be carved or assembled to create a work. Other essential terms include patina, which is the layer of oxidation or tarnish that forms on the surface of a metal sculpture, and armature, which refers to the internal framework or support system used to stabilize or shape a sculptural form.

## Mechanisms

Sculpture creation involves a series of physical and creative steps. The process begins with conceptualization, where the artist envisions the final product, considering factors such as theme, medium, and scale. This idea is then translated into a preliminary design, often through sketches or models, allowing the artist to refine their concept. The next step involves selecting and preparing the materials, which can range from traditional media like marble, wood, or clay, to more modern materials like steel, glass, or found objects. The artist must consider the properties of the chosen material, such as its durability, texture, and workability, as these factors will influence the creation process. With materials in hand, the artist proceeds to execute their design, using various techniques and tools specific to the medium. For example, carving involves subtracting material to reveal the form, while modeling involves adding material to build up the form. As the sculpture takes shape, the artist may refine details, apply finishes, or integrate additional elements. Throughout this process, the artist's vision is continually refined and adapted, with each step informing and influencing the next, until the final work is complete. The causal chain is thus: concept influences design, design dictates material selection, material properties guide technique, and technique determines the final form of the sculpture.

The artist must consider factors like texture, proportion, and balance, making adjustments as the work progresses.

The final step involves presenting the sculpture, which could involve mounting, installing, or displaying it in a specific context to engage the viewer and convey the intended message. Throughout this process, the artist's skill, creativity, and attention to detail are crucial in transforming their initial concept into a physical reality.

## Methods And Frameworks

In sculpture, various methods and frameworks guide the creative process. The Direct Method involves directly shaping materials like clay or stone without a preconceived model, suitable for expressive and spontaneous works. The Indirect Method uses a model or maquette to create a detailed plan before executing the sculpture, ideal for complex and large-scale pieces. The Subtraction Method, exemplified by Michelangelo's technique, starts with a solid block of material and removes excess to reveal the form within, often used for marble and stone sculptures. The Addition Method, commonly used in clay and wax modeling, builds up the form by adding material, suitable for creating intricate details and textures. The Assemblage Method involves combining disparate elements to create a new whole, often used in mixed-media and found-object sculptures. Failure modes include over-reliance on a single method, neglecting to consider the material's properties, and insufficient planning, leading to structural weaknesses or creative stagnation. Understanding these methods and frameworks enables sculptors to approach their work with intention and flexibility, adapting techniques to suit their artistic vision and medium.

## Common Errors

Sculptors often fall into several common pitfalls that can detract from the overall impact and effectiveness of their work. One of the primary mistakes is a lack of consideration for the viewer's perspective, resulting in sculptures that are overly reliant on a single vantage point. This can lead to a work that appears unbalanced or incomplete when viewed from alternative angles. Another error is the failure to balance scale and proportion, either within the sculpture itself or in relation to its surroundings. This can cause the work to appear incongruous or even comical. Additionally, some sculptors neglect to consider the material properties and limitations of their chosen medium, leading to technical issues or an overall lack of cohesion. The misuse of texture and pattern can also be problematic, as it can overwhelm or distract from the underlying form and intent of the sculpture. Furthermore, inadequate attention to the relationship between the sculpture and its environment can result in a work that appears disconnected or incongruous with its setting. By being aware of these potential pitfalls, sculptors can take steps to avoid them and create more effective, engaging, and well-rounded works of art.

## Advanced

The graduate-level extensions of sculpture involve a deep exploration of the medium's theoretical underpinnings, technical innovations, and its intersections with other art forms. One key area of investigation is the concept of "post-studio" sculpture, which challenges traditional notions of the artist's studio as the primary site of production. This idea, influenced by the work of artists such as Robert Smithson and Daniel Buren, highlights the importance of context, site-specificity, and the role of the viewer in shaping the meaning of a sculptural work.

Another significant development is the incorporation of digital technologies, such as 3D printing, virtual reality, and digital modeling, which have expanded the possibilities for sculptural form and presentation. The use of these technologies raises important questions about the nature of materiality, the relationship between the physical and the virtual, and the potential for sculpture to engage with new audiences and environments.

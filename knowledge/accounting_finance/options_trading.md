# Options Trading

> The course assumes prior knowledge of finance and accounting concepts, and its content is consistent with a capstone course in auditing and taxation or a related field.

## Foundations

Options trading is the practice of buying and selling derivative contracts that confer the right, but not the obligation, to buy (call option) or sell (put option) an underlying asset at a specified strike price before or at expiration. Rooted in the principles of leverage, asymmetric payoff, and time decay, options enable sophisticated risk management, speculation, and income strategies. The foundational model for option valuation is the Black-Scholes-Merton (BSM) framework, which prices European options using parameters: underlying price (S), strike price (K), time to expiration (T), risk-free rate (r), volatility (σ), and dividend yield (q). The intrinsic value (max(0, S-K) for calls) plus time value (extrinsic premium) compose the option’s price. Greeks—Delta, Gamma, Theta, Vega, Rho—quantify sensitivities to underlying price, volatility, time decay, and interest rates, forming the basis for dynamic hedging and risk management.

In options trading, a **derivative** is a financial instrument whose value is derived from an underlying asset, such as stocks, commodities, or currencies. The underlying asset is the security that the option is based on. A **call option** gives the holder the right, but not the obligation, to buy the underlying asset at a predetermined price, known as the **strike price**. Conversely, a **put option** gives the holder the right, but not the obligation, to sell the underlying asset at the strike price. The **option premium** is the price paid for the option contract. The **expiration date** is the last day on which the option can be exercised. **American options** can be exercised at any time before expiration, while **European options** can only be exercised on the expiration date. **In-the-money** options have a strike price that is favorable compared to the current market price, while **out-of-the-money** options have an unfavorable strike price. **At-the-money** options have a strike price equal to the current market price. Understanding these core definitions and principles is essential for a practitioner to navigate the complex world of options trading.

## Section 1

OPTION TYPES AND PAYOFF STRUCTURES  
Options are categorized by type (call vs. put), style (American vs. European), and underlying (equities, indices, commodities). The payoff for a European call at expiration is max(0, S_T - K), and for a put max(0, K - S_T). American options allow early exercise, complicating valuation. Real-world practice often involves American equity options with monthly expirations. Payoff diagrams visualize profit/loss profiles, essential for strategy construction. For example, a covered call combines long stock with short call, capping upside but generating premium income. Understanding payoff asymmetry is critical: calls have unlimited upside, puts have limited downside to zero.

## Section 2

BLACK-SCHOLES-MERTON MODEL AND IMPLIED VOLATILITY  
The BSM formula for a European call option price C is:  
C = S e^{-qT} N(d1) - K e^{-rT} N(d2), where  
d1 = [ln(S/K) + (r - q + σ²/2)T] / (σ√T),  
d2 = d1 - σ√T,  
and N(·) is the cumulative standard normal distribution. The model assumes lognormal price distribution, constant volatility, and frictionless markets. Implied volatility (IV) is the σ that equates the BSM price to the market price, extracted via numerical root-finding (e.g., Newton-Raphson). IV surfaces across strikes and expirations reveal market sentiment and skew, crucial for relative value trading and volatility arbitrage.

## Section 3

THE GREEKS AND RISK MANAGEMENT  
Delta (Δ) measures option price sensitivity to underlying price changes: Δ_call ≈ N(d1), Δ_put = Δ_call - 1. Gamma (Γ) is the second derivative, indicating curvature and rate of Delta change. Theta (Θ) quantifies time decay, typically negative for long options. Vega (ν) measures sensitivity to volatility changes, critical for volatility trading. Rho (ρ) captures sensitivity to interest rates. Traders construct Delta-neutral portfolios to hedge directional risk, then manage Gamma and Vega exposures dynamically. For example, a long straddle (buy call and put at same strike) has positive Vega and Gamma, profiting from volatility spikes but losing Theta daily.

## Section 4

STRATEGY FRAMEWORKS AND PAYOFF SYNTHESIS  
Options strategies synthesize complex payoff profiles from basic building blocks:  
- Spreads: vertical (bull call spread: long call K1, short call K2 > K1), horizontal/calendar (long near-term call, short longer-term call at same strike), diagonal (combination of vertical and horizontal).  
- Combinations: straddles (long call + long put same strike), strangles (long call + long put different strikes), butterflies (long call K1, short 2 calls K2, long call K3 with K1 < K2 < K3 equidistant).  
Each strategy has defined max profit/loss, breakeven points, and Greeks profile. For example, a bull call spread limits max loss to net premium paid and max gain to difference in strikes minus premium, suitable for moderately bullish views.

## Section 5

VOLATILITY SURFACE AND SMILE/SMIRK ANALYSIS  
Observed implied volatilities vary with strike and maturity, forming the volatility surface. Smiles (symmetric U-shape) and smirks/skews (asymmetric) reflect market perceptions of tail risk and supply-demand imbalances. The SABR (Stochastic Alpha Beta Rho) model parametrizes the surface with α (volatility level), β (elasticity), ρ (correlation), and ν (volatility of volatility). Traders use the surface to identify mispricings and construct volatility arbitrage trades, such as calendar spreads exploiting term structure or ratio spreads exploiting skew.

## Section 6

EARLY EXERCISE AND AMERICAN OPTION VALUATION  
American options introduce the possibility of early exercise, especially relevant for dividends and deep in-the-money puts. Binomial tree models (Cox-Ross-Rubinstein) discretize price paths to evaluate optimal exercise policies. For example, a call option on a dividend-paying stock may be exercised early to capture dividends exceeding remaining time value. The binomial model calculates option value recursively, comparing hold vs. exercise value at each node. This method also underpins finite difference PDE solvers used in advanced pricing.

## Section 7

ADVANCED TOPICS: STOCHASTIC VOLATILITY AND JUMP DIFFUSION  
Real markets exhibit volatility clustering and jumps, violating BSM assumptions. The Heston model introduces stochastic volatility with mean reversion:  
dS_t = μ S_t dt + √v_t S_t dW_t^S,  
dv_t = κ(θ - v_t) dt + σ_v √v_t dW_t^v,  
with correlation ρ between Brownian motions. This captures volatility smile dynamics and term structure. Merton’s jump diffusion model adds Poisson jumps to price process, modeling sudden large moves. Calibration of these models to market data improves pricing accuracy and hedging efficacy in turbulent markets.

## Mechanisms

In options trading, the mechanism involves a series of steps that facilitate the buying and selling of options contracts. The process begins with the listing of options on an exchange, such as the Chicago Board Options Exchange (CBOE). Here, market makers and other liquidity providers quote prices for options, creating a market. When an investor wants to buy or sell an option, they place an order with their broker, who then sends it to the exchange. The exchange matches the order with a corresponding order from another party, such as a market maker or another investor, based on price and other criteria. Once a match is found, the trade is executed, and the exchange notifies both parties. The buyer and seller then enter into a binding contract, which is cleared and settled through a clearinghouse, such as the Options Clearing Corporation (OCC). The clearinghouse acts as a intermediary, guaranteeing the contract and managing the risk. Throughout the life of the contract, the buyer has the right, but not the obligation, to exercise the option, while the seller is obligated to fulfill the contract if it is exercised. The option's price is determined by market forces, including the underlying asset's price, volatility, time to expiration, and interest rates, which influence the option's premium. As the option approaches expiration, its price converges towards its intrinsic value, and the contract is either exercised or expires worthless.

The profit or loss for both parties is then determined by the difference between the strike price and the market price of the underlying asset at the time of exercise.

## Methods And Frameworks

Options trading utilizes various methods, models, and formulas to evaluate and manage positions. The Black-Scholes model is a widely used framework for pricing European options, applying when the underlying asset's price is known and the risk-free rate is constant. However, its failure mode is the assumption of constant volatility, which can lead to inaccurate pricing in reality. The Binomial model is an alternative, used when the time to expiration is discrete, and the underlying asset's price movements are modeled as a series of binomial distributions. The Greeks (Delta, Gamma, Theta, and Vega) are essential frameworks for measuring options' sensitivities, with Delta used for hedging, Gamma for managing Delta, Theta for time decay, and Vega for volatility risk. The Monte Carlo simulation is applied when modeling complex scenarios, such as path-dependent options or stochastic volatility, but its failure mode is the reliance on random number generation, which can introduce variability in results. The Volatility Smile framework is used to address the Black-Scholes model's limitations, accounting for the empirical fact that implied volatility varies with strike price and expiration date. Each method and model has its strengths and weaknesses, and understanding their applications and limitations is crucial for effective options trading.

The Binomial model is another framework used to estimate option prices, particularly for American options, which can be exercised before expiration. The Greeks, including Delta, Gamma, Theta, and Vega, are used to measure the sensitivity of an option's price to changes in the underlying asset's price, volatility, and time to expiration. They are essential for managing options positions and hedging risks. 
The Monte Carlo simulation is a method used to estimate the expected return and risk of an options portfolio by simulating multiple scenarios. It is suitable when the underlying asset's price is expected to follow a complex distribution or when the options portfolio is complex. However, its failure mode lies in its reliance on historical data and assumptions about future market conditions. 
Each of these methods and frameworks has its strengths and weaknesses, and the choice of which one to use depends on the specific characteristics of the options position and the underlying asset.

## Worked Examples

To illustrate the concepts of options trading, consider the following examples. 
1. **Call Option**: Suppose an investor buys a call option for $100 to purchase 100 shares of XYZ stock at $50 per share. The current market price of XYZ stock is $55. If the investor exercises the option, they will buy the stock at $50 and sell it at $55, earning a profit of $5 per share, or $500 total. 
2. **Put Option**: An investor buys a put option for $200 to sell 100 shares of ABC stock at $40 per share. The current market price of ABC stock is $35. If the investor exercises the option, they will buy the stock at $35 and sell it at $40, earning a profit of $5 per share, or $500 total. 
3. **Straddle**: An investor buys a call option for $300 and a put option for $250, both with a strike price of $60, to trade 100 shares of DEF stock. The current market price of DEF stock is $58. If the stock price rises to $70, the investor will exercise the call option, buying the stock at $60 and selling it at $70, earning a profit of $10 per share, or $1000 total. If the stock price falls to $50, the investor will exercise the put option, buying the stock at $50 and selling it at $60, earning a profit of $10 per share, or $1000 total.

## Applications

In economics finance, options trading is used in practice for hedging, speculation, and portfolio management. Hedging involves using options to reduce or manage risk exposure, such as an investor buying a put option to protect against a potential decline in stock price. Speculation involves buying or selling options with the expectation of making a profit from price movements, such as buying a call option to bet on a stock's potential increase in value. Portfolio management uses options to adjust the risk profile of a portfolio, such as using covered calls to generate income or protective puts to limit potential losses. Options are also used in corporate finance, for example, to hedge against foreign exchange risk or commodity price fluctuations. Additionally, options are used in investment strategies such as spreads, straddles, and iron condors, which involve combining multiple options to profit from different market scenarios. The use of options trading requires a deep understanding of options pricing models, such as the Black-Scholes model, and the ability to analyze and manage risk.

Institutions, such as banks and pension funds, use options to manage their exposure to market fluctuations and interest rate changes. The flexibility and versatility of options trading make it a valuable tool for investors and institutions seeking to manage risk, generate returns, and achieve their financial objectives.

## Common Errors

In options trading, practitioners often make mistakes that can lead to significant financial losses. One common error is misunderstanding the concept of delta hedging, where traders fail to continuously adjust their hedge positions in response to changes in the underlying asset's price, resulting in delta mismatch. Another mistake is ignoring the impact of volatility on option pricing, as traders may underestimate the effect of increased volatility on option values, leading to incorrect pricing and trading decisions. Additionally, traders often confuse the concepts of historic volatility and implied volatility, using the wrong type of volatility in their pricing models. Furthermore, traders may also make errors in managing their risk exposure, such as failing to consider the gamma and vega risks associated with their option positions, which can lead to unexpected losses. Moreover, traders may incorrectly assume that options with longer expiration dates are less risky, when in fact, they can be more sensitive to changes in volatility and interest rates. These mistakes can be attributed to a lack of understanding of the underlying principles of options trading, including the Black-Scholes model and the Greeks, highlighting the importance of ongoing education and training in options trading.

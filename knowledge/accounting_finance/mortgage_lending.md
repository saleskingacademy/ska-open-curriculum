# Mortgage Lending

> The course assumes basic knowledge of accounting and finance principles, but applies them to real situations and methods in mortgage lending.

## Products

Conventional (conforming — Fannie/Freddie limits), FHA (3.5% down, MIP), VA (0% down, funding fee), USDA (rural), Jumbo (non-conforming). Fixed rate vs ARM (adjustment period, caps, index+margin). PROCESS: Pre-qualification, pre-approval, application (1003/URLA), processing, underwriting, closing. QUALIFICATION: DTI (front-end 28%, back-end 36% conventional), LTV, credit score thresholds (620 conventional, 580 FHA). Residual income (VA). DOCUMENTS: Income (W-2, tax returns, pay stubs), assets (bank statements), employment verification, title search, appraisal. REGULATION: TILA (Truth in Lending, Reg Z), RESPA (Real Estate Settlement Procedures, Reg X), HMDA, ECOA (fair lending), TRID (Loan Estimate/Closing Disclosure). SECONDARY MARKET: MBS, GSEs (Fannie Mae, Freddie Mac), Ginnie Mae. Servicing rights (MSR).

## Foundations

In the context of real estate, mortgage lending refers to the process by which a lender provides a loan to a borrower, secured by a lien on a property. A **mortgage** is a debt instrument where the borrower, also known as the **mortgagor**, agrees to repay the loan, plus interest, to the lender, also known as the **mortgagee**. The **collateral** for the loan is the property itself, which can be a residential or commercial real estate. A **lien** is a legal claim or security interest on the property, giving the lender the right to sell the property if the borrower defaults on the loan. **Default** occurs when the borrower fails to meet the terms of the loan, such as missing payments. The **principal** is the initial amount borrowed, while **interest** is the cost of borrowing, expressed as a percentage of the principal. The **amortization schedule** outlines the repayment plan, including the amount and frequency of payments. A **loan-to-value (LTV) ratio** is the percentage of the loan amount compared to the property's value, with higher LTV ratios indicating a higher risk for the lender. Understanding these core definitions and principles is essential for practitioners in the field of mortgage lending.

## Mechanisms

In mortgage lending, the process begins with a potential borrower, typically a homebuyer, applying for a mortgage loan from a lender, such as a bank or mortgage company. The borrower provides financial information, including income, credit history, and savings, which the lender uses to assess creditworthiness. The lender then evaluates the property's value, often through an appraisal, to determine the loan-to-value (LTV) ratio, which is the percentage of the property's value that the loan represents. If the borrower is approved, the lender offers a loan with specific terms, including the interest rate, loan amount, and repayment period. The borrower accepts the loan offer, and the lender disburses the funds, which are then used to purchase the property. The borrower repays the loan, plus interest, through regular installments, typically monthly. The lender holds a lien on the property, giving them the right to foreclose and sell the property if the borrower defaults on the loan. Throughout the process, various third-party services, such as title companies and insurance providers, play critical roles in ensuring the transaction is secure and compliant with relevant laws and regulations. The causal chain is as follows: borrower applies → lender evaluates → lender offers loan → borrower accepts → lender disburses funds → borrower repays loan.

## Methods And Frameworks

In mortgage lending, several methods and frameworks are employed to evaluate creditworthiness and determine loan eligibility. The Debt-to-Income (DTI) ratio method is used to assess a borrower's ability to manage monthly debt payments, including mortgage payments, property taxes, and insurance. The DTI ratio is calculated by dividing total monthly debt payments by gross income. A DTI ratio above 43% may indicate a higher risk of default. The Housing Expense-to-Income (HEI) ratio method is used to evaluate the proportion of gross income allocated to housing expenses, including mortgage payments, property taxes, and insurance. The HEI ratio is calculated by dividing housing expenses by gross income. A HEI ratio above 30% may indicate a higher risk of default. The Loan-to-Value (LTV) ratio method is used to assess the risk of lending by dividing the loan amount by the property's value. A higher LTV ratio increases the risk of default. The failure mode of these methods is that they do not account for individual circumstances, such as credit history and employment stability. The Gross Income Multiplier (GIM) method is used to estimate the maximum loan amount based on gross income. The GIM is calculated by multiplying gross income by a factor, typically 2-3. The failure mode of this method is that it does not account for debt obligations and other financial factors. The Present Value (PV) formula is used to calculate the present value of future cash flows, such as mortgage payments. The PV formula is PV = CF / (1 + r)^n, where CF is the cash flow, r is the discount rate, and n is the number of periods. The failure mode of this formula is that it assumes a constant discount rate and does not account for uncertainty and risk.

## Applications

In real estate, mortgage lending is a crucial aspect of facilitating property transactions. Lenders use various applications to assess creditworthiness, determine loan eligibility, and manage risk. The debt-to-income ratio, loan-to-value ratio, and credit score are key factors considered in the mortgage lending process. Lenders also use automated underwriting systems to streamline the application process and reduce manual errors. Additionally, mortgage lenders employ various loan products, such as fixed-rate and adjustable-rate mortgages, government-backed loans (e.g., FHA, VA), and jumbo loans, to cater to different borrower needs and risk profiles. The application of mortgage lending principles is also evident in the use of mortgage insurance, which protects lenders against default risk, and in the implementation of regulatory requirements, such as the Ability-to-Repay rule, which ensures borrowers have a reasonable ability to repay their loans. Furthermore, mortgage lenders utilize property valuation methods, including appraisals and automated valuation models, to determine the value of the collateral and ensure that the loan amount does not exceed a certain percentage of the property's value. By applying these principles and tools, mortgage lenders can effectively manage risk, provide affordable financing options, and facilitate the acquisition and ownership of real estate properties.

## Common Errors

In mortgage lending, practitioners often make mistakes that can have significant consequences for borrowers and lenders. One common error is miscalculating debt-to-income ratios, which can lead to overextending borrowers and increasing the risk of default. This mistake can occur when lenders fail to account for all of a borrower's monthly debt obligations, such as credit card payments, student loans, and other financial commitments. Another error is neglecting to properly verify borrower income and employment, which can result in lending to unqualified borrowers. Additionally, some lenders may incorrectly assess property values, leading to inadequate collateral and increased risk of loss. These mistakes can be attributed to inadequate training, insufficient due diligence, and failure to adhere to regulatory guidelines. Furthermore, lenders may also fail to properly disclose loan terms, including interest rates, fees, and repayment conditions, which can lead to borrower confusion and potential disputes. These errors can be avoided by implementing robust underwriting procedures, conducting thorough risk assessments, and ensuring compliance with relevant regulations and industry standards.

## Foundations

The term "icp" typically refers to Intracranial Pressure, which is the pressure exerted by the brain, cerebrospinal fluid (CSF), and blood within the cranial vault. A fundamental concept in neurophysiology, icp is measured in millimeters of mercury (mmHg) and is crucial for maintaining proper cerebral perfusion pressure (CPP), defined as the net pressure gradient causing blood to flow to the brain, calculated as mean arterial pressure (MAP) minus icp. The Monro-Kellie doctrine, a principle in neuroscience, states that the sum of volumes of brain, CSF, and intracranial blood is constant, implying that an increase in one component must be compensated by a decrease in another to maintain equilibrium and prevent increased icp. Understanding icp is essential for diagnosing and managing conditions such as hydrocephalus, traumatic brain injury, and cerebral edema, where altered icp can lead to severe neurological consequences.

The brain's blood volume is regulated by cerebral autoregulation, which maintains a constant blood flow over a wide range of blood pressure values. When ICP increases, it compresses the cerebral blood vessels, reducing blood volume and subsequently decreasing ICP. Conversely, when ICP decreases, the blood vessels dilate, increasing blood volume and ICP. ## Common Errors

The mistakes practitioners actually make when defining Intracranial Pressure (ICP) often stem from a lack of understanding of its fundamental principles. One common error is confusing ICP with cerebral perfusion pressure (CPP), which is calculated as the difference between mean arterial pressure (MAP) and ICP. This mistake can lead to incorrect management of patients with head injuries or other conditions affecting ICP. Another error is assuming that ICP is solely determined by the volume of cerebrospinal fluid (CSF), blood, and brain tissue within the cranial vault, neglecting the role of other factors such as cerebral blood volume, CSF production and absorption rates, and the pressure-volume index. Additionally, some practitioners may incorrectly assume that elevated ICP is always indicative of a mass lesion, when in fact it can also result from other causes such as cerebral edema, hydrocephalus, or idiopathic intracranial hypertension. These errors can have significant consequences for patient care and highlight the importance of a thorough understanding of ICP definition and its underlying physiology.

## Measurable learning outcomes

On completion, a learner should be able to (1) convert between fractions, decimals, and percentages; (2) distinguish markup from margin; (3) calculate revenue, variable cost, contribution margin, and operating profit; (4) construct a dated cash-flow schedule; (5) explain why profit and cash are not interchangeable; and (6) solve and check break-even problems with explicit units.

## Lesson 1 — Rates and percentages in business

A percentage means *per hundred*. The number 12% is 12/100 = 0.12. To find 12% of $250, multiply 250 × 0.12 = $30. To increase $250 by 12%, add $30: $280. To decrease $250 by 12%, subtract $30: $220.

**Important distinction:** An increase followed by an equal percentage decrease does not restore the original amount. A price of $100 raised by 20% becomes $120. Reducing $120 by 20% produces $96, because the second percentage is applied to a different base.

**Worked example — sales tax:** An item costs $80 before a hypothetical 7.5% tax. Tax = 80 × 0.075 = $6. Total = $86. This is arithmetic practice, not a statement of the tax rate applicable to any jurisdiction.

**Guided practice:** (a) Find 8% of $425. (b) Increase $425 by 8%. (c) Explain why multiplying by 1.08 is equivalent to adding 8%. (d) A price increases from $50 to $65: compute the percentage increase using the original price as the denominator.

## Lesson 2 — Markup is not margin

Let **C** be unit cost and **P** be selling price, with P > 0 and C ≥ 0.

- Dollar gross profit per unit = P − C.
- Markup on cost = (P − C) / C, provided C > 0.
- Gross margin on sales = (P − C) / P.

A product costs $60 and sells for $100. Dollar gross profit is $40. Markup is 40/60 = 66.67%; gross margin is 40/100 = 40%. The percentages differ because their denominators differ.

**Reverse problem:** To achieve a target gross margin of 35% on a product costing $78, solve (P − 78)/P = 0.35. Then P − 78 = 0.35P, so 0.65P = 78 and P = $120. Verify: (120 − 78)/120 = 0.35.

**Common failure:** Pricing a $78 item at $78 × 1.35 = $105.30 produces a 35% markup, not a 35% margin.

**Guided practice:** A product costs $45 and sells for $75. Find dollar profit, markup, and gross margin. Then find the price needed for a 25% margin if cost remains $45.

## Lesson 3 — Contribution margin and break-even

A business may incur fixed costs regardless of how many units it sells, such as a hypothetical $1,800 monthly facility expense. Variable costs rise with units sold. Let:

- P = selling price per unit;
- V = variable cost per unit;
- F = fixed cost for the period;
- Q = units sold during the period.

Revenue = P × Q. Variable cost = V × Q. Contribution margin per unit = P − V. Operating profit in this simplified model is **Q(P − V) − F**. This model excludes taxes, financing, and other nonmodeled costs.

**Worked example:** P = $50, V = $20, F = $1,800. Contribution per unit = $30. At 40 units, operating profit = 40 × 30 − 1,800 = −$600. At 60 units, operating profit = $0. At 75 units, operating profit = 75 × 30 − 1,800 = $450.

Break-even units = F/(P − V) = 1,800/30 = 60 units. When fractional units result, round *up* to the next whole unit if products cannot be sold fractionally.

**Failure conditions:** If P ≤ V, the contribution per unit is zero or negative and no positive sales quantity can cover positive fixed costs in this simplified model. Do not divide by zero or report a plausible-looking break-even number.

**Guided practice:** For P = $85, V = $34, F = $2,500, calculate break-even units and operating profit at 55 units. Show all intermediate steps.

## Lesson 4 — Cash flow is not profit

Revenue can be recognized before a customer pays; expenses can be incurred before payment. Therefore a business can report accounting profit while having insufficient cash to pay immediate bills.

**Worked example — cash schedule:** Opening cash on Day 1 is $1,000. A customer is invoiced $2,000 on Day 1, but payment is expected on Day 30. On Day 5 the business pays $700 for supplies; on Day 10 it pays $600 in rent. Cash on Day 10 is $1,000 − $700 − $600 = −$300, assuming no other financing or receipts. The customer invoice does not prevent the shortfall. If the business cannot overdraw, it needs at least $300 in additional available liquidity before those payments.

| Date | Cash event | Change | Running cash |
|---|---|---:|---:|
| Day 1 | Opening balance | — | $1,000 |
| Day 1 | Invoice issued; unpaid | $0 | $1,000 |
| Day 5 | Supplies paid | −$700 | $300 |
| Day 10 | Rent paid | −$600 | −$300 |
| Day 30 | Customer payment received, if paid in full | +$2,000 | $1,700 |

The schedule assumes payment on Day 30; real customers may pay late or not at all. A cash forecast should model those risks.

**Guided practice:** Recalculate the running balance if only $1,200 arrives on Day 30. Identify the first date of negative cash and the maximum deficit.

## Lesson 5 — Integrated case study

A small business plans to sell 90 units in a month at $75 each. Variable cost is $30 per unit, fixed operating costs are $2,400, and only 60% of customer invoices are collected during that month. Assume all units are sold and invoiced, all variable and fixed costs are paid in that month, and opening cash is $1,000. Ignore taxes, debt, and depreciation for this exercise.

**Task A — profitability:** Calculate sales revenue, total variable cost, total contribution, and simplified operating profit.

**Task B — liquidity:** Calculate cash receipts from current-month sales, total cash operating payments, and closing cash. State whether the business can cover those payments without additional funding.

**Task C — sensitivity:** Recalculate operating profit if volume falls to 60 units. Explain which costs change and which remain fixed.

**Task D — communication:** Write a 100–150 word recommendation that distinguishes profitability, cash liquidity, and the assumptions underlying both calculations.

## Assessment

1. Convert 0.085 to a percentage and 14% to a decimal.
2. Explain the difference between a 30% markup and a 30% gross margin.
3. A unit costs $24. Find the selling price that produces a 40% gross margin.
4. A service sells for $120, incurs $45 variable cost, and has $3,000 fixed costs. Find whole-unit break-even volume.
5. Explain in two sentences why an unpaid invoice is not available cash.
6. Construct a three-event cash-flow schedule with an opening balance of $500, a $350 payment, and a $200 receipt.
7. For the integrated case study, compute the simplified operating profit and closing cash, showing formulas.
8. Identify two assumptions that could cause the cash forecast to be wrong.

**Suggested scoring:** 1 point each for questions 1–6 and 2 points each for questions 7–8 (10 points total). Mastery threshold for this supplemental unit: 8/10 *and* correct calculations on questions 4 and 7. The instructor or agent should provide targeted feedback and an equivalent retest, not simply repeat the answer.

## Answer key and worked feedback

1. 8.5%; 0.14.
2. Markup divides unit profit by cost; margin divides unit profit by selling price.
3. (P − 24)/P = 0.40; 0.60P = 24; **P = $40**.
4. Contribution = 120 − 45 = $75; break-even = 3,000/75 = **40 services**.
5. The invoice represents a claim for payment, not money received. Immediate obligations must be paid from available cash or financing.
6. Opening $500; after payment $150; after receipt **$350**.
7. Revenue = 90 × 75 = $6,750; variable cost = 90 × 30 = $2,700; contribution = $4,050; operating profit = 4,050 − 2,400 = **$1,650**. Current-month receipts = 0.60 × 6,750 = $4,050. Cash payments = 2,700 + 2,400 = $5,100. Closing cash = 1,000 + 4,050 − 5,100 = **−$50**. The business is profitable under the simplified accounting model but has a $50 projected liquidity deficit.
8. Examples: customers may pay late; unit sales may differ; costs may rise; taxes, debt service, and inventory timing are omitted.

**Case study sensitivity answer:** At 60 units, revenue = $4,500; variable cost = $1,800; contribution = $2,700; operating profit = **$300**. Fixed cost remains $2,400 in the stated relevant range.

## Remediation and extension

- If percentage calculations are incorrect, return to fraction/decimal conversion and redo two problems with different bases.
- If markup and margin are confused, label the denominator before every calculation.
- If break-even is incorrect, first calculate contribution per unit, then divide fixed costs by contribution.
- If cash and profit are confused, place every transaction on a timeline showing invoice date, recognition assumptions, and actual payment date.
- Extension: build a spreadsheet with editable P, V, F, Q, collection rate, and opening cash. Check the spreadsheet against the manual solutions before using it for a real decision.

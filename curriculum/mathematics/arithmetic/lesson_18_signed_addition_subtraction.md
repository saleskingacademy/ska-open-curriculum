## Entry check and outcomes

Order −5, 0 and 3 on a number line. State the distance of −5 from zero. Find 12 − 7. Responses: −5 < 0 < 3; five; five. Distinguish a signed position from a nonnegative distance. Review the number-line and negative-number lessons if a more negative value is incorrectly treated as larger.

By the end, add integers with like and unlike signs, subtract an integer by adding its opposite, distinguish unary signs from binary operations, model signed changes and check results with inverse operations. Multiplication and division sign rules are not prerequisites and are not taught by a slogan here.

## Vocabulary and first principles

The **integers** are whole-number positions extending in both directions from zero. The **opposite** of a number is equally far from zero on the other side; zero is its own opposite. The **absolute value** |a| is the distance from a to zero and is never negative. A **signed change** describes both size and direction.

In −7, the minus sign indicates the negative number seven units below zero. In 4 − 7, minus is a subtraction operation between two quantities. Parentheses help: 4 − (−7) means subtract negative seven, whereas 4 + (−7) means add negative seven. Read the operation and the operand sign separately.

On a number line, adding a positive integer moves right and adding a negative integer moves left. Starting at −3 and adding five lands at two; starting at three and adding negative five lands at −2. The destination depends on both starting state and change. A leftward change is not automatically a negative destination.

## Deriving the rules with zero pairs

Use one positive counter for +1 and one negative counter for −1. A positive-negative pair has net value zero. Adding or removing such a pair changes the representation but not the net value. Five positive counters and three negative counters can cancel three zero pairs, leaving two positive counters. Thus 5 + (−3) = 2.

For like signs, all counters point in the same direction: combine their magnitudes and retain that sign. For unlike signs, cancel opposite pairs; the remaining magnitude is the difference of the magnitudes and its sign comes from the larger magnitude. Equal opposite magnitudes leave zero, which is neither positive nor negative.

Subtraction undoes addition. If x = a − b, the required check is x + b = a. Adding the opposite supplies such an x: (a + (−b)) + b = a + 0 = a. Therefore a − b = a + (−b). This derivation explains why subtracting a negative can increase a result; it does not depend on chanting “two negatives make a positive” regardless of context.

## Worked examples

**E1 — two negative changes.** −8 + (−5) = −13. Eight leftward units followed by five more leftward units is thirteen leftward units. The two minus signs do not turn this addition positive.

**E2 — unlike signs, positive result.** −7 + 12 = 5. Seven opposite pairs cancel, leaving five positives. Check 5 − 12 = −7.

**E3 — unlike signs, negative result.** 9 + (−14) = −5. Cancel nine pairs, leaving five negatives. The starting positive state does not force a positive answer.

**E4 — subtracting a positive.** −6 − 4 = −6 + (−4) = −10. Check −10 + 4 = −6.

**E5 — subtracting a negative.** −6 − (−4) = −6 + 4 = −2. Check −2 + (−4) = −6. Removing four negative counters from six negatives leaves two negatives; an increase need not cross zero.

**E6 — represent a missing negative counter.** For 3 − (−2), start with three positives. Add two zero pairs, giving five positives and two negatives, still net three. Remove the two negatives; five positives remain. Result five. Check 5 + (−2) = 3.

**E7 — signed difference versus distance.** A temperature changes from −4 degrees to 3 degrees on the same scale. Final minus initial is 3 − (−4) = +7 degrees, an increase. Reversing the subtraction gives −7, the change in the opposite direction. The distance between the readings is seven degrees either way. Temperature difference is a change, not a new absolute temperature reading.

## Guided practice

G1. −9 + 4. **Solution:** Cancel four opposite pairs, leaving −5.

G2. 6 − (−8). **Solution:** Add the opposite of −8: 6 + 8 = 14. Verify 14 + (−8) = 6.

G3. −5 + 5. **Solution:** Every counter belongs to a zero pair; result zero.

G4. Is −12 less than −3? **Solution:** Yes. A greater magnitude on the negative side means farther left, not greater signed value.

## Transfer and misconceptions

T1. A hypothetical ledger has net balance −35 units. It receives a +48 adjustment and then a −19 adjustment. Find the final balance. **Solution:** −35 + 48 = 13, then 13 + (−19) = −6 units. These are signed adjustments; the example does not report real money or authorize a transaction.

T2. An elevation changes from −12 units to −5 units relative to the same reference. **Solution:** Change is −5 − (−12) = +7 units; it rises even though both positions are negative. A claim of a seventeen-unit fall confuses magnitudes with a signed difference.

T3. Diagnose “subtracting always makes a number smaller.” **Solution:** Subtracting a positive lowers the value; subtracting zero preserves it; subtracting a negative raises it. Counterexample: 2 − (−3) = 5. State operand conditions rather than applying a whole-number removal rule beyond its scope.

Zero has one integer value. A display format that prints a signed zero does not create two different mathematical integers. Absolute value is not a command to remove every minus sign from an expression: |−3 − 5| is |−8| = 8, whereas 3 − 5 is −2. Find the enclosed value first.

## Computational and AI verification

For a proposed answer x to a − b, check x + b = a with the original signed b. For −9 − (−4), x = −5 passes because −5 + (−4) = −9. Adding positive four instead checks the wrong relation. Record the sign in the input data rather than relying on visual memory.

Audit this constructed AI explanation: “−8 + (−3) equals +11 because two negatives cancel.” The result is −11. The model combines eleven negative counters; cancellation requires opposite signs, not two identical signs. Ask for the operation-specific derivation. A calculator check can confirm a computation but cannot replace interpretation of what a negative business or measurement quantity means.

Later programming lessons distinguish unbounded mathematical integers from fixed-width machine representations. This lesson's integer laws do not authorize assuming every software numeric type stores arbitrarily large exact values. No hardware execution or performance claim is made here.

## Quiz, alternate form and mastery

Each item is worth two points, one for the answer and one for a valid model or inverse check.

Q1. −10 + 6. Q2. −4 + (−7). Q3. 5 − (−9). Q4. −8 − (−3). Q5. Find the signed change from −7 to 2.

Answers: −4; −11; 14; −5; +9, because final minus initial is 2 − (−7).

Alternate form: R1 −13 + 8; R2 −6 + (−8); R3 7 − (−4); R4 −11 − (−5); R5 signed change from −9 to 3. Answers: −5; −14; 11; −6; +12. The same explanation rubric applies.

Require nine of ten independent answers correct, 8/10 quiz points, and a correct new signed-change problem. Correct every operation/sign confusion. If cancellation fails, use counters; if direction fails, use a number line; if inverse checking fails, label the original subtrahend before adding it back. Retrieve: distinguish sign from operation, add the opposite, cancel zero pairs and verify the original relation.

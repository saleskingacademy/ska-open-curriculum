## Entry check and learning outcomes

Explain why 7 + 6 = 13 supports 13 − 7 = 6. Find 9 − 0 and 9 − 9. State whether 5 − 9 has a nonnegative whole-number answer. Entry responses: addition recomposes the whole; nine; zero; no. Return to Lesson 15 if a subtraction answer is treated as independent of its part-whole relationship.

By the end, reconstruct basic subtraction facts from addition, select among counting up, counting back, bridging ten and doubles, explain why each strategy works, and detect off-by-one and invalid decomposition errors. Fluency means accurate, flexible use of facts; an arbitrary speed target is not proof of understanding.

## Vocabulary and scope

A **fact family** connects addition and subtraction equations for the same whole and parts. A **complement** here is the missing part needed to reach a specified whole. A **double** combines two equal addends. **Bridging ten** uses ten as an intermediate quantity without changing the total removal. **Retrieval** recalls a known fact; **reconstruction** derives it when recall is uncertain.

This lesson concentrates on totals from zero through eighteen with nonnegative differences, especially facts arising from two single-digit addends. Not every subtraction with a total at most eighteen has two single-digit parts: 18 − 1 = 17 is within the total range but outside that single-digit-pair subset. The distinction matters when describing practice coverage.

## First principles and derivation

If six plus seven is thirteen, thirteen separates into parts six and seven. Removing six leaves seven; removing seven leaves six. Thus 6 + 7 = 13, 7 + 6 = 13, 13 − 6 = 7 and 13 − 7 = 6 describe one part-whole relationship. They do not say subtraction permits arbitrary reversal. Seven minus thirteen belongs to a different question.

For equal parts, there are fewer distinct equations: 6 + 6 = 12 and 12 − 6 = 6. For a zero part, 0 + 8 = 8 supports 8 − 0 = 8 and 8 − 8 = 0. Fact families include these cases without requiring four different statements every time.

Counting up asks what must be added to the subtrahend to reach the minuend. Counting back removes units from the minuend. Both track the same missing part. Choose the route with fewer or clearer steps, but do not confuse the number of endpoint labels with the number of movements.

To bridge ten in 14 − 6, partition the six removed units into four and two. Removing four reaches ten; removing the remaining two reaches eight. This is (14 − 4) − 2, not 14 − (4 − 2). The first removes six altogether. The second would remove only two, a different problem. Partition the removal using addition: six equals four plus two.

## Worked examples

**E1 — related addition.** Find 15 − 8. Recall or reconstruct 8 + 7 = 15. Therefore the missing part is seven. Check eight plus seven independently by reaching ten: eight plus two plus five is fifteen.

**E2 — count up across ten.** Find 13 − 9. Nine to ten takes one step; ten to thirteen takes three. Total steps one plus three equals four. Check nine plus four equals thirteen.

**E3 — count back a small removal.** Find 12 − 2. One backward step reaches eleven; the second reaches ten. The starting mark twelve is not a step. Check ten plus two equals twelve.

**E4 — bridge ten.** Find 16 − 7. Seven is six plus one. Remove six to reach ten, then one to reach nine. Check nine plus seven equals sixteen. Both parts of the removal were included once.

**E5 — doubles and near doubles.** Since seven plus seven is fourteen, 14 − 7 = 7. Increasing the whole to fifteen while keeping the removed part seven makes the remaining part eight: 15 − 7 = 8. Verify seven plus eight equals fifteen rather than accepting the pattern without a check.

**E6 — compare strategies.** Find 17 − 9. Counting up from nine to ten takes one, then to seventeen takes seven, making eight. Alternatively remove seven to reach ten and then two to reach eight. The partitions are different: the first partitions the distance eight, the second partitions the removal nine. Both yield eight for different explicit reasons.

**E7 — hypothetical allocation.** Eighteen sample kits are ready and nine are allocated. Nine remain, because nine plus nine equals eighteen. This is a doubles fact applied to distinct kits. It is not evidence about a live business or a learner's performance.

## Guided practice

G1. 11 − 8. **Response:** Eight to ten takes two and ten to eleven one, so the difference is three.

G2. 14 − 9. **Response:** Nine to ten takes one, then four more to fourteen; five in all.

G3. 13 − 6. **Response:** Remove three to reach ten, then the remaining three to reach seven. Six plus seven checks thirteen.

G4. A learner gives 16 − 8 = 9 by counting eight as the first step. **Response:** Starting at eight is zero steps toward sixteen. Eight steps reach sixteen, and eight plus eight is sixteen. Record intervals explicitly.

## Transfer and common failure modes

T1. Twelve objects are available. Two are removed, then three more. Find the remainder and explain why 12 − (2 − 3) is not the model. **Solution:** Removing five altogether leaves seven: (12 − 2) − 3 = 7. The parenthesized expression describes a difference between the removal counts, not their combined effect; signed interpretation is outside this lesson's calculation scope.

T2. A learner writes 15 − 8 as 15 − 5 − 2 and reports eight. **Solution:** Five plus two removes only seven. Split eight as five plus three; fifteen minus five is ten, then minus three is seven. Check eight plus seven equals fifteen. Both an incorrect partition and an incorrect final claim need diagnosis.

T3. In 14 − 6 and 15 − 7, both answers are eight. Is every subtraction unchanged when one is added to both inputs? **Solution:** In the stated whole-number domain, yes: if six plus eight equals fourteen, then seven plus eight equals fifteen. In general, b + d = a implies (b + 1) + d = a + 1. Explain the preserved difference. Contrast with adding one to both addends in addition, which increases their sum by two.

T4. A learner remembers 16 − 9 as eight. Give two checks. **Solution:** Nine plus eight is seventeen, so eight is too large. Count up: one from nine to ten plus six from ten to sixteen gives seven. Use the failed check to correct the fact instead of rehearsing the wrong answer faster.

A memorized response without a meaning check can persist as an error. Counting back is valid but can be cumbersome for a large removal. Related addition often reduces the burden. No strategy removes the need to track the whole and the removed amount. Timed drills should not replace explanation, error correction or accessible forms of demonstration.

## Computational and AI-augmented practice

Build a small fact table by choosing whole a from zero through eighteen and removed amount b from zero through a. Enter a proposed difference and verify b + difference = a. There are 190 ordered input pairs: one for a = 0, two for a = 1, and so on through nineteen for a = 18. Adding those row lengths gives 190. This table includes the broader total-at-most-eighteen domain, not only the single-digit-pair subset.

A finite checker can exhaustively verify this finite table, but not every possible integer subtraction. A claim about all whole numbers requires reasoning beyond the table. Later formal verification will make that distinction precise. Here, keep test scope explicit.

Constructed AI output: “Every subtraction fact has four distinct equations, so 8 − 4 = 4 proves four different facts.” Diagnose the overstatement. Equal parts duplicate the addition order and the subtraction relation: four plus four is eight, and eight minus four is four. There are two distinct equations in this family. Ask the tool to list them before accepting its count.

Practice with assistance only after an independent attempt. Record the initial answer, explanation, tool suggestion and final correction. The learning evidence is the learner's justified correction, not the machine's fluent wording. Do not enter personal learner data into a public example file.

## Formative quiz and alternate reassessment

Score each item out of two: one point for result or judgment, one for a valid derivation or inverse check.

Q1. 11 − 7.
Q2. 16 − 9.
Q3. 12 − 6.
Q4. 10 − 0.
Q5. Diagnose 14 − 8 = (14 − 4) − 2.

Answers: Q1 four, since seven plus four equals eleven. Q2 seven, by one to ten plus six. Q3 six, a doubles fact. Q4 ten, since nothing is removed. Q5 the right side removes six rather than eight and equals eight; correct bridging removes four and then four, yielding six.

Alternate form: R1 12 − 8; R2 17 − 9; R3 14 − 7; R4 0 − 0; R5 diagnose 15 − 9 = (15 − 5) − 3. Answers: four; eight; seven; zero; the proposed partition removes eight, yielding seven, whereas the correct partition five plus four yields six. Use the same explanation rubric.

## Mastery, remediation and retrieval

Require nine of ten independent answers correct, at least 8/10 quiz points, and a correct explanation of one new counting-up, one bridging-ten and one zero-boundary item. All invalid partitions must be corrected. Routine accuracy must meet the course's 85% threshold; the ten-item set rounds this to nine correct. Transfer tasks require at least 80% of assigned rubric points and correction of critical reasoning errors.

For each missed fact, rebuild the whole and two parts with counters, write the related addition, and explain a second route. Space later practice across different days and mix facts so the method is selected from the numbers rather than from a fixed worksheet pattern. Learners may speak explanations, use a number line or write equations; no mandatory speed cutoff is imposed here.

Retrieval cues: use the related sum; count movements; partition the entire removal; retain the whole; check zero cases. After mastery, Lesson 17 applies these facts to place-value regrouping in multi-digit subtraction. This lesson alone does not teach that algorithm or complete Level 2.

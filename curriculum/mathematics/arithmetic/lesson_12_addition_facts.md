# Lesson 12: Addition facts

Prerequisites: [Lesson 11](lesson_11_meaning_of_addition.md), counting and place value from the foundation lessons.

## Entry check and objectives

Use objects or counting on to show 7 + 3 = 10. Explain why 3 + 7 gives the same total. Break a collection of eight into two smaller disjoint groups and count each. If you cannot justify these tasks, strengthen addition meaning before working on speed.

An **addition fact** is a small sum worth knowing reliably because it recurs in larger calculations. By the end, derive and use facts with addends zero through nine; count on from the larger addend; use doubles, near doubles, and making ten; explain why these strategies preserve the sum; choose an efficient strategy without forcing it; and verify a recalled fact when uncertain. Fluency means accurate, understandable and increasingly efficient use, not a fixed timed-test speed.

## Vocabulary, notation, and prerequisite-safe language

A **double** uses two equal addends, as in 6 + 6. A **near double** has addends one apart, as in 6 + 7. A **complement to ten** is a whole count which completes another to ten: four complements six. **Decomposition** breaks one contribution into disjoint smaller contributions, such as 7 = 2 + 5. **Strategy** is a deliberate route to an answer. **Recall** means retrieving a known fact; **derivation** means reconstructing it from justified facts or a model.

“Make ten” means regroup contributions to form a group of ten, not change the original total. In 8 + 7 = 8 + (2 + 5) = (8 + 2) + 5 = 15, seven is decomposed into two and five, then grouped with eight. Each equality keeps the same objects and quantity. Parentheses make the grouping visible. This uses the addition laws already justified in Lesson 11.

## First principles: a connected network of facts

Facts are not isolated answers detached from quantities. If you know 6 + 6 = 12 by pairing two groups of six, one extra object produces 6 + 7 = 13. If you know 9 + 1 = 10, then 9 + 4 can be regrouped into ten and three, giving thirteen. A connected network helps repair forgotten facts and detect incorrect recall.

Start with zero facts and one-more facts, then pairs that make ten and doubles. These can be modeled directly with counters or a ten-frame: two rows of five spaces. Filling eight spaces leaves two empty. Thus eight needs two to complete ten. A ten-frame makes the complement visible rather than asking you to trust a chant.

A grid of facts has ten row addends and ten column addends from zero through nine. The result at row a and column b is a + b. Mirror positions across the diagonal agree by commutativity. Diagonal positions are doubles. Adjacent entries in a row rise by one because the second contribution increases by one object. These patterns are consequences of addition, not shortcuts that invent a new rule.

## Conceptual and formal derivations

**Counting on from the larger addend:** For 2 + 8, commute to 8 + 2, then say nine, ten. This uses two increments instead of eight. The final result is unchanged because the joined collection is unchanged. It is useful when one addend is small.

**Doubles:** Pair two equal-sized groups. Five and five make ten; six and six make twelve. A known double can be checked as two matching collections, so an uncertain recalled value can be reconstructed.

**Near doubles:** Because 7 = 6 + 1, 6 + 7 = 6 + (6 + 1) = (6 + 6) + 1 = 13. The “one more” adjustment belongs to the addend, so it must also appear in the sum. A learner who reports twelve has ignored the extra object.

**Make ten:** If the first addend is eight, take its complement two from the other addend. When the other addend is seven, the remaining part is five because seven can be counted as two and five. Then ten and five make fifteen. This strategy helps with sums that cross a ten boundary.

These are equivalent derivations for the same operation. No rule says that every fact must use a particular method. A sound strategy may be chosen by the learner's reliable known facts, and an unfamiliar sum can always be checked with counting or objects.

## Worked examples and reasoning

**Example 1 — small addend.** For 3 + 9, start at nine by commutativity. Three new counts are ten, eleven, twelve. Result twelve. Verify with nine counters and three additional counters. Starting at three would be correct but require more steps.

**Example 2 — zero.** For 0 + 7, an empty first collection joins seven objects, giving seven. This is a fact and an instance of a law. Memorizing it without its meaning can lead to an incorrect assumption that every operation involving zero returns the other number.

**Example 3 — double.** Arrange six counters in one row and another six below. Counting all gives twelve: 6 + 6 = 12. Pairing them makes the equal contributions visible. The numeral twelve represents one ten and two ones, connecting the fact to place value.

**Example 4 — near double.** For 7 + 8, use 7 + 7 = 14, then one extra object gives fifteen. Write 7 + 8 = 7 + (7 + 1) = 14 + 1 = 15. Do not add the extra one twice. Check by making ten: seven needs three of the eight; five remain, so ten plus five is fifteen.

**Example 5 — make ten.** For 9 + 6, decompose six into one and five. Nine plus one makes ten; five remain. Result fifteen. Verify that one and five still total six, and that no original object disappeared during regrouping.

**Example 6 — two routes.** For 8 + 5, one route counts nine, ten, eleven, twelve, thirteen. Another decomposes five as two and three, giving ten plus three. Both yield thirteen. If one route produced twelve, recounting or checking the decomposition would expose the error.

**Example 7 — pattern and its limit.** Knowing 4 + 5 = 9 gives 4 + 6 = 10 because the second addend gained one. But changing both addends, from 4 + 5 to 5 + 6, adds two objects, so the new sum is eleven. A pattern must track every change, not merely notice that digits moved.

## Guided practice

G1. Derive 4 + 9 using a small-addend or make-ten route. **Response:** Nine plus four counts to thirteen; alternatively take one from four for ten, with three left.

G2. Derive 5 + 6 from a double. **Response:** Five plus five is ten, then one more gives eleven.

G3. Derive 7 + 6 by making ten. **Response:** Seven needs three; six is three and three; ten plus the remaining three gives thirteen.

G4. A learner says 8 + 7 = 10 + 7 = 17 after making ten from eight. **Diagnosis:** Two objects were added without being removed from the other contribution. Correct seven's decomposition as two plus five, giving ten plus five, fifteen.

### Solutions and explanatory routes

P1. 13; nine plus one makes ten, three of the four remain.
P2. 12; two equal groups of six or six increments from six.
P3. 13; known double 6 + 6 = 12, one extra.
P4. 14; eight needs two of the six, four remain after forming ten.
P5. 12; seven needs three of five, leaving two with ten.
P6. 9; empty contribution changes nothing.
P7. 14; only the second addend increased by one, so the sum increases by one.
P8. The first sum is 12, the second 14. Each addend increased by one, adding two to the total.
P9. Two extra objects were introduced. Five is two and three, so 8 + 5 = (8 + 2) + 3 = 13.
P10. Near-double: 7 + 7 = 14, then one extra gives 15. Make-ten: seven plus three from eight forms ten, with five left, giving 15. Both explain why fourteen is one too small.

## Applied and multi-step problems

A1. Nine learner cards are on a table. A staff member adds six different cards and another adds four. What is the final count? Choose a grouping and verify it another way. **Solution:** (9 + 6) + 4 = 15 + 4 = 19; alternatively 6 + 4 = 10, then 9 + 10 = 19. The contributions are distinct; otherwise the count of different cards would need overlap checks.

A2. A box has eight black markers and seven white markers, with no marker in both groups. A learner says the box needs fourteen individual spaces. Is that sufficient? **Solution:** Total fifteen by a near double or making ten. Fourteen spaces are one too few. An estimate near fifteen is not a substitute for the exact capacity decision.

A3. You know 8 + 8 = 16. An arrangement instead has eight objects in one group and nine in the other. State the new total, then explain why merely chanting the double can fail. **Solution:** Seventeen, because one contribution increased by one. Facts must match the input actually present.

## Misconceptions, edge cases, and verification

Speed does not excuse an incorrect fact. If recall is unreliable, derive and check before practicing retrieval. Practice only verified facts; repeating a mistaken result can strengthen the wrong association. Avoid making-ten stories that create or discard objects unnoticed.

Zero-addend facts are legitimate and should not be omitted. The largest sum with both addends from zero through nine is eighteen, from 9 + 9. A value above eighteen cannot be a correct sum in this particular domain. This bound can detect some errors, but not an incorrect answer of sixteen for 8 + 7, because sixteen is within the domain's possible range. Bounds screen results; they do not identify every sum.

Error-diagnosis exercise: “Changing 6 + 7 to 7 + 8 adds one to the answer.” Both addends gained one, so the answer rises by two, from thirteen to fifteen. State changes separately before combining them. This anticipates algebraic variation and checking an algorithm's inputs.

## Computational exercise and AI auditing

Design a ten-by-ten fact table on paper. Label rows and columns zero through nine; fill a first row using the zero identity, then derive neighboring row entries by one-more reasoning. Check selected mirrored positions, diagonal doubles and the final 9 + 9 position. Do not copy a generated table before validating its patterns and individual entries.

Optional implementation specification: a later program can loop over each row and column addend and compute their sum. Verification should check all 100 pairs against counted or independently derived expectations, mirror symmetry, each row's one-step increments and zero boundaries. Passing a symmetry check alone is insufficient: a consistently wrong symmetric table could still pass it.

Constructed AI-audit claim: “To calculate 9 + 7, change nine to ten and keep seven, so the sum is seventeen.” The unsupported step increases a contribution without compensation. Repair by moving one from seven to nine: ten plus six, sixteen. Verify by counting on seven steps from nine or joining the collections. This is model and arithmetic verification, not an actual benchmark result.

## Review and quiz

Build retrieval cards in both directions: question on one side, answer and a short derivation on the other. Mix zero, small-addend, double, near-double and make-ten cases. After a mistake, explain the repair before retrying it among other facts. Do not require a single strategy where several are valid.

Key rules: zero preserves the other addend; reversing addends preserves the sum; regrouping preserves the sum when contributions remain unchanged; adding one object to one contribution increases the total by one. Making ten moves objects between groups, not into or out of the combined collection.

Quiz, two points each: one for sum and one for a valid derivation.

Q1. 9 + 5.
Q2. 7 + 7.
Q3. 7 + 8.
Q4. 8 + 4.
Q5. Diagnose “6 + 7 = 12 because six doubled is twelve.”

Answers: Q1 14, one from five completes ten leaving four; Q2 14, two groups of seven; Q3 15, one beyond the double; Q4 12, two from four complete ten leaving two; Q5 seven is one larger than six, so correct sum thirteen. For Q5, one point for identifying the ignored extra object and one for the corrected reasoning/result.

Reassessment: R1 9 + 7; R2 8 + 8; R3 8 + 9; R4 7 + 6. Answers: 16 by ten plus six; 16 by a double; 17 by one beyond that double; 13 by a double plus one or ten plus three. A recalled numeral needs an explanation on this reassessment.

## Mastery and remediation

Require 8/10 quiz points, nine of ten independent tasks correct, and correct solutions to the applied capacity and two-route tasks. Independently calculate a mixed set of ten new facts sampled from addends zero through nine, with at least nine correct and correction of the remaining error. Explain one double, one near double and one make-ten derivation. Do not impose a speed threshold that hides understanding or penalizes accessibility needs.

If facts fail, return to a counted model rather than expanding an unverified drill bank. If decomposition loses objects, use two-colored counters and physically move the complement to ten. If one-more patterns fail, label which addend changed and count the new objects. Reattempt alternate questions after spacing practice. These facts become components of multi-digit addition, later multiplication as repeated equal contributions, and mental arithmetic. Reliable structure makes larger calculations auditable rather than mysterious.

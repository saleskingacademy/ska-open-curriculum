## Entry check and learning outcomes

Find the missing number in 5 + blank = 12, describe the distance from three to eight, and explain why adding zero does not change a count. Responses: seven; five unit intervals; zero contributes nothing. If counting includes both endpoint marks as movements, review the number-line lesson before proceeding.

By the end, model subtraction as removal, comparison and missing-part reasoning; interpret the order of operands; identify the correct whole from a story; attach appropriate units; and verify a difference using addition. This lesson uses nonnegative whole inputs with the first at least as large as the second. Results below zero are acknowledged but their procedures are taught in Lesson 18.

## Vocabulary, notation and first principles

In a − b = d, a is the **minuend**, b the **subtrahend**, and d the **difference**. The labels are optional memory aids; understanding their roles is essential. In the removal model, a is the starting quantity, b is removed, and d remains. In the missing-part model, b and d together compose a. Thus b + d = a checks a − b = d.

Place twelve counters on a table. Separate five into a marked tray. Seven remain outside the tray. The original twelve consist of the removed five and the remaining seven. Therefore 12 − 5 = 7, checked by 5 + 7 = 12. Subtraction answers a question about the same part-whole relationship as addition.

For whole numbers with a at least b, counting forward from b to a identifies a whole-number difference. Each additional step increases the count by one, so two different nonnegative counts cannot both complete the same missing part. This explains why the difference is fixed by the inputs, not chosen by a preferred method.

## Three models, one relationship

Removal describes a change: twelve items were present, five leave, seven remain. Comparison need not describe a change: one shelf contains twelve items and another contains five; the first has seven more. Missing-part reasoning describes composition: a collection contains twelve items, five are marked, and seven are unmarked, provided those two categories are disjoint and exhaustive.

The equation is the same in all three cases, but the interpretation differs. A correct number without the correct unit and relationship is not a complete modeling solution. Before calculating, identify the whole, the known part and the requested part or difference.

Number lines support two equivalent routes in the stated domain. Start at twelve and move five unit intervals left to land at seven; or start at five and count seven intervals to reach twelve. Counting endpoint labels is different from counting intervals. From five to twelve there are eight integer marks including both endpoints, but seven unit intervals.

## Worked examples

**E1 — removal.** Fourteen sample cards are prepared and six are distributed. Eight remain: 14 − 6 = 8. Check six distributed plus eight retained equals fourteen prepared. This assumes no other cards were added, lost or distributed.

**E2 — comparison.** A learner completes sixteen problems and another completes nine. The first completes seven more: 16 − 9 = 7. No problems are physically removed. Check nine plus seven equals sixteen. This count alone says nothing about accuracy or problem difficulty.

**E3 — missing part.** Eighteen seats are occupied; eleven occupants are in group A and all remaining occupants are in group B. Then seven are in group B: 18 − 11 = 7. If group membership overlaps, the inference is not justified without more information. Arithmetic cannot repair an invalid partition.

**E4 — count up.** Find 43 − 38. From thirty-eight to forty is two steps; from forty to forty-three is three. The difference is two plus three, or five. Check 38 + 5 = 43. This example anticipates an efficient method without requiring the multi-digit written subtraction algorithm.

**E5 — comparison language.** Mira has thirteen tokens, which is four fewer than Jo has. Jo has seventeen because thirteen plus four equals seventeen. The word “fewer” appears, but the unknown is the larger quantity. Translate the relation: Jo's amount minus four equals thirteen. Keyword matching alone would wrongly choose thirteen minus four.

**E6 — zero boundaries.** Removing zero from nine leaves nine: 9 − 0 = 9. Removing all nine leaves zero: 9 − 9 = 0. Removing zero from zero leaves zero. The expression 0 − 9 has no nonnegative whole-number result; it is −9 in the integers. It is not silently replaced by zero or nine.

## Guided practice

G1. Eleven lamps are available and four are allocated. How many remain? **Response:** Seven lamps, since four plus seven equals eleven.

G2. Compare collections of seventeen and twelve objects. **Response:** The larger exceeds the smaller by five objects. Check twelve plus five equals seventeen.

G3. A complete set has twenty objects, eight of one type and the rest of another. **Response:** Twelve of the other type, assuming each object belongs to exactly one of those types.

G4. A learner counts the marks 6, 7, 8, 9, 10 and says 10 − 6 = 5. **Response:** There are five marks but four unit intervals; six plus four equals ten. The correct difference is four.

## Operation laws, errors and transfer

Subtraction is not commutative. Twelve minus five is seven, whereas five minus twelve is not seven; in integers it is negative seven. Even when both directions produce whole numbers, equality in both directions occurs for equal inputs, not as a general rearrangement rule. Do not change operand order to make a problem look easier.

Subtraction is not associative. Compare (15 − 6) − 2 = 7 with 15 − (6 − 2) = 11. The first removes six and then two; the second removes the quantity four. Parentheses change what is removed. The addition regrouping rule cannot be transferred without checking its conditions.

T1. A simulation begins with 47 requests, records 19 completed, and contains no cancellations or new arrivals. How many remain? **Solution:** Twenty-eight, verified by nineteen plus twenty-eight equals forty-seven. If new arrivals occurred, that equation would no longer describe the entire state change. State assumptions before trusting a dashboard count.

T2. Of twelve learners, seven selected a mathematics workshop and eight selected a computing workshop. Does 12 − 7 determine how many selected computing? **Solution:** No. Learners may select both, neither, or more than one option. Twelve minus seven gives five who did not select mathematics, assuming seven counts distinct members of the twelve; it does not identify their computing choices. A partition requires mutually exclusive categories covering the whole.

T3. A report claims that six fewer than ten is sixteen. **Solution:** Six fewer than ten is four, checked by six plus four equals ten. Compare this with “ten is six fewer than an unknown,” where the unknown is sixteen. Grammar identifies the relationship; a keyword alone does not.

## Computational and AI-augmented practice

Specify a subtraction checker for this lesson: accept known whole numbers a, b and proposed d; require a at least b and d nonnegative; accept the arithmetic relation only if b + d = a. Check input meaning separately. For a = 31, b = 14 and proposed d = 17, addition returns thirty-one. If d is sixteen, it returns thirty, rejecting the proposal.

This check uses an inverse relationship, not another repetition of the same subtraction steps. It still cannot verify whether fourteen was the correct recorded removal. A sound workflow audits both the input record and the computation.

Constructed AI response: “There were thirteen supplies; five were used; eight remain. Therefore this verifies the warehouse's live inventory.” The arithmetic is correct, but the production claim is unsupported. The example has no live connection or verified record. Keep mathematical validation separate from real-world observation.

## Formative quiz and alternate reassessment

Award two points each: one for the answer or judgment and one for a correct model, inverse check or explanation.

Q1. 15 − 7.
Q2. Twenty-four items comprise nine selected and the rest unselected. Find the unselected count.
Q3. Eighteen objects compared with eleven: how many more?
Q4. Sam has twelve cards, three fewer than Lee. Find Lee's count.
Q5. Does changing 14 − 6 to 6 − 14 preserve the answer? Explain the whole-number boundary.

Answers: Q1 eight, checked by seven plus eight. Q2 fifteen, assuming a disjoint complete partition. Q3 seven objects, checked by eleven plus seven. Q4 fifteen cards, since fifteen minus three is twelve. Q5 no; fourteen minus six is eight, and the reversed expression has no nonnegative whole-number result.

Alternate form: R1 16 − 7; R2 twenty-seven objects with eleven selected; R3 compare nineteen and thirteen; R4 Ana has sixteen cards, four fewer than Ben; R5 compare 9 − 0 and 0 − 9. Answers: nine; sixteen unselected; six more; twenty cards; nine versus no whole-number result, or negative nine when explicitly using integers.

## Mastery, remediation and retrieval

Require nine of ten independent answers correct, at least 8/10 quiz points, and correct interpretation of a new removal, comparison and missing-part story. The unknown-larger case and zero boundaries must be corrected before progression. A correct bare answer does not replace modeling evidence.

If interpretation fails, label three boxes in writing: whole, known part, unknown part. For comparison, align one-to-one pairs and count the excess. For counting errors, mark movements rather than endpoint labels. For operand reversal, read the original question aloud and explain which quantity is the whole. Oral explanation, tactile modeling and written equations are equally acceptable ways to demonstrate the reasoning.

Retrieval cues: identify the relationship; preserve operand order; count intervals; check with addition; audit assumptions. Continue to subtraction facts after these concepts are reliable.

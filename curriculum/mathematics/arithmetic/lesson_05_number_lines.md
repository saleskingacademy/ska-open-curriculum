## Objectives, notation, and vocabulary

Build a labeled line with equal intervals; identify origin, direction and scale; locate values from marks; count intervals rather than endpoints; distinguish position and distance; interpret directed movement concretely; and explain why an uneven or unlabeled drawing can mislead.

A number line assigns numerical positions to points on a straight line. The origin is the point labeled zero. A tick is a marked point. An interval is the gap between two points. Scale tells how many numerical units each equal physical gap represents. Distance is nonnegative separation. Displacement includes direction. Use < and > for order and |a| for a's distance from zero, introduced with concrete markers. Subtraction expressions for distance are previews until the relevant operation lessons.

## First principles and formal explanation

Lay a strip on a table, choose zero, and place equally spaced marks to its right labeled 1, 2, 3, 4. Equal physical gaps represent equal numerical changes. Extend left with −1, −2, −3 when a signed reference is needed. The orientation used here increases to the right. A vertical representation may increase upward; always label direction.

The scale need not be one. Marks labeled 0, 5, 10, 15 at equal spacing represent five numerical units per gap. A point at the second mark to the right of zero is ten, not two. First count gaps, then interpret their scale. Scale and count of marks are distinct information.

For unit spacing, a path from −2 to 3 crosses five gaps: −2 to −1, then to 0, 1, 2, 3. There are six endpoint/intermediate labels, but only five gaps. This derives the separation without assuming a signed subtraction procedure. Distance does not inherit the negative sign of a starting point.

A formal line model requires consistent interval weights and an order-preserving labeling convention. A sketch may not be physically exact, but the labels must make the intended scale clear. If labels claim equal unit gaps while the numerical steps differ, the model is inconsistent.

## Additional worked examples

Example 1: With unit ticks from 0 through 6, the marker at 4 is four intervals from zero. Its position and distance from zero both equal four in this nonnegative case.

Example 2: With ticks −4 through 2, the separation of endpoints is six intervals. Seven labeled marks are present. A mark count answers how many marked positions, not how far apart the endpoints are.

Example 3: A line's adjacent labels are 0, 10, 20 and 30. Moving two gaps right from zero reaches 20, while moving one gap left from 30 reaches 20. Both routes verify the same position and scale.

## Guided practice

G1. Locate 3 on a line with zero and unit spacing. Answer: three equal gaps to the right of zero.
G2. From −1 move two unit gaps right. Answer: pass zero and end at 1; count movements, not starting labels.
G3. On ticks 0, 2, 4, 6, find the value after three gaps from zero. Answer: 6, since each gap represents two units.

## Independent set

P1. Draw or build a unit line from 0 to 5 inclusive. Count marks and gaps separately.
P2. Locate −3, 0 and 2 on an equally spaced line and state their order.
P3. Find the distance from −2 to 4 by listing gaps.
P4. A marker at −5 has what position and what distance from zero?
P5. Starting at 2, move three unit intervals right. State the endpoint.
P6. Starting at 3, move five unit intervals left. State the endpoint.
P7. A scale has ticks 0, 5, 10, 15. How many gaps separate 0 from 15, and what numerical distance do they represent?
P8. A diagram puts labels 0, 1, 3 at equal physical spacing and claims each gap is one unit. Diagnose the inconsistency.
P9. There are nine unit-spaced marks from 0 through 8. Explain why their endpoint distance is not nine.
P10. Describe two different paths from −1 to 2, one direct and one with a detour. Explain how endpoint separation can remain fixed despite different total travel.

### Explanatory solutions

P1. Six marks and five gaps, including both endpoints as marks.
P2. −3 < 0 < 2, with left-to-right numerical increase.
P3. Six units: −2 to −1 to 0 to 1 to 2 to 3 to 4, six movements.
P4. Position −5; distance 5 units.
P5. 5, through 3, 4, 5.
P6. −2, through 2, 1, 0, −1, −2.
P7. Three gaps representing fifteen units, because each is five units.
P8. The first labeled change is one but the second two. Equal physical gaps cannot both represent one unit with those labels. Insert a tick for 2 or state a different consistent scale.
P9. Eight gaps lie between nine marks; endpoint separation is eight units.
P10. Direct: −1 to 0 to 1 to 2, three units traveled. Detour: −1 to −2 then return through −1, 0, 1, 2, five units traveled. Endpoints are unchanged, so separation remains three. Travel length and endpoint separation are different quantities.

## Applications, diagnosis, and boundaries

Temperature, elevation, calendar offsets and measurement rulers use chosen origins and scales. An origin is a reference, not automatically the absence of the measured phenomenon. A ruler may not place its zero at the physical edge, so read labels rather than assuming the edge is zero.

Diagnosis exercise: “The marker is at −4, so it is a negative distance away.” The sign identifies location relative to the reference. Distance from origin is four, nonnegative. Diagnosis exercise: “Adding four means move to label four.” In a movement model, begin at the actual initial position and move four intervals; starting at three reaches seven, not four. Formal addition is Lesson 11, but the movement can be checked by counting now.

Zero to zero has distance zero. A single marked point has no endpoint gap to itself. Negative and positive positions can have equal distance from origin without being the same point. A line with only whole-number ticks can still represent intermediate positions conceptually; later fractions and decimals will specify them. Do not assume whole ticks mean that no values exist between them.

## Tool use, algorithm trace, and AI audit

Optional algorithm task: given a starting label, a direction, a number of unit movements and a unit scale, list every reached position. Test (start 0, right, three moves, scale 2): 2, 4, 6. If you list 0, 2, 4 as the three reached positions, you counted the starting position as a move. Distinguish the initial state from processed movement steps, a later computational skill.

No calculator is needed. Verify by a second route or a tactile strip with equal gaps. Constructed AI claim: “There are five marks at 0 through 4, so their length is five units.” Correct four unit intervals. The diagram exposes the erroneous object being counted; replacing the answer without naming that error gives weak verification.

## Review, quiz, alternate tasks, and mastery

Keep a review sheet labeling origin, tick, gap, scale, increasing direction, position and distance. Key rules: consistent equal gaps carry equal numerical changes; distance counts scaled intervals; position can be negative while distance cannot. Travel may include detours, so it can exceed endpoint separation. Later geometry and rate problems depend on these distinctions.

Quiz, two points per item for result and representation:
Q1. How many marks and gaps occur at unit labels 0 through 4?
Q2. What is the distance from −3 to 2?
Q3. State position and distance from zero for −7.
Q4. On ticks 0, 4, 8, 12, what distance is represented by two gaps?
Q5. Starting at −2, move three unit intervals right. Where do you stop?

Answers: five marks/four gaps; five units by crossing zero; position −7/distance 7; eight units because each gap is four; 1 via −1, 0, 1. Reassessment: marks/gaps from −2 through 3; distance from −1 to 4; two rightward gaps from 0 on a five-unit scale. Expected six/five; five units; endpoint ten.

Require 8/10 quiz points, nine of ten independent tasks correct, and corrected scale/sign/endpoint errors. Build an unfamiliar scaled line and explain every interval before advancing. Remediation: separate labels from spaces using movable cards and tokens placed in gaps; count only gap tokens for distance; state the scale aloud. Reattempt alternate tasks without copying.

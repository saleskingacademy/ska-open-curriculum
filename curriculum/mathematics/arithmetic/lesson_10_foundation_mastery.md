## Purpose, entry tasks, and measurable objectives

A foundation is mastered when its ideas can be used together without a question announcing the method. This lesson joins counting, notation, place value, order, number lines, signed references, estimation, rounding, and mathematical language. It asks you to choose a representation, explain your choice, and check the result. There is no new high-level arithmetic algorithm to memorize.

Before starting, count twelve physical objects exactly, explain the zero in 305, locate −2 and 3 on a line, and round 248 to tens with a stated convention. If any task fails, repair it in its source lesson before doing mixed tasks. Do not use a good total score to hide a recurring count or place-value error.

After completing the lesson, you should independently identify what a problem asks; choose an exact count, comparison, estimate, bound, or rounded report; preserve signs and units; justify conclusions; diagnose plausible errors; and create a small auditable record connecting physical quantities to mathematical notation. These are foundational competencies. They do not claim that the entire Arithmetic course or a formal academic credential has been completed.

## Vocabulary and a first-principles workflow

**Mastery** is reproducible competent performance, including reasoning and transfer. **Transfer** means using an idea in an unfamiliar setting. **Representation** means a way to show a value or relationship. **Verification** means checking by evidence, preferably with an approach that can expose the original method's error. **Remediation** is targeted work on a demonstrated gap. A **rubric** describes how evidence is scored.

Use a five-part workflow: identify, represent, reason, verify, report. Identify the quantity and the question. Represent with counters, a place-value chart, a line, words, or notation. Reason using the relevant definition or procedure. Verify by recounting, another representation, or a justified bound. Report the result with units and its exact or approximate status.

This workflow follows directly from the early lessons. Counting uses one-to-one correspondence; place value gives positional meaning to numerals; a line establishes order and intervals; estimation preserves useful scale while discarding detail; rounding selects an allowed report under a convention; mathematical language states what the conclusion actually claims. Each tool solves a different part of the communication problem.

## Formal principles to retain

A finite collection's exact count does not change when its objects are rearranged without adding or removing them. The numeral's layout and the collection's layout are separate matters. Equality asserts the same value, and order places signed values consistently on a chosen number line. Distance is nonnegative and counts intervals, not marked endpoints.

A decimal numeral's digit weights depend on position. A zero placeholder may contribute no quantity while remaining necessary to locate other digits. Nearest rounding changes a number to an allowed nearby value and has an error bound of half the place unit for an exact input. An estimate or bound supports only the conclusions it justifies. Inclusive boundary symbols include their endpoints; strict ones do not.

These principles derive from models already developed, so use them as reasons rather than unexplained slogans. For example, a number-line diagram explains why seven labels can have only six unit gaps; a place-value chart explains why 507 is not 57; a boundary example explains why “at most ten” permits ten.

## Worked integrated examples

**Example 1 — stock record.** A shelf has six complete groups of ten packets and four loose packets. Count by tens to sixty, then four more to sixty-four. Record exactly 64 packets. The numeral 64 represents six tens and four ones. A nearest-ten report is about 60 packets under ties upward, with a gap of four. If a container holds only 60, the rounded report does not justify using it: the exact stock exceeds its capacity. Verification: lay out the groups and count all sixty-four once. Reporting an exact count and an approximate count serves two different purposes.

**Example 2 — basement locations.** Storage rooms are labeled −3, 0, and 2 relative to ground floor. Order them as −3 < 0 < 2. The separation from −3 to 2 is five one-floor intervals: three to ground and two above. The location −3 is negative, but its distance from ground is three floors. A record reading “negative three boxes” confuses floor label with object count. Ask for an actual box count rather than importing the sign from location.

**Example 3 — event planning.** Confirmed estimates bound attendance from 74 through 79 inclusive. Eighty seats are sufficient under those bounds. Seventy-five seats might be sufficient for some values, but the information does not guarantee it. A report may say “attendance expected in the range 74–79, inclusive,” rather than “exactly 77.” If the bounds depend on unreliable inputs, report that limitation too. A calculation cannot validate the assumptions supplying its numbers.

**Example 4 — decimal labels.** A measured label reads 4.096 m. Compare it with 4.09 m by writing the latter as 4.090 m. The former is larger. Rounded to hundredths, 4.096 m reports 4.10 m under ties upward; the thousandths digit 6 increases the hundredths and carries across 9. This report is not exactly equal to the original. Verify by identifying neighbors 4.09 and 4.10 and midpoint 4.095. The original lies above the midpoint.

**Example 5 — checking communication.** A learner writes “At least 30 means fewer than 30, so 30 is excluded.” Translate “at least” as ≥. Test 29, 30, and 31: the first fails, the second and third pass for whole counts. The mistake is linguistic and logical, not a failure of subtraction. Repair the interpretation before assigning computation drills.

## Guided practice

G1. Write 708 in expanded quantity form and explain its zero. **Response:** Seven hundreds, zero tens, eight ones; equivalently 700 + 8. The zero reserves the tens place. The expanded sum omits a zero contribution without changing the numeral's place structure.

G2. A reading is −6 and a second is −1 on the same scale. Which reading is larger, and what is their separation? **Response:** −1 is larger, and the gap is five scale units. Signed order and unsigned gap answer different questions.

G3. A count is 95. Give nearest-ten rounding under ties upward and the smallest ten-group capacity that covers it. **Response:** Both give 100 here. This agreement does not make the methods identical: for 91, nearest is 90 while covering capacity is 100.

G4. An exact count is unavailable, but a label says approximately fifty. Can you assert 49 ≤ N ≤ 51? **Response:** Not without a specified tolerance or other supporting information. “Approximately” alone does not supply those exact bounds.

## Independent mixed problem set

P1. A row of equal-spaced marks is labeled 0 through 8 inclusive. How many marks and how many one-unit gaps are there?
P2. Write 60,204 in words and explain both zero positions.
P3. Compare 0.507 and 0.57, showing a representation-based reason.
P4. Order 5, −3, 0, −8, and 2.
P5. State the distance from −4 to 3 and explain with unit intervals.
P6. Round 1,995 to tens and to hundreds under nonnegative nearest, ties upward.
P7. A confirmed count is 151 and storage is offered in groups of fifty. Give the smallest sufficient capacity among 100, 150, and 200.
P8. A whole count satisfies 4 < N ≤ 8. List possible values and explain both endpoints.
P9. A rough report says “about 100 items.” Someone concludes the maximum is 100. Diagnose the unsupported step.
P10. A point is at −2 and the record says “distance from origin: −2.” Repair the record while preserving the original position information.

### Explanatory solutions

P1. Nine marks and eight gaps. The endpoints are both counted as marks, but intervals lie between consecutive marks.
P2. Sixty thousand two hundred four. The thousands zero contributes no thousands beyond the six ten-thousands; the tens zero places four in ones, not tens. Expanded quantity: 60,000 + 200 + 4.
P3. 0.507 < 0.570; equal tenths, but zero hundredths is less than seven hundredths.
P4. −8, −3, 0, 2, 5, following left-to-right order.
P5. Seven units, four from −4 to zero and three from zero to 3. Position signs do not make distance negative.
P6. 2,000 in both cases: an exact nearest-ten tie goes upward, and the number is above the nearest-hundred midpoint 1,950. State the two different reasons.
P7. 200. Capacity 150 cannot cover 151, even though the gap is only one. Choose the next allowed sufficient option.
P8. 5, 6, 7, 8; four is excluded by <, eight included by ≤.
P9. An approximation has been turned into an unsupported upper bound. Obtain a bound or exact count before asserting a maximum.
P10. Position −2; distance from origin 2 units. Keep both labels so information is not lost.

## A small foundation investigation

Choose 20–60 similar safe household objects, or use a prepared set of accessible tokens. Before counting, estimate the quantity with a counted benchmark of ten and state a tolerance of five objects. Count exactly with a method that prevents double-counting. Rearrange without adding or removing objects and recount independently. Write the exact numeral in place-value words. Give a nearest-ten report using the main convention. Describe a ten-group capacity that covers all items. Compare estimate, exact count, and rounded report, explaining the role of each.

Submit a one-page record or equivalent spoken/tactile demonstration showing your benchmark, estimate, first count, independent recount, error size, rounded report, covering capacity, and conclusions. This is a foundational project, not the course capstone. No machine or AI is needed.

Rubric: five dimensions, each worth 0–2 points. (1) quantity/count integrity; (2) place-value representation; (3) stated approximation/tie conventions; (4) justified verification; (5) clear distinction between exact, approximate, and guaranteed capacity. Award 2 for correct independent evidence, 1 for partially correct evidence needing a specific repair, and 0 for missing or fundamentally incorrect evidence. Require at least 8/10 and 2 in count integrity and verification. Correct every factual error even if the score passes. A copied tool response does not supply the independent evidence.

## Computational and AI-augmented treatment

Algorithm trace exercise: write instructions for a partner to count objects once, using separate “uncounted” and “counted” areas. The partner must be able to follow them without guessing. Test the empty collection, a single object, and a rearranged set of twelve. Expected counts are 0, 1, and 12. If an object moves back to the uncounted area unnoticed, the process can overcount; include a check for accidental reuse. This anticipates loops, process invariants, and debugging without requiring a programming language.

Constructed AI-audit exercise: “There are seven markers from −3 to 3, so the end-to-end distance is seven units; since it is below ground, the distance is negative.” Diagnose both mistakes. Seven integer markers have six gaps, and distance is nonnegative. The actual distance is 6 units. Verify with a diagram or tactile spacing. A convincing explanation must identify the flawed step, not merely replace the final numeral.

Calculators may be used to check selected values after human work, but they do not replace counts, domain checks, or interpretation. Record the entered problem and explain why it matches the original situation. If a device's rounding convention is unknown, check the mathematical midpoint directly rather than inferring the policy from a single rounded display.

## Common failures and review routes

A learner may correctly round while miscounting the original collection, compare unsigned magnitudes while ignoring signs, or obtain the right number with the wrong unit. These are different failure modes. Correct the cause: count integrity in Lesson 1; digit meaning in Lesson 3; signed order in Lessons 4–6; approximation guarantees in Lesson 7; rounding place in Lesson 8; conditions and units in Lesson 9.

Edge cases to include in review are an empty collection, a one-object collection, a numeral with internal zeros, a negative position paired with positive distance, an exact midpoint, a carry to a longer numeral, and a capacity only one unit too small. These cases expose rules that routine examples can hide.

Review by explaining one task from each source lesson with no notes. Then alternate representations: show a count as objects and a numeral, a comparison as words and a line, and a limit as words and symbols. Repeatedly reading a completed solution is not independent practice; cover it, reconstruct it, then compare.

## Quiz, answers, and alternate tasks

Two points per question: one for result, one for reasoning.

Q1. How many integer-labeled marks and gaps are present from −2 to 2 inclusive?
Q2. What does the digit 6 contribute in 6,040?
Q3. Which is larger, −7 or −2? Explain.
Q4. Round 8.995 to hundredths under ties upward.
Q5. A known count is from 31 through 38 inclusive. Are 35 spaces guaranteed enough?

Answers: Q1 five marks and four gaps, because intervals lie between marks; Q2 6,000 from six thousands; Q3 −2, farther right; Q4 9.00 through a tie and carry; Q5 no, because 36–38 remain possible. This does not say 35 is definitely insufficient for every possible count.

Reassessment: R1 marks and gaps from −4 to 1; R2 value of 5 in 50,006; R3 7.995 to hundredths; R4 whole counts with 2 ≤ N < 5. Answers: six marks/five gaps; 50,000; 8.00; 2, 3, 4. Explain each independently after targeted remediation.

## Mastery evidence and transition

Require at least 8/10 on this lesson quiz, nine of ten independent problems correct, the project rubric threshold, and the [Level 1 examination](assessments/level_01_foundation_examination.md) thresholds. The examination samples the whole module, while this lesson teaches how to integrate and verify. It is not a comprehensive Arithmetic final.

If a critical skill fails, identify the outcome in the examination blueprint, complete its remediation route, and attempt the alternate form after practice. Do not average the first and second scores to conceal whether the skill is now mastered. Retain both records and the corrected explanations. After demonstration, Lesson 11 develops addition explicitly from joined quantities. Algebra later generalizes equality and operation laws; statistics examines uncertainty; formal mathematics makes conditions and justifications precise. Foundation mastery is the entry to those studies, not evidence that they have already been learned.

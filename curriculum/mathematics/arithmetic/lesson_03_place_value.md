## Outcomes, vocabulary, and symbols

Explain digit versus digit value; build whole numerals from ones, tens and hundreds; read internal zeros; translate standard and expanded form; explain adjacent-place weights; and identify tenths and hundredths as equal subdivisions of a whole unit.

A place is a digit's position in a positional numeral. Place value is its unit weight. Standard form is the ordinary written numeral. Expanded form shows its place contributions. A bundle of ten ones is one ten; ten tens form one hundred. A decimal point separates ones from tenths. In decimal notation each place has ten times the weight of its immediate right neighbor. Use × to mean repeated equal contributions here; its general calculation lesson comes later.

## First principles and derivation

Build thirteen objects into one group of ten and three loose ones. Writing 13 records one ten and three ones, not one object and three objects. Next build forty-two as four tens and two ones. Exchanging ten ones for one ten preserves quantity, which is why later arithmetic can regroup without changing a sum.

With digits restricted to 0–9, a place does not need to hold a count of ten: exchange it into the next place. One hundred equals ten tens, which equals one hundred ones. Continuing these groupings explains thousands and higher places. Formally, a numeral such as 352 denotes three hundred-unit contributions, five ten-unit contributions and two one-unit contributions. Its expanded form 300 + 50 + 2 describes the same collection structure.

To the right of the point, divide a whole unit into ten equal parts. One part is a tenth; divide a tenth into ten equal parts and obtain hundredths. Ten hundredths equal one tenth, matching the same adjacent-place relationship. Thus 0.4 means four tenths, while 0.04 means four hundredths. Neither is interpreted by counting printed digits alone.

## Additional worked examples

Example 1: Build 406 from four hundreds, zero tens and six ones. Expanded form is 400 + 6. The omitted zero term contributes nothing in the expansion, but the numeral needs its placeholder so six remains in ones.

Example 2: Read 50,028 as fifty thousand twenty-eight. Its five contributes 50,000, its two 20, and its eight 8. The internal zero positions record no thousands or hundreds contributions.

Example 3: Compare representations 2.5 and 2.50 by subdividing the same half unit as five tenths or fifty hundredths. They have equal value. In 2.05, the five describes five hundredths, so it has a different contribution.

## Applications, errors, and edge cases

Invoices, meter readings and inventory reports depend on correct place interpretation. A stock entry 1,050 differs from 150, so omitting a placeholder can materially change decisions. A measurement 0.05 differs from 0.5 by its subdivision; a nearby-looking printed form may describe a very different size.

Diagnosis exercise: “In 6,006 both sixes contribute six.” Correct the first to 6,000 and the last to 6. Their digit identity matches, but their units differ. Diagnosis exercise: “Appending any zero leaves value unchanged.” Appending a decimal trailing zero preserves value, as in 3.4 and 3.40; appending a whole-number rightmost zero changes 34 into 340. The zero's position and context matter.

Zero by itself represents zero. Leading zeros do not change ordinary numerical whole value, but identifiers may require them. Decimal trailing zeros can convey a reporting place without changing value. Commas in this material group thousands; the decimal point separates fractional places. Other notation conventions exist, so establish the convention before reading imported numerical text.

## Computational and AI treatment

Optional algorithm task: describe reading a whole numeral by naming each digit's place contribution and combining the contributions. Trace 508: five hundreds, zero tens, eight ones. Check by bundles. Later number bases change the group size, while the positional idea remains.

A calculator cannot infer whether a copied numeral lost a zero. Compare the original entry and its place chart before accepting a display. Constructed AI claim: “The 4 in 4,040 always contributes four.” Correct contributions are 4,000 and 40, distinguished by position. Verify with expanded notation rather than another generated assertion.

## Review, formulas, quiz, and mastery

Review the place names from ones through ten-thousands and tenths through thousandths. Key relationships: ten ones = one ten; ten tens = one hundred; ten tenths = one whole; ten hundredths = one tenth. A digit's contribution is its count of the place unit. Practice both decomposition and rebuilding so recognition does not substitute for understanding.

Quiz, two points each for value and reason:
Q1. Expand 3,208.
Q2. Write six hundreds, zero tens and five ones.
Q3. State the value of 9 in 0.093.
Q4. Explain why 7.20 equals 7.2.
Q5. Explain why 207 is not 27.

Answers: 3,000 + 200 + 8; 605; nine hundredths or 0.09; zero hundredths adds no value; 207 has two hundreds rather than two tens and needs the tens placeholder. Reassessment: expand 4,009; build 80,023; identify 6's contribution in 0.064. Expected 4,000 + 9; 80,023 from eight ten-thousands, two tens, three ones; 0.06 or six hundredths.

Require 8/10 quiz points, nine of ten independent tasks correct, and correction of every zero/place error. Rebuild one whole numeral with bundles and one decimal with equal subdivisions. Remediation: label place columns before inserting digits; physically exchange ten small units for one larger unit; contrast 305/35 and 0.3/0.03. Reattempt alternate items with new numerals. Addition, rounding, decimals and computer number bases all rely on matching positional units rather than the appearance of the digit alone.

# Customer Problems and Research

**Curriculum expansion:** Additive instructional draft; not a replacement for canonical lesson identifiers. **Level:** 1. **Evidence status:** worked cases are hypothetical and no live operational outcomes are claimed.

## Learning outcomes
Separate assumptions from observations; choose appropriate research methods; calculate simple proportions; distinguish a convenience sample from a representative sample; write a problem statement without overstating demand.

## Why research precedes product design
A product solves a problem only when its intended users actually experience that problem and the solution improves an outcome they value. Founders frequently mistake personal preference, compliments and survey enthusiasm for purchasing behavior. Research reduces uncertainty but cannot remove it. A good research plan begins with a decision: which assumption must be tested before investing more resources?

**Primary research** collects new evidence through interviews, observation, surveys or experiments. **Secondary research** analyzes existing reports, studies and records. **Qualitative evidence** explains motives and context; **quantitative evidence** estimates frequencies, amounts and differences. Each method has different strengths and failure modes.

An interview can reveal that a customer struggles with reconciliation, but ten interviews cannot establish the prevalence of that problem across an entire industry. A large survey may estimate prevalence if its sampling and measurement are defensible, but poorly phrased questions can still bias results.

## Research ethics and question design
Seek informed permission for interviews, explain how notes will be used, and avoid collecting confidential customer data unnecessarily. Ask about observed past behavior: “Tell me about the last time this happened,” not “Would you definitely buy an AI solution?” Distinguish what the respondent did from what they predict they might do. Do not promise anonymity unless the collection and storage design can actually deliver it.

A neutral question: “How many hours did your team spend reconciling invoices last month?” A leading question: “How much time does your outdated system waste?” The second presumes the conclusion. A question that combines two issues—“Are your sales and finance systems unreliable?”—cannot isolate the answer.

## Worked example — interpreting a survey
A founder surveys 80 members of an online group and finds 48 report difficulty tracking follow-ups. The observed sample proportion is 48/80 = **60%**. This does **not** establish that 60% of all businesses have that problem: the group may overrepresent people already interested in automation, and respondents may self-select based on frustration.

If 20 of those 48 respondents agree to a follow-up interview, that is 20/48 ≈ **41.7%** of the problem-reporting respondents and 20/80 = **25%** of all survey respondents. Agreement to an interview is not evidence of willingness to pay. A next step might be to compare interviews across different industries, company sizes and current systems, then test a narrowly specified offer.

## Worked example — economic severity
A team reports 15 duplicate entries per week, each taking an average of 6 minutes to resolve. Weekly rework = 15 × 6 = 90 minutes = 1.5 hours. At an illustrative fully loaded labor cost of $30/hour, direct time value is $45 weekly, before uncertainty. If a proposed tool costs $200 monthly, the stated rework savings alone do not prove positive financial value. Consider other documented impacts, implementation cost, error severity and adoption rates. Do not fabricate benefits to close the gap.

## Research log
Record the date, hypothesis, population of interest, recruitment method, number invited, number participating, exact questions, evidence, uncertainty and decision. Keep raw personal records private. A public curriculum case should use fictionalized or properly de-identified examples.

### Practical assignment
Write three competing explanations for low conversion: (a) weak product fit, (b) unclear messaging, (c) checkout friction. For each, propose one observation that would help distinguish it from the others. Example: compare drop-off by checkout step without assuming the cause from a single metric.

## Exercises
1. Why is “Would you buy this?” weaker than asking about a recent purchase?
2. In a sample of 50 respondents, 15 report a problem. Calculate the sample proportion and explain one reason not to generalize.
3. Design two neutral questions about invoice reconciliation.
4. Calculate the weekly rework time for 24 errors at five minutes each.
5. Explain why a signed pilot agreement is stronger behavioral evidence than a positive survey comment, while still not guaranteeing long-term demand.

### Answer key
1. Hypothetical intentions often diverge from behavior; past action offers more concrete evidence, though context matters.
2. 15/50 = **30%**; selection bias, nonresponse or small sample size may limit generalization.
3. Examples: “How many invoices required correction last month?” and “What steps do you follow when a discrepancy appears?”
4. 120 minutes = **2 hours**.
5. It involves a real commitment, but retention, usage and willingness to renew remain unproven.

## Mastery and transfer
Require correct proportions and a research plan containing a testable hypothesis, a neutral question, an explicit sampling limitation and an evidence-based next decision. Repeat with a different product category after seven days. Do not certify a learner based only on recalling definitions.

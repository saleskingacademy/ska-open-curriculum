# Sales — Ethical Discovery and Qualification

## Outcomes and prerequisites
Prerequisites: buyer journey, basic CRM documentation, elementary arithmetic, professional communication. Learners will separate evidence from inference, identify business problems without manipulation, qualify authority and feasibility, document a mutually agreed next step, and honor consent and opt-outs.

## Core lesson
**Discovery** is the disciplined process of learning the buyer's goals, constraints, decision process and success criteria. **Qualification** determines whether a proposed solution is plausibly valuable and deliverable, not whether a prospect can be pressured into a purchase.

A useful discovery sequence begins with permission and context: “Would it be useful to compare your current process with a measurable improvement target?” Next, explore the current workflow and ask for observable evidence: “How many requests arrive each week? What happens when one is missed? How is that recorded?” Separate the respondent's estimate from audited measurements. Identify the economic decision maker, users, procurement, and technical stakeholders without assuming one enthusiastic participant can authorize spending.

Translate needs into falsifiable requirements. “We need automation” is a broad preference. “Reduce median response time from 18 hours to under 4 hours while keeping error rate below 2% over a 30-day pilot” is a measurable candidate requirement. Before proposing a solution, verify the baseline, measurement method, implementation burden and tradeoffs. Do not fabricate urgency, testimonials, customer results, or ROI.

A qualified opportunity has at least a plausible problem-solution fit, a measurable outcome, a feasible delivery path, a legitimate decision process, and a mutually acceptable next step. A buyer may decline or lack resources; a documented disqualification is preferable to a misleading forecast.

### Worked case
A prospective customer reports 120 inquiries per month, 20% initially unanswered, and an average gross contribution of $80 per successfully converted incremental inquiry. The seller must not calculate 24×$80 as proven incremental value: 24 inquiries were reportedly unanswered, but the number that could be recovered and converted is unknown. Suppose a pilot demonstrably recovers 15 additional inquiries and 4 convert, with $80 contribution each. Observed incremental contribution attributable to those 4 conversions is $320 before pilot and service costs, *if* the attribution is valid. Compare against a baseline or control and account for implementation expense. A conversion count alone does not prove causal incrementality.

### CRM evidence protocol
Record timestamp, source, buyer-reported metric, confidence level, unresolved questions, next permissioned action, and opt-out state. Keep hypotheses in a distinct field. Example: “Buyer reports 120 inquiries/month and 20% unanswered; baseline not independently verified. Hypothesis: workflow triage could reduce missed inquiries. Procurement owner unknown. Next action: request permission for a baseline review; no outreach after opt-out.”

## Exercises
1. Rewrite “They loved the demo; deal is guaranteed” into an evidence-based CRM note.
2. Design five discovery questions that elicit baseline, costs, success criteria, constraints and decision process.
3. Explain why 24 missed inquiries cannot be equated with 24 incremental sales.
4. Draft an ethical disqualification message when projected benefits do not cover costs.

## Eight-question quiz with answers and rationales
1. What distinguishes an observation from a hypothesis? **Answer:** An observation records a reported or measured event; a hypothesis interprets it and needs testing. **Rationale:** Buyer enthusiasm is not proof of approval.
2. What is a measurable success criterion? **Answer:** A defined metric, baseline, target and time window. **Rationale:** “Better automation” is not independently testable.
3. Does an enthusiastic end user necessarily control budget? **Answer:** No. **Rationale:** Approval roles differ across organizations.
4. Why verify a baseline? **Answer:** To distinguish actual improvement from unsupported claims and normal variation.
5. What is 20% of 120 inquiries? **Answer:** 24 inquiries. **Rationale:** 0.20×120=24; this is not a count of incremental conversions.
6. What is contribution from four validated incremental conversions at $80 each, before other costs? **Answer:** $320. **Rationale:** 4×80=320.
7. What should happen after a prospect opts out? **Answer:** Suppress further promotional contact across workflows. **Rationale:** Respect expressed preference and applicable law.
8. What makes a next step mutual? **Answer:** The buyer explicitly agrees to the action and its purpose. **Rationale:** A unilateral calendar invite does not establish consent.

## Applied test and solutions
**Scenario:** A prospect reports 250 monthly requests, 12% missed, and $55 contribution per incremental sale. A seller asserts all missed requests will convert if recovered.
A. Calculate reported missed requests. **Solution:** 250×.12=30.
B. Identify the reasoning error. **Solution:** Recovery and conversion are distinct; neither 100% recovery nor 100% conversion is established.
C. If a controlled pilot supports six additional conversions, calculate incremental contribution before costs. **Solution:** 6×$55=$330.
D. List three required qualification facts. **Solution:** Verified baseline and outcome target, buying/approval process, and delivery feasibility; other defensible facts accepted.
E. Write a permission-based next step and a stop condition. **Sample solution:** Request agreement to a scoped baseline review; stop outreach upon opt-out or when value cannot be substantiated.

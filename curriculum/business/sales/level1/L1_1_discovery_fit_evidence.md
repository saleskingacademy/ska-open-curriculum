# Discovery, Fit and Evidence

**Curriculum expansion:** Additive instructional draft; not a replacement for canonical lesson identifiers. **Level:** 1. **Evidence status:** worked cases are hypothetical and no live operational outcomes are claimed.

## Learning objectives
Distinguish a buyer's stated request from a verified problem; conduct permission-based discovery; evaluate fit without inventing evidence; identify an appropriate next step or disqualification.

## The role of discovery
Selling is not merely persuading someone to purchase. Effective discovery establishes whether an offer addresses a real need, whether the prospect has authority and capacity to act, and whether the seller can deliver what is promised. A buyer's initial statement is a hypothesis, not a complete diagnosis. “We need AI” may mean slow data entry, inconsistent service, weak reporting or something unrelated to AI.

**Problem statement:** a concrete difference between current and desired outcomes. **Constraint:** a limit such as budget, timing, security, compliance or staffing. **Decision process:** who evaluates, approves and implements a purchase. **Evidence:** records or observations supporting a claim. **Fit:** whether the offer's verified capabilities address the problem within constraints.

A discovery conversation should seek permission, use open questions, confirm understanding and avoid pressuring the prospect to disclose confidential information. Ask “What happens when this task is delayed?” rather than assuming a financial loss. A claimed savings figure requires a documented baseline, realistic implementation cost and validated assumptions.

## A structured conversation
1. State identity and purpose accurately; verify that contact is permitted.
2. Ask about current workflow: “How is this handled today?”
3. Explore frequency, stakeholders and consequences: “How often does the delay occur?”
4. Ask for evidence, where appropriate: “Do you have a recent example or non-sensitive metric?”
5. Clarify constraints: “What security or approval requirements would a solution have to meet?”
6. Summarize neutrally: “I heard that requests are reviewed manually and the backlog peaks on Mondays. Is that accurate?”
7. Propose a reversible next step only if fit is plausible: an agreed demonstration, scoped evaluation or referral.
8. Record uncertainty and consent; stop outreach when requested.

## Worked case — evaluate automation value
A small service team reports 120 inbound requests per week. Each requires approximately 4 minutes of triage. Baseline triage time = 120 × 4 = 480 minutes = 8 hours per week. A proposed classifier might reduce manual review to 2 minutes per request for 80 requests, while the remaining 40 still require 4 minutes. New review time = 80 × 2 + 40 × 4 = 320 minutes, or 5 hours 20 minutes. Estimated time saving = 160 minutes (2 hours 40 minutes) weekly.

This is a **scenario**, not a measured result. It excludes model review, exceptions, integration, training, compliance and error recovery. At an illustrative labor cost of $25/hour, the gross time value is about $66.67 weekly. If the service costs $250 monthly, the gross time value alone may not justify purchase; other verified benefits and costs must be assessed. The seller should not claim an ROI based on the gross estimate alone.

## Failure modes
**Leading questions:** “You lose thousands every month, right?” pushes a conclusion instead of gathering evidence. **Premature solutioning:** recommending a complex agent before understanding the workflow. **Unverified authority:** assuming the person speaking can approve a contract. **Unsupported scarcity:** inventing deadlines or limited inventory. **Overcollection:** asking for private customer data when aggregate statistics suffice.

**Objections** can signal unresolved information rather than a request for pressure. “It's too expensive” may mean insufficient value, timing, cash constraints or lack of decision authority. Ask for clarification without arguing.

## Roleplay
Seller: “What is the most time-consuming part of handling new inquiries?”
Buyer: “Sorting them.”
Seller: “Roughly how many come in during a typical week, and how do you know?”
Buyer: “About 120, from our ticket report.”
Seller: “What happens when a ticket is misclassified?”
Buyer: “It can delay urgent requests.”
Seller: “Then any proposal would need a reliable escalation rule, not just faster sorting. May I summarize what we know and what still needs testing?”

This dialogue demonstrates validation rather than an immediate close.

## Exercises
1. Rewrite “Our AI will double your sales” as a testable, non-promissory hypothesis.
2. List three questions that uncover a prospect's decision process without assuming authority.
3. Recalculate weekly time saved if only 60 requests improve from four minutes to two minutes.
4. Identify two risks in asking a prospect to upload customer records into a demonstration environment.
5. Write a disqualification statement when the buyer needs a capability the product cannot provide.

### Solutions and rubric
1. Example: “We can test whether this workflow reduces median triage time, measuring baseline and post-deployment performance.” Avoid guaranteed sales claims.
2. “Who else reviews this decision?”; “What criteria must be met?”; “What is the approval process?”
3. 60 × 2 = 120 minutes, or two hours per week, before overhead.
4. Privacy/confidentiality exposure and lack of authorization or appropriate retention controls.
5. “Our current product does not meet that requirement, so I would not recommend purchasing it for this use case.” Any equivalent transparent response passes.

## Mastery gate
Pass a roleplay rubric: accurate listening, two open questions, one evidence check, one constraint check, clear summary and an ethical next step. A fabricated ROI or ignored opt-out is an automatic remediation trigger.

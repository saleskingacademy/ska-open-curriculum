# Sales Mastery — Advanced Evidence-Based Deal Diagnosis
Level: 5 (advanced undergraduate / professional application). Prerequisites: ethical discovery, qualification, unit economics, probability, CRM data hygiene.

## Learning outcomes
After this lesson, the learner can (1) distinguish observed buyer evidence from seller inference; (2) diagnose stalled opportunities using competing hypotheses; (3) quantify an expected-value decision without treating a probability estimate as fact; (4) design an ethical, testable next action; and (5) explain the limits of a deal forecast.

## 1. Observation is not interpretation
A buyer saying “send me information” is an observation. “The buyer is interested” is a hypothesis. “The buyer has authority and budget” is an unsupported leap unless corroborated. In a CRM, store these separately:
- Evidence: dated buyer statement, approved meeting notes, documented stakeholder action, or a verifiable procurement milestone.
- Interpretation: a clearly labeled seller hypothesis.
- Unknown: a material question not yet resolved.
- Next test: a respectful action likely to discriminate between hypotheses.

Never infer sensitive personal attributes or manufacture buyer intent from public profiles. Obtain contact details and conduct outreach lawfully and according to applicable consent and suppression requirements.

## 2. A hypothesis-based diagnosis
For a stalled B2B software opportunity, consider four rival explanations: insufficient economic value, unclear decision authority, unresolved implementation risk, or simple scheduling delay. Each predicts different evidence. A buyer who can quantify value but repeatedly requests security documentation supports an implementation-risk hypothesis more strongly than a price objection hypothesis. A buyer who cannot name an approval process supports an authority/process uncertainty hypothesis. A good diagnosis seeks disconfirming evidence, not merely supportive anecdotes.

**Worked example.** A team offers a workflow product for a $1,499 implementation charge and $2,499 monthly subscription. A prospect reports that employees currently spend 35 hours each month on manual processing. At a fully loaded labor cost of $45/hour, the reported time cost is $1,575/month. Even if all 35 hours were eliminated, labor-time savings alone would not cover the $2,499 monthly subscription. The seller must not claim positive ROI on that evidence. Other possible benefits—error reduction, faster collections, additional sales, risk reduction—require separate evidence and defensible measurement. Ask the buyer which outcome is economically material and how it is measured. If the product cannot demonstrate sufficient value, disqualifying the deal is an ethical and efficient outcome.

## 3. Decision model with explicit assumptions
Suppose the seller estimates a 30% probability of a purchase, a $1,200 expected contribution margin conditional on a purchase over the evaluation horizon, and $100 of incremental cost for a proof-of-value exercise. The simple expected incremental contribution is 0.30 × $1,200 − $100 = $260. This is a planning calculation, not a prediction or proof of actual profit. If the close probability is only 5%, the same calculation yields −$40. Record how probability and margin were estimated, their time horizon, and sensitivity to uncertainty. Do not substitute gross contract value for contribution margin.

**Sensitivity exercise.** With a fixed $100 evaluation cost and $1,200 conditional margin, calculate the break-even close probability: $100 / $1,200 = 8.33%. Explain why a forecast based on ten historical opportunities is unstable, and identify a minimum of three confounders (selection bias, differing deal sizes, stage definitions, market shifts, or missing lost-deal records).

## 4. Ethical next-action experiment
Draft a short message asking the prospect to verify one assumption: “You mentioned 35 hours of manual work each month. Before we propose a pilot, could we confirm whether the principal objective is labor savings, error reduction, or faster revenue collection?” The purpose is discovery, not pressure. Record the response, update the hypothesis, and offer a decision path: measurable pilot, defer, or close-lost. Avoid fake deadlines, fabricated scarcity, and unverified performance claims.

## 5. Failure modes and remediation
- **Confirmation bias:** Only documenting favorable buyer signals. Remedy: maintain a disconfirming-evidence column.
- **False precision:** Reporting a close probability as a measured fact. Remedy: use ranges and provenance.
- **Misleading ROI:** Counting hypothetical gross revenue as net benefit. Remedy: separate revenue, cost, contribution, and uncertainty.
- **Process confusion:** Treating an enthusiastic user as a budget owner. Remedy: ask about procurement without pressuring for personal information.
- **Automation drift:** Automatically sending follow-ups after a stop request. Remedy: enforce opt-out and suppression before every send.

## Practice
1. Create a four-column evidence table (observation, hypothesis, missing evidence, ethical next test) for a stalled deal.
2. Recalculate the example using 20 hours/month at $45/hour. State what can and cannot be concluded.
3. Write two rival hypotheses for a buyer requesting security documentation, and specify evidence that would distinguish them.
4. Explain why a positive expected value does not guarantee an individual sale.

## Assessment — five questions with solutions
1. **Question:** A prospect requests a brochure. Is this proof of purchase intent? **Answer:** No. It is an observed request; purchase intent remains a hypothesis requiring corroboration.
2. **Question:** What is the maximum monthly labor saving from 35 hours at $45/hour? **Answer:** $1,575, assuming all hours are truly recoverable; actual realized savings may be lower.
3. **Question:** At $1,200 conditional margin and $100 evaluation cost, what probability breaks even? **Answer:** 8.33% (approximately), because p × 1,200 = 100.
4. **Question:** Which evidence most directly tests an implementation-risk hypothesis? **Answer:** A buyer's specific security, integration, or deployment acceptance criteria and whether the proposed solution satisfies them.
5. **Question:** Why should an automated follow-up stop after an opt-out? **Answer:** Continued contact violates the expressed preference and may violate applicable marketing requirements; suppression is a mandatory control.

## Advanced transfer

At doctoral level, propose a preregistered study testing whether hypothesis-led discovery improves forecast calibration relative to conventional stage labels; specify unit of randomization, leakage controls, missingness strategy, confidence intervals, and reproducibility. At postdoctoral/research-frontier level, develop and critique a transportability framework across industries and deal sizes, with privacy-preserving evaluation and external replication. These are progression prompts, **not** claims that this single lesson constitutes graduate or postdoctoral completion.
